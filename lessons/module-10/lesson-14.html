Interview Prep — Measurement and Debug

Every hardware interview has one of these, and it is usually the question the decision turns on. "Here is a board that does not work. What do you do?" There is no formula. What they are grading is whether you have a method, and whether you distrust your instruments the right amount.

The method, said out loud, before touching anything. Interviewers are listening for structure, and this structure works on any fault.
  1. What changed? New board, new firmware, new component lot, new temperature. If nothing changed, something changed and you have not found it.
  2. Is it the board or the measurement? Probe somewhere you know the answer first. Confirm the instrument before trusting it.
  3. Power first, always. Every rail, at the load, under load, with the scope AC-coupled as well as DC. Most "logic bugs" are power bugs.
  4. Bisect. Halve the signal chain, decide which half is wrong, repeat. Do not sweep left to right.
  5. Form a hypothesis that predicts something. Then test the prediction rather than the fault. "If it is X, then Y should also be true" is what makes debugging converge.

Questions

1 Method Standard

An amplifier board has no output. Walk me through it.

How to answer

Do not start at the input. Start at power, then bisect.

  1. Rails, at the chip. Not at the regulator — at the pin, under load. A rail that is 4.6 V instead of 5 V, or that has 500 mV of ripple, explains almost anything downstream.
  2. DC bias points, midpoint first. One measurement in the middle of the chain tells you which half to throw away. An op-amp output sitting hard at a rail is a saturated stage and tells you the DC is wrong before you inject any signal at all.
  3. Inject and follow. Signal into the input, then check the midpoint you just measured. Present but wrong means the fault is upstream and it is a gain or bias problem; absent means it is upstream and it is a broken path.
  4. Check the obvious physical things in parallel: is anything hot, is anything on backwards, is the part the one the BOM says.

"And I would look at the DC before anything else, because a saturated stage produces no output and looks identical to a dead stage on an AC measurement. That distinction is usually the whole fault."

What they are testing: whether you bisect or sweep. Sweeping left to right takes \(N\) measurements; bisecting takes \(\log_2 N\), and on a real board with twenty nodes that is the difference between an afternoon and five minutes. Say the word "bisect".
2 Trap Standard

Your scope shows a 3.3 V logic signal with 1.5 V of overshoot and heavy ringing. Is the signal bad?

How to answer

Possibly, but check the probe first — there are three ways to see ringing that is not there.

  • Ground lead inductance. Six inches is about 150 nH and resonates with the probe capacitance at roughly 100–200 MHz. Use a ground spring.
  • Probe compensation. An uncompensated 10× probe over- or under-shoots on every edge. Check it on the calibrator square wave before believing any edge.
  • Insufficient bandwidth — the opposite error. A 100 MHz scope on a 1 ns edge rounds it off and hides real ringing. \(BW \approx 0.35/t_r\), so a 1 ns edge needs 350 MHz to be seen honestly.

"If it survives all three, then it is real, and I would look for an impedance discontinuity: an unterminated line, a stub, or a return path interrupted by a plane split. The quick test is to add a series resistor at the source to match the trace impedance — if the ringing collapses, it was reflection."

What they are testing: whether you validate the measurement before acting on it. Both directions matter: candidates who only know about ground leads miss that too little bandwidth hides real problems.
3 Trap Harder

A circuit misbehaves, and it starts working correctly as soon as you put a scope probe on the suspect node. Now what?

How to answer

The probe is part of the circuit, and that is a clue rather than an annoyance.

A 10× probe adds about 10 pF and 10 MΩ. Ten picofarads is enough to:

  • Damp an oscillation, by adding a pole that costs the loop the gain it needed at the offending frequency.
  • Slow an edge enough to fix a race or a marginal setup time.
  • Provide a return path for leakage on a very high-impedance node, so a floating input stops floating.

"So the fault is telling me it is marginal at high frequency or at high impedance. I would switch to a low-capacitance active probe — under a picofarad — and see if the fault returns, which distinguishes 'the probe is loading it' from 'the probe is grounding it'. If a permanent small capacitor genuinely fixes it, that can be a legitimate solution, but only after I understand why, because a 10 pF fix has no margin and will come back at temperature."

What they are testing: whether you treat a Heisenbug as information. The bad answer is "leave the probe on it". The good one uses the loading as a diagnostic that narrows the mechanism.
4 Method Harder

One board in fifty fails. The other forty-nine are fine. How do you find it?

How to answer

Intermittent and rare, so the first job is not to find the fault — it is to make it happen on demand.

  1. Get a reliable reproduction. Freeze spray and a heat gun, component by component, will localise a temperature-sensitive part in minutes. Flexing the board finds cracked joints and cracked ceramics. Marginal supply voltage finds anything near a threshold.
  2. Compare, do not just inspect. Put the bad board next to a good one and diff everything: X-ray or AOI on the assembly, date codes, and the actual waveforms at the same nodes.
  3. Suspect the process before the design. One in fifty is a manufacturing rate, not a design rate. A design marginality usually shows up on all boards at some corner; a solder or component defect shows up on a few boards at every corner.
  4. Then check whether it is marginal by design. If the good boards are only just passing, the fifty-to-one is where the distribution crosses the limit, and the fix is margin, not rework.

"The distinguishing question is whether the forty-nine good boards have any margin. If they pass by a mile, it is a defect and I want the failure analysis. If they pass by a hair, the population is centred wrong and the one failure is just the tail — and fixing that one board fixes nothing."

What they are testing: statistical thinking about hardware. The insight that a rare failure is either a defect or a distribution problem, and that they need completely different responses, is what a manufacturing-aware engineer brings.
5 Rapid

What bandwidth do you need to measure a 2 ns rise time honestly?

Answer

\(BW = 0.35/t_r = 175\,\mathrm{MHz}\) to see it at all, and you want three to five times that to see it accurately — call it 500 MHz to 1 GHz. Remember the scope and probe combine in quadrature: \(t_{measured} = \sqrt{t_{actual}^2 + t_{scope}^2}\), so a 175 MHz scope measuring a 2 ns edge reports about 2.8 ns.

6 Rapid

When would you use a current probe instead of a voltage probe?

Answer

When you need to know where current is going rather than what a node is doing: finding which of five parallel branches draws the supply spike, measuring inductor current to check for saturation or DCM, or measuring common-mode current on a cable for EMI, which voltage probing simply cannot see. Also whenever the interesting node has no ground you can safely reference — a current probe is galvanically isolated, which is why it is the right instrument on the primary side of an offline supply.

7 Rapid

Your DMM reads 2.5 V on a node you expect to be 0 V or 5 V.

Answer

Most likely the node is floating and the meter's 10 MΩ input is forming a divider with leakage, or the node is switching and you are reading the average of a 50% duty cycle. Both are common and both are resolved the same way: put a scope on it. A meter reports an average and cannot distinguish a steady 2.5 V from a square wave, and that mistake accounts for a surprising share of confusing measurements.

If you remember four things

Power first, at the pin, under load
Most logic bugs are power bugs, and the regulator output is not where to look.
Bisect, do not sweep
\(\log_2 N\) instead of \(N\). Say the word in the interview.
Validate the instrument
Ground leads invent ringing; too little bandwidth hides it. Both are errors.
A rare failure is a distribution
Defect or margin — completely different responses. Ask how much margin the good boards have.
