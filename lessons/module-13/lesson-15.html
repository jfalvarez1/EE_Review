Multiphase and Coupled-Inductor Voltage Regulators

The buck converter in the earlier lessons is the one in the textbooks and it is not the one in a server, a graphics card or an AI accelerator. Those run a topology that looks superficially similar and has three additions the textbook version does not have: many phases, magnetically coupled inductors, and a control loop that is deliberately allowed to droop.

The specification that forced all of it. A modern processor core rail is roughly 12 V in, 0.7–1.1 V out, hundreds of amps, and the load can change by half its full current in under a microsecond. Nothing about that is a scaled-up version of a 3 A point-of-load converter — the numbers are far enough outside the textbook's assumptions that the topology had to change.
Why one phase cannot do it

A single-phase buck is one person with one bucket filling a tank that is being drained unpredictably. To keep up with a sudden demand they need a small bucket they can move quickly — but a small bucket means many trips, and each trip splashes, so the tank level ripples.

Interleaving is ten people with the same buckets, taking turns. The splashes now land at different moments and partly cancel, so the level is smoother even though each bucket is unchanged. Coupled inductors are the part that sounds impossible: the ten people are roped together in a way that makes each bucket feel heavy when they are taking turns, and light when they all rush at once.

Where it breaks: the rope is not a metaphor for cooperation, it is negative mutual inductance, and it works because the phases are 180°/N apart in steady state and in phase during a transient. Change the interleaving and the trick inverts.

1. What interleaving buys, exactly

Run \(N\) phases from the same input to the same output, switching \(360°/N\) apart. Four things improve, and only the first is obvious.

  • Current sharing. Each phase carries \(I_{out}/N\), so the FETs, the inductor and the copper all get easier.
  • Ripple cancellation at the output. The phase ripples do not add — they partly cancel, and at particular duty cycles they cancel completely.
  • Effective ripple frequency of \(N f_{sw}\). The output capacitor sees \(N\) times the switching rate, so the same ripple spec needs far less capacitance.
  • Thermal spreading. The loss is distributed across the board rather than concentrated in one package.
\[ K(D,N) = \frac{\bigl[(m+1) - ND\bigr]\bigl[ND - m\bigr]}{ND(1-D)}, \qquad m = \lfloor ND \rfloor \] The normalised output ripple: total ripple divided by what one phase alone would produce.
\[ D = \frac{k}{N} \;\Longrightarrow\; K = 0 \] Exact cancellation whenever the duty cycle is an integer multiple of \(1/N\). At 12 V in and 1.2 V out, \(D=0.1\), so ten phases cancel the output ripple entirely.
\[ N=2,\ D=0.1: K = 0.89 \qquad N=8,\ D=0.1: K = 0.22 \qquad N=10,\ D=0.1: K = 0 \] Two phases at this duty cancel almost nothing. The benefit is strongly duty-dependent, which is why phase count is chosen against the actual conversion ratio and not by rule of thumb.

2. The dilemma interleaving does not solve

Pick the inductor and you are choosing between two things that both matter and that pull in opposite directions.

\[ \Delta I_{ripple} = \frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{Lf_{sw}} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{L} \] Steady state wants L LARGE. Ripple current costs conduction loss, core loss, and output capacitance.
\[ \left|\frac{di}{dt}\right|_{max} = \frac{V_{in}-V_{out}}{L} \;\propto\; \frac{1}{L} \] Transient wants L SMALL. When the load steps, this is the fastest the inductor current can possibly follow, and everything the loop cannot supply comes out of the output capacitors.
\[ \text{One } L, \text{ two requirements, opposite directions.} \] In an uncoupled design this is a hard trade and you simply choose a point on it. Coupling is what removes the word "one".

3. Coupled inductors: two different inductances from one part

Wind two phases on a shared core so they are inversely coupled — mutual inductance \(M = \rho L\) with \(\rho\) negative, typically \(-0.2\) to \(-0.7\). Now write the two-winding equations and the trick appears on its own.

\[ v_1 = L\frac{di_1}{dt} + M\frac{di_2}{dt}, \qquad v_2 = M\frac{di_1}{dt} + L\frac{di_2}{dt} \]
\[ \frac{di_1}{dt} = \frac{L\,v_1 - M\,v_2}{L^2 - M^2} \] Invert the pair. Everything below is this one expression evaluated in two different situations.
\[ \textbf{Transient: } v_1 = v_2 = v \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{di}{dt} = \frac{v}{L+M} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{L_{tr} = L(1+\rho)} \] During a load step every phase drives in the same direction, so the mutual term SUBTRACTS from the self term. With \(\rho = -0.5\) the transient inductance is half the self inductance and the current slews twice as fast.
\[ \textbf{Steady state } (D<0.5): \; v_1 = V_{in}-V_{out},\; v_2 = -V_{out} \] Phase 1 is on while phase 2 is off — that is what interleaving means — so the two windings see opposite-signed voltages and the mutual term now ADDS.
\[ L_{eff} = \frac{v_1}{di_1/dt} = \frac{(V_{in}-V_{out})\,L\,(1-\rho^2)}{(V_{in}-V_{out}) + \rho V_{out}} \]
The comparison that shows the benefit

Comparing coupled and uncoupled at the same self inductance proves nothing, because the two designs would then have different transient speeds. The honest comparison is at equal transient inductance — give the coupled design a bigger self inductance so that \(L_{tr}\) matches. Same transient response, 37% less ripple current at \(\rho=-0.5\). That is less conduction loss, less core loss, less output capacitance and a smaller inductor — bought by winding two phases on one core instead of two.

What it costs. The magnetics are no longer a catalogue part — coupling coefficient becomes a specification you have to hold in production. Phases are no longer independent, so a phase-shedding controller that drops phases at light load changes the effective coupling of the ones remaining. And the current in an individual winding is no longer a clean triangle, which makes current sensing and per-phase balancing harder than in an uncoupled design.

A 12 V to 1.2 V regulator runs 8 phases. Someone proposes going to 10. What happens to the output ripple, and why is the answer not "it falls by 20%"?

  1. It falls by 20%, in proportion to the phase count
    Ripple cancellation is not proportional to N. It depends on where \(ND\) sits relative to the nearest integer, which is a sawtooth function of duty cycle rather than a smooth one.
  2. It falls essentially to zero, because \(D = 0.1 = 1/10\)
    Right. \(K = 0\) whenever \(D\) is an exact multiple of \(1/N\). At 12 V to 1.2 V the duty cycle is 0.1, so ten phases cancel completely while eight leave 22% of one phase's ripple. That is a step change, not a proportional one — and it is why phase count is chosen against the actual conversion ratio. Change the input to 13.2 V and D becomes 0.091, the exact cancellation is gone, and ten phases are only slightly better than eight.
  3. It rises, because more phases means more switching noise
    More phases raise the effective ripple frequency to \(Nf_{sw}\), which makes the output capacitor's job easier, not harder. The switching noise spectrum moves up; the ripple amplitude does not rise.
  4. It does not change, since each phase still has the same inductor
    Each phase's own ripple is unchanged, which is true and is not the question — the output sees the SUM of the phase ripples, and how they add depends on their phase relationship.

Set the interactive above to 8 phases and then 10 and watch K collapse. Then move \(V_{in}\) by a volt and watch the benefit largely disappear — which is the practical caution.

4. The trans-inductor VR, and where it came from

Coupled inductors improve the transient by a factor of \((1+\rho)\), which is bounded — you cannot push \(\rho\) to \(-1\) without the steady-state behaviour falling apart. As processor \(di/dt\) kept rising, that bound started to bind.

The trans-inductor voltage regulator attacks it differently. Each phase inductor gains a secondary winding, and all the secondaries are connected in series in a single loop with one shared compensating inductor \(L_c\). The primary side still behaves like an ordinary multiphase buck in steady state. But during a transient, a change in any one phase is transformer-coupled into the shared loop and therefore into every other phase at once.

\[ \text{Steady state: the loop current is small, phases behave normally} \]
\[ \text{Transient: all phases respond together, and } L_c \text{ sets how strongly} \] Smaller \(L_c\) means tighter coupling between phases and a faster collective response. \(L_c \to \infty\) is the ordinary uncoupled multiphase buck.

So \(L_c\) becomes a design knob that trades transient speed against steady-state ripple, independently of the main inductors. The costs are real: an extra magnetic component, a loop carrying current around the whole regulator, and current sensing that is harder still than the coupled-inductor case.

5. What else changed, and what it replaced

The textbook buck is not wrong — it is the 1990s point-of-load converter, and the assumptions underneath it have all moved. Each row below is a thing that changed and the reason it changed.

ThenNowWhy it moved
Discrete controller, driver, and two FETs Smart power stage / DrMOS: driver and both FETs in one package with current and temperature telemetry The gate loop inductance between a separate driver and FET became a limiting factor at high \(di/dt\). Integrating it shortened the loop and made per-phase current sensing accurate enough for real balancing.
One or two phases 8–20+ phases, with phase shedding at light load Hundreds of amps cannot be carried by one inductor, and \(K(D,N)\) rewards a phase count matched to the conversion ratio.
Independent inductors Coupled inductors, and TLVR where the transient demands it Load steps outran what a single inductance value could serve, as shown above.
Regulate to a fixed voltage Load line / adaptive voltage positioning: let the output droop deliberately with current If the rail is allowed to sit high at light load and low at heavy load, a load step starts from the top of the window and ends at the bottom, so the same tolerance band absorbs twice the excursion — and less output capacitance is needed. It also saves power at high load.
Analog compensation, fixed at design time Digital control with PMBus telemetry: per-rail voltage, current, temperature and fault logging A system with dozens of rails needs to be commissioned, monitored and sequenced by software, and compensation that can be tuned after layout is worth a great deal.
12 V distributed around the board 48 V distribution, converted near the load At constant power, four times the voltage is a quarter of the current and a sixteenth of the distribution loss. Rack-level 48 V is now normal in data centres.
48 V → 12 V → 1 V in two regulated stages Hybrid and switched-capacitor first stages: unregulated 4:1 or 8:1 divider feeding a regulated buck A switched-capacitor divider moves energy in capacitors rather than magnetics and can exceed 98% efficiency, because it is not regulating. Regulation is left to the stage that must do it anyway.
Regulator beside the processor Vertical power delivery: the last stage underneath the package At 500 A, a milliohm of PDN resistance is 250 W. Shortening the path from the regulator to the die stopped being a layout nicety and became the dominant loss.
Silicon MOSFETs everywhere GaN in the higher-voltage stages No body-diode reverse recovery and much lower gate charge, so the same efficiency at several times the switching frequency — which shrinks the magnetics, which is usually the point.
The thread running through all of it

Every one of those changes is a response to the same two pressures: current went up and \(di/dt\) went up. Higher current makes distribution loss dominate, which drives 48 V, vertical delivery and phase count. Higher \(di/dt\) makes the transient response dominate, which drives coupled inductors, TLVR, load line and digital control. If you can name which of the two a given technique serves, you understand why it exists.

Build it in Circuit Toy

Start from Two-Phase Interleaved Buck in the gallery — it already has two phases running 180° apart into a shared output.

  1. Run a transient and measure the output ripple. Then set the duty cycle to 0.5 by making \(V_{out} = V_{in}/2\) and watch it collapse: that is \(K(0.5, 2) = 0\), the exact-cancellation result, on a real solver.
  2. Move the duty away from 0.5 in small steps and plot the ripple against \(D\). You should get the sawtooth shape of \(K(D,N)\), not a smooth curve.
  3. Halve both inductors and re-run. Ripple doubles and the transient recovery gets faster — the trade in section 2, measured rather than asserted.
  4. Compare against Buck Converter driven at the same total current to see what the second phase was worth.
