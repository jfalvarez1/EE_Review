Interview Prep — Power Supply Design

Power supply interviews reward people who think in loops — current loops, control loops and thermal loops — and punish people who think in schematics. Almost every question below is really asking "where does the current go, and what does that path enclose?"

The relationships worth having instantly.
Buck
Vout = D·Vin
Boost
Vout = Vin/(1−D)
Inductor ripple
ΔI = V·t/L = Vout(1−D)/(L·f)
Capacitor ripple
ΔV = ΔI·ESR + ΔI/(8·f·C)
Design point
ΔI ≈ 30% of Iout
Thermal
Tj = Ta + P·θja

Part 1 — Design it

1 Design Standard

12 V to 3.3 V at 3 A, 500 kHz. Pick the inductor and the output capacitor, and justify both.

How to answer
\[ D = \frac{3.3}{12} = 0.275 \]
\[ \Delta I = 0.3 \times 3\,\mathrm{A} = 0.9\,\mathrm{A} \] 30% is the standard starting point: smaller inductors mean higher ripple current and more core and capacitor loss; larger ones cost size, DCR and transient response.
\[ L = \frac{V_{out}(1-D)}{\Delta I\,f} = \frac{3.3(0.725)}{0.9\times500\times10^3} = 5.3\,\mu\mathrm{H} \;\rightarrow\; 4.7\,\mu\mathrm{H} \]
\[ I_{sat} > I_{out} + \frac{\Delta I}{2} = 3.45\,\mathrm{A}, \text{ specify } \geq 5\,\mathrm{A} \] Saturation rating against PEAK, not average, with margin for a transient. Saturating an inductor is a hard failure, not a soft one.
\[ \Delta V = \Delta I\cdot\mathrm{ESR} + \frac{\Delta I}{8fC} \]
\[ \text{For } 33\,\mathrm{mV} \text{ with } 5\,\mathrm{m}\Omega: \ 0.9(0.005) = 4.5\,\mathrm{mV}; \ C \geq \frac{0.9}{8(5\times10^5)(0.028)} = 8\,\mu\mathrm{F} \]

"So on paper 10 µF is enough for ripple — but ripple is almost never what sizes the output capacitor. The load step is. If this rail has to hold 100 mV during a 1.5 A step and the loop takes 20 µs to respond, I need \(C = I\Delta t/\Delta V = 1.5\times20\mu/0.1 = 300\,\mu\mathrm{F}\) of effective capacitance. And with ceramics I would specify against the DC-bias derating, not the printed value — a 22 µF X5R at 3.3 V can be 12 µF."

What they are testing: whether you know ripple is the easy constraint and transient is the real one. Every candidate computes the ripple capacitor; the ones who then say "but the load step needs thirty times that" have built supplies. The ceramic derating remark is a second strong signal.
2 Derive Standard

Where do you place the input capacitor, and why does it matter more than the output one?

How to answer

Because the input loop is the one carrying discontinuous current, and a discontinuous current in a loop of area \(A\) is an antenna.

\[ \text{Buck input current: 0 to } I_{out} \text{ in nanoseconds, every cycle} \] The high-side FET chops it. The output inductor current, by contrast, is continuous and boring.
\[ V_{spike} = L_{loop}\frac{di}{dt} = (10\,\mathrm{nH})\frac{3\,\mathrm{A}}{5\,\mathrm{ns}} = 6\,\mathrm{V} \] Ten nanohenries is about a centimetre of trace. Six volts of overshoot on a 12 V rail is a 16.5 V transient on a FET you may have rated at 20 V.

"So the input ceramic goes immediately across the high-side drain and the low-side source, on the same layer, with the shortest possible loop and a ground plane directly underneath to cancel the field. It is the single most important placement decision on a switching supply layout — get it wrong and you get ringing, EMI, and FETs that fail from avalanche rather than heat. The output loop is far more forgiving because the inductor keeps its current continuous."

What they are testing: whether you can identify the hot loop. "Input cap close to the FETs" is the answer, but the reason — discontinuous current, \(L\,di/dt\), loop area — is what they want, and it should come with a number.
3 Estimate Harder

An LDO drops 5 V to 3.3 V at 500 mA in a SOT-223 with \(\theta_{JA} = 60\,\mathrm{°C/W}\), ambient 70 °C. Is it thermally acceptable?

How to answer
\[ P = (V_{in}-V_{out})I = (1.7)(0.5) = 0.85\,\mathrm{W} \]
\[ T_j = 70 + (0.85)(60) = 121\,\mathrm{°C} \] Under a 125 °C limit, but only by 4 °C — and \(\theta_{JA}\) is quoted on a JEDEC board with a specific copper area you almost certainly do not have.
\[ \eta = \frac{3.3}{5} = 66\% \]

"So technically inside the limit and not a design I would ship. The margin is inside the tolerance of the thermal model, and it assumes ambient never exceeds 70. I would either add copper and check against the datasheet's \(\theta_{JA}\)-versus-area curve, move to a package with an exposed pad, or ask whether this should be a switcher at all — at 66% efficiency a buck would put perhaps 0.15 W into the package instead of 0.85. The reason to stay with the LDO would be noise: if this rail feeds a PLL or an ADC reference, the switcher's ripple may cost more than the heat does."

What they are testing: whether you treat a datasheet thermal number as a measurement or as a claim about a test board. And whether you can hold the LDO-versus- switcher trade in both directions rather than reflexively preferring efficiency.

Part 2 — Diagnose it

4 Trap Standard

You measure 800 mV of ripple on a rail that should have 20 mV. The supply is fine. What did you do wrong?

How to answer

Measured it with the probe's ground lead.

A six-inch ground lead is roughly 150 nH. Together with the probe's input capacitance it forms a resonant loop, and the switching edges ring it. You are looking at an artefact of the measurement, not the rail.

\[ f_{ring} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{(150\mathrm{nH})(12\mathrm{pF})}} \approx 120\,\mathrm{MHz} \] Which is why the "ripple" looks like a burst of high-frequency ringing on each edge rather than a sawtooth at the switching frequency.

"The right way is a ground spring — the little barrel tip with a spring ground, giving a loop of a few millimetres — or the tip-and-barrel technique. And AC coupling with the vertical scale turned up, because you are looking for 20 mV on top of 3.3 V. If the ringing survives that, then it is real and I would look at the hot loop from question 2."

What they are testing: measurement literacy. This is the most common false alarm in power work, and being able to name the ground spring and estimate the ring frequency proves you have made this mistake and learned from it.
5 Trap Harder

A supply works on the bench and fails at power-up in the product. Same board. What kinds of things cause that?

How to answer

Almost always something about the rate or the order, neither of which a bench supply reproduces.

  • Inrush. The bench supply ramps; the product hot-plugs. Charging the bulk capacitance through the source impedance can trip the upstream current limit, and a supply that trips and retries can latch into hiccup forever.
  • Sequencing. A part with a core and an I/O rail usually specifies which comes up first and by how much. Get it backwards and internal ESD structures conduct from the I/O rail into the unpowered core — sometimes latch-up, sometimes just a device that never starts.
  • Soft-start into a constant-power load. A downstream switcher looks like a negative incremental resistance: as its input voltage falls it draws MORE current. During a slow ramp that can prevent the upstream rail from ever coming up.
  • Pre-bias. If a rail already has voltage on it at start-up, a synchronous buck can sink current and pull it down before regulating, which upsets whatever is holding it.

"So I would scope the rails together on a slow timebase during an actual cold plug-in, not a bench power-on, and look at order and slew rate before suspecting the regulator. The bench supply's soft ramp is the difference, and it hides all four of these."

What they are testing: whether you know that a bench supply is not a power system. "Works on the bench, fails in the product" is a real and common report, and the number of distinct mechanisms you can name maps directly to how many you have debugged.
6 Trap Nasty

A design passes conducted emissions on the first board and fails by 10 dB on production units. Nothing changed in the schematic.

How to answer

Something changed that is not in the schematic. In rough order of likelihood:

  • A second-source component. A different inductor with different shielding, or a common-mode choke from another vendor with different leakage inductance, will change the spectrum substantially while meeting the same printed specification.
  • A layout revision. "Nothing changed in the schematic" is not "nothing changed on the board". A moved stitching via or a widened gap in the return plane changes the loop area and therefore the emissions.
  • Spread spectrum turned off. Many controllers have a dithering option that lowers the measured peak by 8– 12 dB without reducing total energy. A different bootstrap resistor or strap setting is easy to miss.
  • Cable and fixture differences. The cable is usually the antenna, and the first board was probably tested with a different one.

"The reason it is 10 dB rather than 1 dB points at either the dithering or the cable, because those are the two things that change the measurement by that much. I would compare the spectra rather than just the pass/fail — if the peaks moved in frequency it is the inductor or the clock; if they stayed put and got taller it is loop area or dithering."

What they are testing: whether you investigate systematically under commercial pressure. The best answer is diagnostic — comparing spectra to distinguish causes — rather than a list of things to try.
7 Rapid

Why put a 0.1 µF and a 10 µF next to each other instead of one 10.1 µF?

Answer

Because a capacitor is only capacitive below its self-resonant frequency, and SRF falls as capacitance rises — a bigger part means a bigger body and more ESL. The 10 µF handles bulk energy up to a few megahertz; the 0.1 µF, in a smaller package with less inductance, stays capacitive to tens of megahertz. The caution is that between the two SRFs there is an anti-resonance where the parallel combination is inductive and impedance peaks, so more values are not always better — a decade apart is a reasonable rule and three staggered values is usually the practical maximum.

8 Rapid

Where does the ground plane cut go in a mixed-signal board?

Answer

Usually nowhere. A split plane forces return current to detour around the gap, which increases loop area and radiates — the exact problem the split was meant to solve. The modern practice is one solid ground plane with careful partitioning: place the analog and digital sections in separate areas so their return currents naturally stay under their own signals, and never route a fast signal across a gap. A split is defensible only when there is a genuine isolation requirement, and then the barrier is deliberate and nothing crosses it.

If you remember four things

Find the discontinuous loop
Input side on a buck. Shortest possible, plane underneath. It is the layout decision that matters.
Transient sizes the output cap
Ripple is the easy constraint. \(C = I\Delta t/\Delta V\) is the real one.
Probe grounds lie
Six inches of ground lead invents ripple that is not there. Use a spring.
Bench supplies lie too
They ramp. Products hot-plug, and sequencing and inrush only appear then.
