Design: Discharging a Rail to a Deadline

Somebody hands you a requirement: this rail must be below X volts within Y milliseconds of shutdown. That is a complete specification and it has a closed-form answer, which makes it a good first design to do end to end — brief, equation, numbers, real parts, corners, and a way to prove it on the bench.

Why anyone asks for this. Three reasons, and they push the design in different directions.
  • Clean restart. A synchronous buck starting into a rail that still has charge on it will sink current to pull it down to its soft-start ramp. That reverse current has nowhere good to go and can damage the low-side FET. Sequencers and PMICs therefore require rails to be at zero before the next start.
  • Safety. Standards such as IEC 61010 require accessible parts to fall below 60 V within a set time after disconnection — typically 5 seconds. On a 400 V bus this is the requirement that sizes the circuit.
  • Service. An engineer opening the enclosure should not find a charged bulk capacitor. This is the same requirement as safety but with a longer allowance.

1. The one equation, and what it does not tell you

\[ V(t) = V_0\,e^{-t/RC} \] A capacitor discharging into a resistor. No approximation, no small-signal assumption.
\[ t = RC\ln\!\frac{V_0}{V_f} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{R = \frac{t}{C\,\ln(V_0/V_f)}} \] Rearranged for the thing you are choosing. Everything else in the brief is given.
\[ E = \tfrac{1}{2}CV_0^2 \] And this is the number the equation above hides. It does NOT depend on R. Whatever resistor you pick, the same joules end up in it — R only decides how fast.
A way to hold this

A capacitor discharging through a resistor is a bucket of water draining through a hole. The hole size is R and the bucket size is C. Two things follow immediately and they are the two things people get backwards.

First, the water drains fastest at the start, when the depth is greatest, and the last inch takes forever — which is why the answer is an exponential rather than a straight line, and why "how long to reach zero" has no answer while "how long to reach a tenth" has a clean one. That is the whole reason a specification must name a target voltage and not just a time.

Second, and this is the useful part: the amount of water is fixed by the bucket, not by the hole. Drill a bigger hole and it empties sooner, but exactly the same volume comes out. That is \(E=\frac12CV_0^2\) with no R in it, and it is why the resistor is chosen for the energy it must swallow separately from the value that sets the time.

Where it breaks: water drains at a rate set by \(\sqrt{\text{depth}}\), not by depth, so a real bucket does not empty exponentially. The analogy is right about what depends on what and wrong about the shape of the curve — take the reasoning from it and the exponential from the algebra.
The two ways this design fails

A resistor permanently across a rail dissipates \(V_0^2/R\) forever, not just during shutdown. On a low-voltage rail that is a rounding error; on a high-voltage bus it is a heater. That is the first failure, and it is the one that decides between a passive bleeder and a switched one.

The second is that the resistor has to absorb \(\frac12CV_0^2\) in a single pulse lasting a few time constants. A part rated for 1 W continuous is not automatically able to take 40 joules in five seconds, and thick-film resistors fail by cracking rather than by getting warm. Check the manufacturer's single-pulse curve, not the power rating.

Same equation, opposite conclusion

On the 1.2 V rail the constraint was time and the energy was nothing. On the 400 V bus the time constraint is easy and the constraints are standing power and pulse energy. Recognising which of the three binds is most of the design work, and it is why the calculator below reports all three rather than just the resistor.

Halve the resistor. What happens to the energy the resistor has to absorb?

  1. It doubles, because the peak current doubles
    The peak current does double, and the peak power does too — but only for half as long. Power is not energy.
  2. It halves, because the discharge is over sooner
    The discharge is indeed twice as fast, but the charge that has to leave the capacitor has not changed.
  3. It does not change
    Right. \(E = \frac12CV_0^2\) contains no R at all. Every joule that was stored in the capacitor ends up in the resistor whatever its value; R only sets how long that takes and therefore the peak power. This is why a discharge resistor is selected on pulse energy and not on power rating.
  4. It depends on the capacitor's ESR
    ESR shares a little of the dissipation, but it is milliohms against ohms or kilohms here — under a percent. The stored energy is still fixed by C and \(V_0\).

So the resistor choice is two independent questions: what value meets the deadline, and what construction survives \(\frac12CV_0^2\) delivered in a few time constants.

4. Switching it: the circuit, and the part everyone gets wrong

The discharge FET is off whenever the gate is held low. Q1 does that, and Q1's base is driven from a rail that is still alive. When that rail collapses, R2 pulls M1's gate up and the discharge starts.

Where does the gate drive come from? This is the question that decides whether the circuit works, and it is not obvious, because the thing you are discharging is by definition the thing that is dying.
  • From a rail that outlives it. The clean answer in a sequenced system. The shutdown order is the reverse of the start-up order, so when the 1.2 V core is being discharged the 3.3 V rail is still up — and 3.3 V comfortably enhances a logic-level FET whose threshold is around 1 V.
  • From the rail itself, through a pull-up. Simple and self-powered, and it stops working at \(V_{GS} = V_{th}\). A 12 V rail discharged this way stalls somewhere around 2 V and stays there, which fails a specification written as "below 1 V". If you use this, write the spec against \(V_{th}\), not against zero.
  • From a held-up local supply. A small capacitor and a diode charge from the rail during normal operation and keep a gate driver alive through the discharge. This is what high-voltage drives do, because a 400 V bus has no friendly neighbour rail.

Sizing the FET

\[ R_{DS(on)} \ll R \] The FET is a switch, not the resistor. If \(R_{DS(on)}\) is a meaningful fraction of R, the discharge time depends on the FET's temperature — which is not a design, it is a coincidence.
\[ V_{DS(max)} > V_0 \text{ with margin}, \qquad I_D > \frac{V_0}{R} \]
\[ \text{Check } R_{DS(on)} \text{ at the ACTUAL } V_{GS} \] Datasheets headline \(R_{DS(on)}\) at \(V_{GS}=10\,\mathrm{V}\). At 3.3 V it can be two or three times higher, and on a non-logic-level part the FET may not turn on at all.

5. The corners

A design is not finished at the nominal calculation. Four things move, and they do not all move the same way.

What variesBy how muchEffect on discharge timeWhat to do
CapacitanceElectrolytic −20/+80%; ceramic loses 50–80% to DC bias Directly proportional. The high tolerance corner is the one that misses the deadline. Design against \(C_{max}\), and use the DC-bias-derated value for ceramics rather than the printed one.
Resistor±1% typical, ±5% for pulse-rated parts Directly proportional. Small compared with the capacitor. Rounding down to E24 usually swamps this.
TemperatureElectrolytic capacitance rises at high temperature, falls sharply below 0 °C Slower when hot, faster when cold. The hot corner is worst. Check at maximum ambient with maximum capacitance together — the corners correlate.
Load still attachedAnything from nothing to most of the discharge Helps, and you must not rely on it. Design for no load. A downstream device in reset may draw almost nothing.
The worst case is not the nominal times a margin. With \(C\) at +80% and hot, a 47 ms nominal discharge becomes 85 ms and misses a 50 ms deadline outright. Put \(C_{max}\) into the calculator above and size the resistor from that, then confirm the peak current at \(C_{min}\) is still acceptable. Designing at nominal and adding 20% is how these fail in production.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse 10 V applied to a rail designed for 1.2 V The discharge resistor is the first thing to find out. \(P = V^2/R = 100/12 = \mathbf{8.3\ W}\) in a part sized for a 1.6 mJ pulse — typically an 0603 rated at 0.1 W. That is 83 times its rating. And if the FET is on, its \(R_{DS(on)}\) carries 833 mA. The 0603 resistor opens within a second or two, usually with a visible mark, and in doing so it disconnects the discharge path — which is a benign failure, because the circuit's job is only needed at shutdown. Design it that way deliberately: a small resistor is a fuse you did not have to buy. What is not acceptable is the resistor surviving while the FET fails short, which puts a permanent 12 Ω across the rail.
Abuse EN and the rail fail together (a shared upstream supply dies) R2 pulls M1's gate up from the rail being discharged. As the rail falls, so does \(V_{GS}\), and below \(V_{th}\) the FET turns off with charge still on the capacitor. The rail stalls at roughly \(V_{th}\) — 1 to 2 V — and stays there. This is a design failure, not a component failure, and it is why the gate drive must come from a rail that outlives this one. If it cannot, write the specification against \(V_{th}\) rather than against zero.
Abuse EN left floating (unpopulated connector, broken track) Q1's base floats. Leakage and noise decide whether it conducts, so the discharge FET may be on, off, or somewhere in between — the last being the dangerous one, since a partly-on FET is a resistor dissipating continuously. A pull-up on Q1's base to the EN rail makes the default state "discharge off". Never leave a gate or a base to decide for itself; a floating input is a coin toss made at the factory.
Abuse Rail shorted to ground while EN is alive Nothing, from this circuit's point of view. The FET is held off and the resistor sees no voltage. No action. Worth confirming so that the discharge circuit is eliminated as a suspect when someone is debugging a shorted rail.
Abuse Repeated fast power cycling, 10 Hz for an hour The resistor absorbs \(\frac12CV_0^2\) = 1.6 mJ per cycle, so 16 mW average. Trivial here. On the 400 V bus of worked example B it would be 37.6 J × 10 = 376 W average, which no discharge resistor survives. At low voltage, no action. At high voltage, the repetition rate is a specification and the resistor must be rated for average power as well as single-pulse energy. This is the case people forget, because a discharge resistor is instinctively treated as a one-shot part.
The overvoltage row is the general lesson. Ask of every component: what does it do when the input is three times what it should be? The good answer is that something cheap and identifiable fails first, in a way that leaves the rest safe. The bad answer is that everything survives except one expensive part, or that nothing fails but everything degrades. Choosing which part is the weakest is a design decision, and if you do not make it, the layout makes it for you.

7. Proving it on the bench

  1. Scope on the rail, DC coupled, trigger on the falling edge with a threshold just below \(V_0\). Single shot.
  2. Cut power at the input, not at the load. You are testing the shutdown path, and pulling the load changes the discharge.
  3. Measure the time from the trigger to the target voltage, with cursors. Compare against the calculator.
  4. Repeat with the board at maximum operating temperature and, if you can, with a high-tolerance capacitor lot. This is the corner that fails.
  5. Check the resistor with a thermal camera or a fingertip a second after discharge. A part that is noticeably hot after one event is being asked for more pulse energy than it wants.
  6. Finally, cycle power rapidly — off and immediately on — and confirm the downstream converter starts cleanly every time. That is the requirement the whole circuit exists to satisfy, and it is the only test that checks it directly.
