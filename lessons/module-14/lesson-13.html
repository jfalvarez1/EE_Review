Design: An Analog Power Sequencer

A modern processor or FPGA has three to six rails and a datasheet page that says which order they must arrive in, how close together, and what happens if you get it wrong. That page is a specification, and this lesson turns it into a circuit — three ways, with the tolerance analysis that decides between them.

1. Why the order matters, physically

The rule is not arbitrary and it is not about inrush. It is about the structures inside the die that exist to protect the pins.

\[ \text{Every I/O pin has an ESD diode to its own I/O rail} \] Standard practice, and the reason a pin can survive being touched.
\[ \text{If the I/O rail is up while the core is at 0 V} \] Internal level shifters and shared wells now have a forward-biased path from the powered rail into the unpowered one. Current flows where the designer never intended it to.
\[ \Longrightarrow \text{CMOS latch-up} \] The parasitic PNPN structure between an n-well PMOS and a substrate NMOS is an SCR. Trigger it and it becomes a low-impedance path from supply to ground that STAYS ON until power is removed — and if the supply can deliver enough current, the die is destroyed before anyone notices.
Why reverse order on the way down

Powering up in the wrong order is like filling the upper floors of a building before the lower ones — the water has to find its way down through structures that were built for a different purpose, and something gives.

Shutdown is the same problem read backwards. If the core rail collapses first, the I/O rail is momentarily high against a dead core — exactly the forbidden state, arrived at from the other direction. That is why the shutdown order is the reverse of the start-up order and not simply "everything off at once": at once is not achievable, and whichever rail happens to have the least capacitance wins.

Where it breaks: water finds its own level and charge does not — the actual mechanism is a forward-biased junction and a latching parasitic SCR, which has no hydraulic analogue and behaves very differently once triggered. The picture is right about ordering and says nothing about why the fault persists.

2. Method 1 — RC delay on the enable pin

The cheapest thing that could work: an RC from the previous rail to the next regulator's enable pin. Two components per rail.

\[ V_{EN}(t) = V\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right) \]
\[ t_{delay} = RC\ln\!\frac{1}{1 - V_{th}/V} \]
\[ V = 3.3\,\mathrm{V},\; V_{th} = 1.2\,\mathrm{V} \;\Longrightarrow\; t = 0.452\,RC \]
\[ RC = \frac{10\,\mathrm{ms}}{0.452} = 22.1\,\mathrm{ms} \;\Rightarrow\; C = 100\,\mathrm{nF},\ R = 221\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \]
And now the tolerance, which is the whole story

The enable threshold on a regulator is not 1.2 V. It is specified as a range, typically 0.8 V to 1.6 V. Put that spread through the same RC:

Enable thresholdActual delayAgainst a 10 ms target
0.8 V (minimum)6.1 ms−39%
1.2 V (typical)10.0 msnominal
1.6 V (maximum)14.7 ms+47%

A 2.4:1 spread from one component's datasheet tolerance alone, before adding the resistor, the capacitor, or temperature. And it is worse than it looks: the delays are measured from when the previous rail started rising, not from when it was valid, so the error accumulates down a chain of three.

When it is still the right answer: when the requirement is an ordering rather than a timing — "3.3 V after 1.2 V, at least 1 ms apart, no maximum" — and the RC is sized so even the fastest corner satisfies the minimum. Many datasheets are written exactly that way, and then two components is the correct engineering answer.

3. Method 2 — a comparator on the previous rail

Instead of timing a delay, measure the thing you actually care about: whether the previous rail is up. The next regulator is enabled when the previous one crosses a threshold.

One stage of the chain. R1 and R2 set the fraction of the previous rail at which the next one is released; the comparator's own reference sets the accuracy. Repeat it per rail and the enables cascade.

\[ V_{trip} = V_{ref}\left(1 + \frac{R_1}{R_2}\right) \] Set to about 90% of the previous rail. Now the threshold accuracy is the reference's — 1% or better — instead of a regulator enable pin's 2:1.
\[ \text{Add hysteresis: } R_h \text{ from output to the + input} \] Without it, supply noise around the trip point makes the comparator chatter and the downstream regulator start and stop. 50–100 mV is plenty.
\[ t_{delay} \text{ now depends on the RAIL's ramp, not on an RC} \] Which is the point: if the previous rail is slow because it is heavily loaded, the next one waits. An RC timer does not know that and fires anyway.
The property that makes this worth the parts

An RC delay answers "has enough time passed?" A comparator answers "is the rail actually up?" Those are the same question only when nothing unusual happens. On a cold start into a large load, into a partially charged bulk capacitor, or when a regulator is in current limit, they are different questions and only the second one has the right answer.

The argument for the IC is usually the fault case, not the sequence. Getting three rails up in order is easy. Getting them all down, in reverse, within microseconds, because rail two just went into current limit — that is what a discrete chain does badly and a sequencer does as its main job. If a rail failing while the others stay up would damage the device, the IC has already paid for itself.

Your RC-delayed sequencer works on every board you build. A production batch arrives where the 3.3 V rail sometimes comes up before the 1.2 V rail. Nothing in the design changed. What is the most likely cause?

  1. The RC components are out of tolerance
    1% resistors and 10% capacitors cannot produce an ordering inversion from a 10 ms nominal delay. The numbers do not reach.
  2. The 3.3 V regulator is faster than it used to be
    Ramp rate affects when a rail becomes valid, not when it is enabled. It cannot make an enabled-later rail start earlier.
  3. The 1.2 V regulator's soft-start is now slower than the RC delay
    Right, and it is the classic one. The RC times from when the first regulator is enabled, not from when its rail is up. If the 1.2 V rail is heavily loaded — or a second-source part has a longer soft-start, or its output capacitance grew — then the 1.2 V rail may still be ramping when the RC releases the 3.3 V one. The delay was measured against the wrong event all along, and it worked only because the two happened to be close. This is exactly the failure a comparator cannot have, because it measures the rail rather than the clock.
  4. Board-to-board capacitance variation
    A contributor to the ramp time, and the mechanism above is the reason it matters. Naming the mechanism is the answer; naming a contributor is not.

Increase the soft-start slider above until the bands overlap and watch the verdict fail. Then switch the method to comparator and watch it become impossible by construction.

5. Shutdown, which is where the discharge circuit connects

Coming down is harder than going up, because you no longer control the energy — the rails coast down on their own bulk capacitance, at rates set by their loads.

\[ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{I_{load}}{C_{bulk}} \] A lightly loaded rail with a lot of capacitance falls slowly; a heavily loaded one collapses. Nothing about that ordering is under your control.
\[ \text{1.2 V core: heavily loaded, little capacitance} \Rightarrow \text{falls FIRST} \] Which is precisely the forbidden state: I/O up against a dead core. The natural shutdown order is usually the wrong one.

So shutdown needs the same active intervention as start-up, in reverse: disable in reverse order, and discharge each rail deliberately so it falls when you say rather than when its load decides. That is the circuit from the previous lesson, and this is why it exists — the discharge FET's enable comes from the sequencer, and its gate drive comes from a rail that has not been turned off yet.

The two lessons compose. The sequencer decides the order; the discharge circuits make the falling edges happen on schedule rather than on load. Neither works properly without the other, and a design that sequences the enables but lets the rails coast down has solved half the problem — the easier half.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse 3.3 V rail forced up first, core held at 0 Internal level shifters forward-bias into the unpowered core. Current flows through structures never designed to carry it, and the parasitic PNPN between an n-well PMOS and the substrate NMOS can trigger. CMOS latch-up: a low-impedance path from 3.3 V to ground that stays on until power is removed. If the 3.3 V regulator can deliver an amp, the die reaches its fusing temperature in milliseconds. If it current-limits, the part gets hot and survives, and you have a board that works after a power cycle and nobody knows why. Series resistance in the offending path or a current-limited rail turns destruction into a puzzle; correct sequencing prevents both.
Abuse 10 V applied to the enable input expecting 3.3 The enable pin is usually rated to its own supply plus 0.3 V. Above that, its ESD diode conducts into the regulator's \(V_{IN}\) or \(V_{OUT}\) node. Through a typical 100 kΩ series resistor that is only \((10-3.6)/100\mathrm{k} = 64\,\mu\mathrm{A}\). Nothing happens, and that is worth knowing: the high impedance an enable pin already needs makes it accidentally robust. Contrast with a gate driven through 100 Ω, where the same 10 V is 60 mA and destroys the controller. The same abuse, three orders of magnitude apart in consequence, because of one resistor.
Abuse One rail shorted while the others are up That regulator current-limits or hiccups. The others carry on, so the device sits with two of three rails present — which is a state the datasheet forbids and which no amount of start-up sequencing addressed. Any rail's power-good going false must shut the whole chain down in reverse order. A discrete comparator chain does not do this unless you build it; a sequencer IC does it as its main function. This single row is usually the argument for the IC.
Abuse Power cycled every 50 ms for an hour Rails do not fully discharge between cycles. The next start-up begins with charge already on the output capacitors, so a synchronous buck sinks current to pull its output down to the soft-start ramp. Discharge circuits sized so every rail is below 0.2 V before the next enable. Without them the low-side FET carries reverse current with nowhere to send it, and pre-biased start-up is a documented failure mode with its own section in most controller datasheets.
Abuse Enable left floating A high-impedance CMOS input with nothing driving it. Leakage and coupling decide the state, and the intermediate state is the worst one: a regulator in linear mode rather than switching. A pull-down on every enable, so the default state is off. The same rule as a MOSFET gate, for the same reason — a floating input is a coin toss made at the factory.
The comparison worth extracting from row two. Ten volts into an enable pin behind 100 kΩ is 64 µA and nothing happens. Ten volts into a FET gate behind 100 Ω is 60 mA and the controller dies. Same abuse, same voltage, three orders of magnitude apart — and the only difference is a resistor chosen for a different reason. When you size a series resistor, you are choosing what survives.

7. Build it in Circuit Toy

The gallery has the pieces: Comparator with hysteresis, Window comparator (OV/UV detection) and Voltage Divider for the sensing chain, plus Buck Converter for the rails themselves.

  1. Build the RC-delay version: a 3.3 V step through 221 kΩ and 100 nF into a comparator whose reference is 1.2 V. Measure the delay and confirm 0.452 RC.
  2. Now move the reference to 0.8 V and then 1.6 V without changing the RC. The delay should go 6.1, 10.0, 14.7 ms — the tolerance table in section 2, measured.
  3. Open Comparator with hysteresis and confirm the two trip points differ by the amount the feedback resistor sets. Then remove the hysteresis and inject noise on the input to see the chatter it prevents.
  4. Finally build the comparator-on-the-rail version and slow the first rail's ramp down. The RC version fires anyway; the comparator version waits. That difference is the checkpoint, made visible.
