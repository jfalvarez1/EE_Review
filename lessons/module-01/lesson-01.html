The Ideal Amplifier

An amplifier is a specification before it is a circuit. Long before you know whether it is built from BJTs, FETs or an op-amp, you can say what it must do, and four numbers say it: gain, input impedance, output impedance, and bandwidth.

This lesson is about the first three. They are not decoration on top of gain — they are the reason a gain of 10 built by a careful person measures 9.09 on the bench.

Why this comes first: every configuration in the next lesson (inverting, non-inverting, buffer, summing, difference) is solved with two rules and no knowledge of transistors whatsoever. You can design working circuits for months before you open the box. The box gets opened later in the path, as the answer to “how was that built”, not as a prerequisite.

1. Why there are exactly four kinds of amplifier

An amplifier's input is either a voltage or a current. Its output is either a voltage or a current. Two choices, two ports, four combinations — and that is the whole list. There is no fifth kind.

TypeInOutGain, and its units Ideal ZinIdeal Zout
Voltage amplifierVV Av = Vout/Vin  [V/V] ∞0

The impedance columns are not arbitrary conventions to memorise. Each one follows in one line from what the amplifier is trying not to disturb, and section 2 derives them.

Where this returns: those same four combinations reappear later as the four feedback topologies — series-shunt, series-series, shunt-shunt, shunt-series. That is not a coincidence. Feedback is how an engineer builds a chosen row of this table on purpose, by raising or lowering each impedance deliberately.

2. The loading theorem, derived

Nothing you measure is an ideal source. A real signal source is a Thévenin equivalent: an open-circuit voltage \(v_s\) behind a series impedance \(R_s\) — a microphone's coil resistance, a bridge arm, the output impedance of the stage before yours. Connect an amplifier of input impedance \(\zin\) and you have built a divider whether you meant to or not.

\[ v_{in} = v_s \cdot \frac{\zin}{R_s + \zin} \] Voltage division. \(\zin\) and \(R_s\) carry the same current, so the input sees the fraction of \(v_s\) that falls across it.
\[ \varepsilon \;\equiv\; \frac{v_s - v_{in}}{v_s} \;=\; 1 - \frac{\zin}{R_s+\zin} \;=\; \frac{R_s}{R_s + \zin} \] Define the fractional error as the signal you failed to capture, over the signal that was available. The \(\zin\) terms cancel to leave something with only one ratio in it.
\[ \varepsilon = \frac{R_s}{R_s+\zin} = \frac{R_s/\zin}{1 + R_s/\zin} \approx \frac{R_s}{\zin} \quad\text{for } \zin \gg R_s \] Divide top and bottom by \(\zin\). When the ratio \(R_s/\zin\) is small the denominator is nearly 1, and the error collapses to the impedance ratio itself.
Result — the rule worth memorising \[ \boxed{\;\varepsilon \approx \frac{R_s}{\zin}\;} \]

The fractional signal loss is just the ratio of the two impedances. Want to lose less than \(0.1\%\)? Make \(\zin \geq 1000\,R_s\). That is the entire content of “input impedance should be high”, and unlike the slogan it tells you how high.

The output does the same thing against the load, in mirror image. With an open-circuit gain \(A_{v0}\) and output impedance \(\zout\) driving a load \(R_L\):

\[ v_L = A_{v0}\,v_{in}\cdot\frac{R_L}{\zout + R_L} \] The same divider, formed by \(\zout\) against the load.
\[ A_v \;=\; \frac{v_L}{v_s} \;=\; A_{v0}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{\zin}{R_s+\zin}}_{\text{input loss}}\cdot\underbrace{\frac{R_L}{\zout+R_L}}_{\text{output loss}} \] Chain the two. The gain you actually measure is the device's gain multiplied by two efficiencies, both less than one.

And now the other three rows of the table follow with no new ideas. If the input is a current, you want to capture all of it, so \(\zin\) must be small — a short circuit takes the whole current and leaves none in the source's shunt resistance. If the output is a current, it must not care what you hang on it, so \(\zout\) must be large. Four rows, one argument, applied twice.

3. Cascading: why the losses compound

Stages do not sit in isolation. Stage \(k\)'s output impedance is stage \(k{+}1\)'s source impedance, so the same divider appears at every junction:

\[ A_{v,\text{total}} \;=\; \frac{\zin_1}{R_s+\zin_1}\;\left(\prod_{k=1}^{N} A_{v0,k}\right)\;\left(\prod_{k=1}^{N-1}\frac{\zin_{k+1}}{\zout_k+\zin_{k+1}}\right)\frac{R_L}{\zout_N+R_L} \] One input divider, \(N\) open-circuit gains, \(N-1\) inter-stage dividers, one output divider.
\[ \text{if every junction has the same } \varepsilon:\quad A_{v,\text{total}} = \left(\prod A_{v0,k}\right)(1-\varepsilon)^{N+1} \] Identical stages make the loss a power, not a sum.
\[ (1-\varepsilon)^{N+1} \approx 1 - (N{+}1)\varepsilon \quad \text{for small }\varepsilon \] First-order binomial expansion. Small losses do add, to first order — which is the useful mental model, provided \(\varepsilon\) really is small.

Three stages each losing \(9.1\%\) do not lose \(27\%\); they deliver \(0.909^{4} = 68\%\), and the linear estimate \(1-4(0.091)=64\%\) is already showing its error. The compounding is silent, because every stage measures correctly on its own.

4. Worked example: the gain of 10 that measures 9.09

A sensor with an open-circuit output of \(100\,\mathrm{mV}\) and a source resistance of \(R_s = 100\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) drives a non-inverting amplifier configured for a gain of \(10\), built on an LM358. You expect \(1.000\,\mathrm{V}\) and you measure \(0.909\,\mathrm{V}\). Nothing is broken.

\[ \zin \approx 1\,\mathrm{M\Omega} \quad\text{(LM358, bipolar input stage)} \] From the datasheet. A bipolar input draws base current, so its input resistance is megohms, not teraohms.
\[ v_{in} = 100\,\mathrm{mV}\cdot\frac{1\,\mathrm{M}}{100\,\mathrm{k}+1\,\mathrm{M}} = 100\,\mathrm{mV}\times 0.909 = 90.9\,\mathrm{mV} \]
\[ v_{out} = 10 \times 90.9\,\mathrm{mV} = 0.909\,\mathrm{V},\qquad \varepsilon = \frac{100\,\mathrm{k}}{1.1\,\mathrm{M}} = 9.1\% \] The amplifier's gain was exactly 10. The system gain was 9.09, and the missing 9.1% never entered the amplifier at all.

Swap to an OPA1656 (CMOS input, \(\zin \sim 10^{13}\,\Omega\)) and the same arithmetic gives \(\varepsilon \approx 10^{-8}\) — utterly negligible. The measurement reads \(1.000\,\mathrm{V}\).

The part number was the fix. No topology change and no extra components: the bipolar input of the LM358 presents megohms, the CMOS input of the OPA1656 presents teraohms. This is what “pick the right part” means in practice.

4. Real input impedances, by input stage

Input impedance is set by what the input transistors are, which is why it clusters by technology rather than varying smoothly:

PartInput stageZin (differential)IbiasWhere it hurts
LM358 / LM324Bipolar PNP~1–2 MΩ20–250 nAAny source above ~10 kΩ
TL072JFET~1012 Ω~30 pA (65 pA max)Bias current doubles every 10 °C
OPA1656CMOS~1013 Ω~2 pAEssentially nothing, at a price
LMP7721CMOS, guarded>1013 Ω3 fA typPCB leakage now dominates, not the part
Read that last row carefully. At 3 fA the amplifier has stopped being the limit — a fingerprint across the PCB conducts more than the input pin does. Choosing a better part past this point buys you nothing; the design problem has moved to guard rings and cleaning. Knowing when a spec stops mattering is as much of the skill as reading it.

5. What bites you

  • The 10× probe that changes the circuit. A 1× scope probe is 1 MΩ shunted by ~100 pF. On a high-impedance node that capacitance is the dominant load: at 100 kHz, 100 pF is 16 kΩ. The circuit that oscillates only when you probe it is not a mystery — you added the load that caused it.
  • DC input impedance is not the number you need above DC. Every datasheet's teraohms is a DC figure. In parallel sits Cin, a few pF, and that is what your source sees at frequency. A 1013 Ω input with 8 pF is 200 kΩ at 100 kHz.
  • Bias current through a large resistor is an offset. A TL072's 65 pA into 10 MΩ is 0.65 mV of DC error — larger than the op-amp's own offset voltage. This is why the DC-errors lesson treats Vos, Ib and Ios together: which one dominates depends entirely on your source impedance.
  • Cascading multiplies the losses. Three stages each losing 9% do not lose 27%, they deliver 0.9093 = 75% — and the error compounds silently because every stage looks fine on its own.

Problem Set 1 — loading and the four amplifier types

Everything here is answerable from this lesson alone. Levels 1–2 check that you have the mechanism; 3–4 ask you to invert it or apply it somewhere it was not derived; the challenge problems are the ones worth arguing about.

1.1 Level 1

A \(600\,\Omega\) microphone drives an amplifier with \(\zin = 10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\). What fraction of the signal is lost, in percent and in dB?

Solution

\[ \varepsilon = \frac{R_s}{R_s+\zin} = \frac{600}{10600} = 5.66\% \]

In dB, what matters is the surviving fraction: \[ 20\log_{10}(1-\varepsilon) = 20\log_{10}(0.9434) = -0.51\,\mathrm{dB} \]

Inaudible, and entirely normal — microphone inputs are deliberately built as bridging inputs at roughly \(10\times\) the source impedance. Note the approximation \(R_s/\zin = 6.0\%\) is close but not exact here, because \(\zin\) is only 17× \(R_s\).

1.2 Level 1

An amplifier has \(\zout = 50\,\Omega\) and an open-circuit gain of 20. What gain do you measure into an \(8\,\Omega\) loudspeaker?

Solution

\[ A_v = 20\cdot\frac{R_L}{\zout+R_L} = 20\cdot\frac{8}{58} = 2.76 \]

You lose \(86\%\) of the signal. The output divider is not a small correction here — \(\zout \gg R_L\) inverts the assumption the approximation was derived under, and the exact expression is the only one that applies. This is why a power amplifier's output stage exists at all: to get \(\zout\) far below the load.

1.3 Level 2

You need total system loss below \(0.05\%\) from a source of \(R_s = 47\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\), driving a load of \(R_L = 2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\). Specify the minimum \(\zin\) and the maximum \(\zout\), assuming you want the two error terms to contribute equally.

Solution

Equal contribution means each gets \(0.025\%\), since to first order the losses add:

\[ \frac{R_s}{\zin} \le 2.5\times10^{-4} \;\Rightarrow\; \zin \ge \frac{47\,\mathrm{k}}{2.5\times10^{-4}} = 188\,\mathrm{M\Omega} \]

\[ \frac{\zout}{R_L} \le 2.5\times10^{-4} \;\Rightarrow\; \zout \le 2\,\mathrm{k}\times2.5\times10^{-4} = 0.5\,\Omega \]

The \(\zin\) requirement demands a FET-input part; the \(\zout\) requirement of half an ohm is only reachable with feedback, which is the subject of stage 3 of the path. Note the asymmetry: high \(\zin\) is bought with a part number, low \(\zout\) is bought with loop gain.

1.4 Level 2

A photodiode is modelled as a current source \(i_p\) in parallel with a shunt resistance \(R_{sh} = 10^9\,\Omega\). It drives a transimpedance amplifier of input impedance \(\zin\). Derive the fraction of \(i_p\) that reaches the amplifier, and state the condition for \(\le 0.01\%\) loss.

Solution

Current divides in inverse proportion to impedance, so the amplifier gets

\[ \frac{i_{in}}{i_p} = \frac{R_{sh}}{R_{sh}+\zin},\qquad \varepsilon_i = \frac{\zin}{R_{sh}+\zin} \approx \frac{\zin}{R_{sh}} \]

which is the voltage result with the roles of the two impedances swapped — exactly as the four-row table predicts. For \(\varepsilon_i \le 10^{-4}\): \(\zin \le 10^{-4}\times10^{9} = 100\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\).

Worth noticing: \(100\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) is not a small impedance in absolute terms. “\(\zin \to 0\)” means small compared to the source, and nothing else.

1.5 Level 3

Four identical stages are cascaded. Each has \(\zin = 50\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\), \(\zout = 1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) and an open-circuit gain of 10. The source is \(R_s = 5\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) and the final load is \(R_L = 1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\). Compute the exact overall gain, and compare it with the naive \(10^4\).

Solution

Input divider: \(\dfrac{50}{55} = 0.909\). Three inter-stage dividers, each \(\dfrac{50}{51} = 0.980\). Output divider: \(\dfrac{1}{2} = 0.500\).

\[ A_v = 10^4 \times 0.909 \times 0.980^3 \times 0.500 = 10^4 \times 0.4278 = 4278 \]

You designed \(10\,000\) and built \(4278\) — a factor of 2.3 lost, and the output divider alone accounts for half of it. The inter-stage losses are almost irrelevant at \(2\%\) each; the error budget is dominated by the one junction where \(\zout\) is comparable to what it drives. Fixing the wrong term is the classic waste of effort here.

1.6 Level 3

An amplifier's datasheet quotes \(\zin = 10^{13}\,\Omega\) in parallel with \(C_{in} = 8\,\mathrm{pF}\). Your source is \(R_s = 1\,\mathrm{M\Omega}\). Above what frequency does the loss exceed \(1\%\)?

Solution

The resistive term is negligible \((10^{-7})\), so the capacitance sets it. Loss exceeds \(1\%\) when the capacitive reactance falls to roughly \(100\,R_s\):

\[ |Z_C| = \frac{1}{2\pi f C_{in}} = 100\,R_s \;\Rightarrow\; f = \frac{1}{2\pi C_{in}\cdot 100R_s} \]

\[ f = \frac{1}{2\pi (8\times10^{-12})(10^{8})} = 199\,\mathrm{Hz} \]

Two hundred hertz. The teraohms on the datasheet bought you almost nothing above DC, because a few picofarads across a megohm source is a pole in the audio band. Every input-impedance figure on a datasheet is a DC figure, and this is the one that actually constrains you.

1.7 Level 4

Show that for a source of fixed \(R_s\) driving a load \(R_L\) directly, maximum power transfer occurs at \(R_L = R_s\), and explain why signal amplifiers nonetheless use \(\zin \gg R_s\).

Solution

\[ P_L = i^2 R_L = \left(\frac{v_s}{R_s+R_L}\right)^2 R_L \]

\[ \frac{dP_L}{dR_L} = v_s^2\,\frac{(R_s+R_L)^2 - R_L\cdot 2(R_s+R_L)}{(R_s+R_L)^4} = v_s^2\,\frac{R_s - R_L}{(R_s+R_L)^3} \]

which vanishes at \(R_L = R_s\), and the second derivative is negative there, so it is a maximum. At that point the voltage delivered is exactly \(v_s/2\) — half the signal is thrown away, a loss of 6 dB, and half the power is burned in the source.

An amplifier's job is to transfer information, not power, and it can supply its own energy from the rails. Deliberately discarding 6 dB of signal-to-noise ratio to satisfy a power criterion that does not apply is a genuinely bad trade. Matching belongs where reflections matter — transmission lines, RF, anything where the interconnect is a non-negligible fraction of a wavelength.

1.8 Challenge

You must transmit a signal from a \(1\,\mathrm{M\Omega}\) source down 2 m of coaxial cable (\(100\,\mathrm{pF/m}\)) to an amplifier with \(\zin = 10^{12}\,\Omega \parallel 5\,\mathrm{pF}\). The signal is 10 kHz. Quantify the problem, then propose a fix that does not change either the source or the amplifier — and say what it costs.

Solution

Total capacitance: \(2\,\mathrm{m}\times100\,\mathrm{pF/m} + 5\,\mathrm{pF} = 205\,\mathrm{pF}\).

\[ |Z_C| = \frac{1}{2\pi(10^4)(205\times10^{-12})} = 77.6\,\mathrm{k\Omega} \]

Against a \(1\,\mathrm{M\Omega}\) source that is a divider of

\[ \frac{|Z_C|}{|R_s + Z_C|} \approx \frac{77.6\,\mathrm{k}}{1.003\,\mathrm{M}} = 7.7\% \]

— you lose \(92\%\) of the signal, and with a frequency-dependent factor, so the waveform is distorted as well as attenuated. The \(-3\,\)dB point is at \(f = 1/(2\pi R_s C) = 777\,\mathrm{Hz}\).

The fix: move a buffer to the source end. A unity-gain follower placed at the sensor presents \(\zin\) to the source and a low \(\zout\) to the cable, so the cable capacitance is driven by an impedance of ohms instead of a megohm. Neither the source nor the far amplifier changes.

What it costs: a part, power at the sensor end, and the buffer's own noise and offset now sit in front of everything. You have also moved an active device into whatever environment the sensor lives in — which may be hot, wet, or radioactive. The alternative, driven guarding (driving the cable shield from the buffer output so the inner conductor sees almost no potential difference across the capacitance), removes even the residual and is standard practice in electrometry.

Lab 1 — measure an input impedance you were told

Time 40 min Prerequisite this lesson Skill the substitution method

A datasheet number is a claim. This measures it, using a method that needs no instrument more accurate than the thing you already have — and which is the standard way to measure an impedance you cannot probe directly.

The idea. Put a known resistor \(R_{test}\) in series with the input and adjust it until the output falls to exactly half. At that point the divider is delivering \(50\%\), which by the loading theorem means

\[ \frac{\zin}{R_{test}+\zin} = \frac{1}{2} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\;R_{test} = \zin\;} \]

You have measured an impedance by matching it against one you know, and the amplifier's own gain cancels out entirely — which is why the method works with a scope you have not calibrated.

Equipment signal generator, oscilloscope, decade resistance box (or a handful of resistors), the amplifier under test
  1. Configure the amplifier for a gain of about 10, non-inverting. Drive it directly from the generator at 1 kHz, 100 mV pk-pk. Record the output amplitude \(V_{ref}\). This is your 100% reference.
  2. Insert \(R_{test}\) in series with the input, between the generator and the amplifier. Start at \(10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\).
  3. Increase \(R_{test}\) until the output reads exactly \(V_{ref}/2\). Record that resistance — it equals \(\zin\).
  4. Repeat at 10 kHz and 100 kHz. The value will fall. It is falling because \(C_{in}\) is in parallel with \(\zin\), and you are now measuring the parallel combination.
  5. From the 100 kHz result, back out \(C_{in}\): \(|Z| = 1/(2\pi f C_{in})\) once the capacitive term dominates.

What you should find. On a TL072 at 1 kHz you will run out of decade box long before the output halves — the JFET input is \(\sim10^{12}\,\Omega\) and no practical resistor competes with it. That failure is the result: it proves \(\zin\) is enormous, and it tells you the method has a ceiling set by your largest resistor. At 100 kHz you will succeed easily, because by then you are measuring \(C_{in}\), not \(\zin\), and a few picofarads is only tens of kilohms.

The trap. Your scope probe is \(\sim10\,\mathrm{M\Omega}\) shunted by \(\sim100\,\mathrm{pF}\). Probe the amplifier's input node and you have added a load far heavier than the one you are trying to measure, and the number you get is your probe. Probe the output only. Being unable to observe a node without changing it is the central difficulty of high-impedance measurement, and meeting it here is the point of the lab.
