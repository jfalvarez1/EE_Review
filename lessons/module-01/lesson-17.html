Interview Prep — Op-Amps

Everything in this module, in the form it actually gets asked. Nobody will ask you to recite the golden rules. They will put a circuit on a whiteboard, hand you a marker, and watch how you think while you take it apart.

What is actually being graded. Not the answer. The answer is usually two lines of algebra and they already know it. What they are watching is whether you start from something true or start from something memorised, whether you say what you are assuming, whether you check the result against a sanity case, and what you do when the circuit is not one of the four you learned. A candidate who derives \(-R_f/R_1\) from the golden rules in thirty seconds is worth more than one who says it in two, because only one of them can still answer when the resistor moves.

The method that works on every one of these

  1. Say the assumption out loud. "Negative feedback, op-amp in its linear region, so I can use \(V_+ = V_-\) and no input current." If either of those is false — and in about a third of interview circuits one of them is — you have just told them you noticed.
  2. Label every node. Put a name on each one before writing any equation. Most wrong answers are bookkeeping failures, not physics failures.
  3. Write KCL at the summing junction. Almost every op-amp result in this module is one node equation.
  4. Sanity-check with a special case. Set a resistor to zero or infinity and ask whether the answer degenerates to something you already know. Do this out loud; it is the single thing that most distinguishes people who have built circuits from people who have read about them.

Part 1 — Derive it, no formula sheet

Whiteboard questions. Work from \(V_+ = V_-\) and \(I_{in} = 0\) every time; do not reach for a remembered result.

1 Derive Warm-up

Derive the gain of an inverting amplifier. Then tell me why the minus input is at zero volts when nothing connects it to ground.

How to answer
\[ V_- = V_+ = 0 \] Negative feedback drives the output until the inputs match. \(V_+\) is grounded, so \(V_-\) is held at zero.
\[ I_1 = \frac{V_{in} - 0}{R_1}, \qquad I_f = \frac{0 - V_{out}}{R_f} \]
\[ I_1 = I_f \quad\text{(the input draws none)} \] This is the whole derivation. One node, one equation.
\[ \frac{V_{in}}{R_1} = -\frac{V_{out}}{R_f} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = -\frac{R_f}{R_1}} \]

"The minus input is a virtual ground. It is not connected to ground — the output moves to whatever voltage is needed to hold it there. Break the feedback loop and it stops being zero immediately, which is why it fails the moment the output saturates."

What they are testing: whether "virtual ground" is a phrase you repeat or a mechanism you understand. The follow-up is almost always "what happens to it when the output hits the rail?" — and the answer is that the loop is broken, the golden rules no longer apply, and the minus input goes wherever the resistors put it.
2 Derive Warm-up

Non-inverting amplifier. Derive the gain, then explain why it cannot be less than one.

How to answer
\[ V_- = V_+ = V_{in} \]
\[ V_- = V_{out}\cdot\frac{R_1}{R_1 + R_f} \] The divider from output to ground, unloaded because the input draws nothing.
\[ V_{in} = V_{out}\frac{R_1}{R_1+R_f} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1}} \]

"The feedback network is a divider, and the gain is its reciprocal. A passive divider can only attenuate, so its reciprocal can only be \(\geq 1\). To get below unity you have to attenuate at the input first, or use the inverting topology."

What they are testing: whether you see closed-loop gain as \(1/\beta\) rather than as a formula. That reframing is what makes the stability material later make sense, and an interviewer asking this is usually setting up a question about noise gain.
3 Derive Standard

Difference amplifier: \(V_1\) through \(R_1\) to the minus input, \(R_f\) from output to minus, \(V_2\) through \(R_2\) to the plus input, \(R_g\) from plus to ground. Derive \(V_{out}\). Then: what sets the CMRR?

How to answer

Superposition is faster than a single node equation here, and saying so is part of the answer.

\[ V_{out}\big|_{V_2=0} = -\frac{R_f}{R_1}V_1 \] Plus input grounded through \(R_2\|R_g\); no current flows in it, so it is at zero and this is a plain inverting amp.
\[ V_+ = V_2\frac{R_g}{R_2+R_g}, \qquad V_{out}\big|_{V_1=0} = V_+\left(1+\frac{R_f}{R_1}\right) \]
\[ V_{out} = V_2\frac{R_g}{R_2+R_g}\left(1+\frac{R_f}{R_1}\right) - \frac{R_f}{R_1}V_1 \]
\[ \text{If } \frac{R_g}{R_2} = \frac{R_f}{R_1}: \quad \boxed{V_{out} = \frac{R_f}{R_1}(V_2 - V_1)} \] Only when the ratios match does the common-mode term vanish. That matching condition IS the CMRR.

"CMRR here is set by resistor matching, not by the op-amp. With 1% resistors the worst-case ratio error is about 2%, which caps CMRR near \(1/0.02 = 50\), about 34 dB — nowhere near the op-amp's 100 dB. That is the entire reason instrumentation amplifiers exist: the matching is done on-die and laser trimmed."

What they are testing: the follow-up is the real question. Anyone can turn the crank; the point is whether you know that a difference amp built from discrete 1% resistors has terrible CMRR and why you would reach for an INA instead. If you volunteer the 34 dB number before being asked, the interview usually moves on to something harder.
4 Derive Standard

Derive the closed-loop gain of an inverting amplifier without assuming infinite open-loop gain. Then tell me the gain error for \(R_f/R_1 = 100\) and \(A = 10^5\).

How to answer
\[ V_{out} = -A\,V_-, \qquad\text{so}\qquad V_- = -\frac{V_{out}}{A} \] No longer zero. Everything else follows from carrying that small term.
\[ \frac{V_{in}-V_-}{R_1} = \frac{V_- - V_{out}}{R_f} \]
\[ \frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = \frac{-R_f/R_1}{1 + \dfrac{1 + R_f/R_1}{A}} \] The ideal answer times a correction factor. The term that matters is \((1+R_f/R_1)/A\) — note that the numerator is the NOISE gain, not the signal gain.
\[ \text{error} \approx \frac{1+R_f/R_1}{A} = \frac{101}{10^5} = 1.01\times10^{-3} = 0.1\% \]

"Gain error is noise gain divided by open-loop gain. At DC with \(10^5\) of loop gain that is a tenth of a percent — but \(A\) falls at 20 dB/decade, so at 10 kHz on a 1 MHz part \(A\) is only 100 and the same circuit has a gain error of 100%. The DC number is almost never the one that bites you."

What they are testing: whether you know that the correction involves the noise gain \(1+R_f/R_1\) and not \(R_f/R_1\). It is a small thing that separates people who derived it from people who half-remember it. The frequency follow-up is where the question is going.
5 Derive Standard

Inverting amplifier with a capacitor \(C_f\) across \(R_f\). Derive the transfer function. Where is the pole, and what is this circuit for?

How to answer
\[ Z_f = R_f \,\|\, \frac{1}{sC_f} = \frac{R_f}{1 + sR_fC_f} \] Do the whole problem in impedances and the algebra is the same as the resistive case.
\[ \frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = -\frac{Z_f}{R_1} = -\frac{R_f/R_1}{1 + sR_fC_f} \]
\[ f_p = \frac{1}{2\pi R_fC_f} \]

"DC gain \(-R_f/R_1\), rolling off at one pole. Two reasons to build it: it is a single-pole low-pass that costs one capacitor, and it is how you stabilise a transimpedance stage — \(C_f\) cancels the phase lag that the photodiode's capacitance and \(R_f\) create at the summing junction."

What they are testing: whether you reflexively generalise \(R\to Z\). Everyone who does this comfortably can also do the integrator, the differentiator and every filter in module 21 without learning anything new, and interviewers know it.
6 Derive Harder

Derive the input impedance seen by the source for (a) the inverting configuration and (b) the non-inverting configuration. Why are they so different?

How to answer
\[ \text{(a)}\quad Z_{in} = \frac{V_{in}}{I_{in}} = \frac{V_{in}}{(V_{in}-0)/R_1} = R_1 \] The source drives into a virtual ground, so it sees \(R_1\) and nothing else. The op-amp's own input impedance is irrelevant — it is on the far side of a node held at zero.
\[ \text{(b)}\quad Z_{in} = Z_{in,\,\text{ol}}\,(1 + A\beta) \] The source drives the op-amp input directly, and series-shunt feedback multiplies the open-loop input impedance by the loop gain. A 1 MΩ input with 80 dB of loop gain looks like 10 GΩ — in practice bounded by the input bias current and the ESD structures, not by this formula.

"That difference is usually the deciding factor. If the source has any output impedance of its own, the inverting configuration divides against \(R_1\) and changes your gain; the non-inverting one does not load it at all. Sensor front ends are non-inverting for exactly this reason."

What they are testing: whether you can choose a topology for a reason. "Which configuration would you use for a high-impedance sensor?" is the same question asked from the other end, and it comes up constantly.
7 Derive Harder

Define noise gain. Derive it for an inverting amplifier of signal gain \(-10\), and explain why stability depends on it rather than on the signal gain.

How to answer
\[ \text{Noise gain} = \frac{1}{\beta} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1} \] The gain the circuit applies to anything appearing at the op-amp's own input — its offset, its noise. It does not care which end of \(R_1\) the signal came in.
\[ \text{signal gain} = -10 \;\Rightarrow\; R_f/R_1 = 10 \;\Rightarrow\; \text{noise gain} = 11 \]

"Loop gain is \(A\beta\), and \(\beta\) is set by the feedback network alone. The signal path does not appear in the loop at all, so stability, bandwidth and offset all follow the noise gain. That is why an inverting amp of gain \(-1\) has a noise gain of 2 and behaves like a gain-of-2 stage for bandwidth purposes, and why a unity-gain follower — noise gain 1, the worst case for \(\beta\) — is the hardest configuration to keep stable, not the easiest."

What they are testing: this is the single most reliable separator in an op-amp interview. The claim that a follower is the hardest case to stabilise sounds backwards to anyone who has only used the formulas, and obvious to anyone who has thought in terms of \(\beta\). If you get one concept from this module, get this one.
8 Derive Harder

An op-amp has GBW = 10 MHz. Derive the closed-loop bandwidth at a signal gain of \(-9\). Then: the same part is asked to produce a 20 V peak-to-peak sine at 100 kHz and its slew rate is 5 V/µs. Does it?

How to answer
\[ f_{-3\mathrm{dB}} = \frac{\text{GBW}}{\text{noise gain}} = \frac{10\,\mathrm{MHz}}{1+9} = 1\,\mathrm{MHz} \] Noise gain again, not the 9.
\[ \text{required } \frac{dV}{dt}\Big|_{max} = 2\pi f V_{pk} = 2\pi(10^5)(10) = 6.28\,\mathrm{V/\mu s} \]
\[ 6.28 > 5 \;\Longrightarrow\; \textbf{no} \]
\[ f_{FP} = \frac{SR}{2\pi V_{pk}} = \frac{5\times10^6}{2\pi\cdot 10} = 79.6\,\mathrm{kHz} \] Full-power bandwidth. Below this the part is linear; above it the output is a triangle wave whatever the small-signal bandwidth says.

"Small-signal bandwidth is 1 MHz and it is irrelevant here. Full-power bandwidth is 80 kHz, so at 100 kHz and full swing the part slews and the output distorts badly. Two separate limits, and the one that bites depends on amplitude — which is why a circuit can look perfect on a network analyser at 10 mV and fall apart with real signals."

What they are testing: whether you keep small-signal and large-signal limits separate. Candidates who conflate them will confidently say "10 MHz part, 100 kHz signal, no problem" and be wrong. Volunteering the \(f_{FP}\) formula unprompted is a strong signal.

Part 2 — What does this one do?

The circuits interviewers draw when they want to see you think rather than recall. None of these is one of the four standard configurations, and in several of them the golden rules do not apply at all — noticing that is the answer.

9 Trap Standard

An op-amp with \(R_1\) from \(V_{in}\) to the plus input, \(R_f\) from the output back to the plus input, and the minus input grounded. What is \(V_{out}\)?

How to answer

Positive feedback. The output is at a rail, and which rail depends on history, not on \(V_{in}\).

Do not write \(V_+ = V_-\). That identity is a consequence of negative feedback, and there is none here. Feedback to the plus input reinforces any deviation instead of correcting it, so the output runs away to whichever rail it started toward and stays there.

\[ V_+ = V_{in}\frac{R_f}{R_1+R_f} + V_{out}\frac{R_1}{R_1+R_f} \]
\[ V_{out} = +V_{sat} \text{ while } V_+ > 0; \quad \text{it flips when } V_{in} < -V_{sat}\frac{R_1}{R_f} \] Which makes this a Schmitt trigger, not a broken amplifier. The hysteresis width is \(2V_{sat}R_1/R_f\).

"That is positive feedback, so it is a comparator with hysteresis rather than an amplifier. It sits at a rail and snaps to the other one when the input crosses a threshold set by the divider. It is a Schmitt trigger — deliberately, if the designer meant it, and a latched-up mess if they did not."

What they are testing: whether you check for negative feedback before applying the golden rules. A large fraction of candidates write \(V_+ = V_-\) on autopilot and derive a gain for a circuit that has none. Say "first, is the feedback negative?" out loud on every circuit and you will never fall into this one.
10 Trace it Standard

Two op-amps. The first is an inverting stage with \(R_f = 100\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), \(R_1 = 10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). Its output feeds a second inverting stage with \(R_f = 4.7\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), \(R_1 = 47\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). Input is 50 mV DC. Output? Both run on ±15 V.

How to answer
\[ A_1 = -\frac{100k}{10k} = -10, \qquad V_{mid} = -10(0.05) = -0.5\,\mathrm{V} \]
\[ A_2 = -\frac{4.7k}{47k} = -0.1, \qquad V_{out} = -0.1(-0.5) = +50\,\mathrm{mV} \]
\[ A_{total} = (-10)(-0.1) = +1 \]

"Unity gain overall, non-inverting, built out of two inverting stages. Which raises the obvious question: why would anyone do this? Amplifying by ten and then attenuating by ten degrades the noise and the offset for nothing — the first stage's offset gets multiplied by 10 and then divided by 10, so it survives at full size, while a follower would have added almost none. Unless the intermediate node is being used for something, this is a worse buffer than a piece of wire."

What they are testing: the arithmetic is trivial and they know it. The question is whether you notice the circuit is pointless and say so. Interviewers use deliberately silly circuits to see whether you evaluate what you are handed or just compute with it.
11 Trap Harder

A voltage follower: output tied directly to the minus input, signal into the plus input. Now put a 10 nF capacitor from the output to ground. What happens, and why?

How to answer

It rings, and with enough capacitance it oscillates.

The op-amp has a non-zero open-loop output impedance \(R_o\) — tens of ohms typically. That \(R_o\) and the load capacitance form a pole inside the feedback loop:

\[ f_p = \frac{1}{2\pi R_o C_L} \]
\[ R_o = 50\,\Omega,\; C_L = 10\,\mathrm{nF} \;\Rightarrow\; f_p = 318\,\mathrm{kHz} \]
If that lands anywhere near the crossover frequency, it adds up to 90° of lag on top of the 90° the dominant pole already contributes. Phase margin goes to zero and the loop oscillates.

"And it is worst in the follower, because a follower has \(\beta = 1\) — the highest loop gain and therefore the highest crossover frequency of any configuration. The fixes are to put a small isolation resistor, 10 to 50 ohms, between the output and the capacitor and close the DC loop outside it, or to use a part specified as unconditionally stable into capacitive loads."

What they are testing: a real failure that ships. "My buffer oscillates when I connect a cable" is a support question that arrives constantly, and a cable is a capacitor. The answer they want ends with the isolation resistor, because that is what you actually do about it.
12 Trap Harder

A textbook integrator: \(R\) from \(V_{in}\) to the minus input, \(C\) from the minus input to the output, plus input grounded. You build it, apply zero volts in, and come back in a minute. What do you find?

How to answer

The output is sitting hard against a rail.

\[ \text{At DC: } Z_C = \frac{1}{sC}\Big|_{s\to 0} = \infty \] There is no DC feedback path at all, so the DC gain is the open-loop gain — a hundred thousand or more.
\[ V_{out}(t) = -\frac{1}{RC}\int V_{os}\,dt + \frac{I_B t}{C} \] The offset voltage and the input bias current are both integrated. Neither is zero, so the output ramps whatever the input does.
\[ V_{os} = 1\,\mathrm{mV},\; R = 10\mathrm{k},\; C = 100\mathrm{nF} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{10^{-3}}{10^{-3}} = 1\,\mathrm{V/s} \] Fifteen seconds to the rail from a one-millivolt offset.

"An ideal integrator has infinite DC gain, so any offset at all integrates to a rail. The standard fix is a large resistor across \(C\) to bound the DC gain at \(R_f/R\) — which turns it into a leaky integrator and puts a pole at \(1/2\pi R_fC\). You choose \(R_f\) so that pole sits well below the lowest frequency you actually want to integrate."

What they are testing: whether you have built one. Everyone can write \(V_{out} = -\frac{1}{RC}\int V_{in}\,dt\); the people who have used it know it does not work as drawn, and can name the resistor and say what it costs.
13 Trace it Harder

A summing junction with three inputs to the minus node: \(V_1\) through 10 kΩ, \(V_2\) through 20 kΩ, \(V_3\) through 100 kΩ. \(R_f = 100\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). Plus input grounded. Give \(V_{out}\), then tell me the noise gain.

How to answer
\[ V_{out} = -R_f\left(\frac{V_1}{R_1}+\frac{V_2}{R_2}+\frac{V_3}{R_3}\right) = -(10V_1 + 5V_2 + V_3) \] One KCL at the virtual ground. The inputs do not interact, because each one drives into a node held at zero — which is the whole reason the summing junction is useful.
\[ R_{eq} = 10k \,\|\, 20k \,\|\, 100k = 6.25\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \]
\[ \text{noise gain} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_{eq}} = 1 + \frac{100k}{6.25k} = 17 \] The PARALLEL combination of all input resistors, not any single one.

"Signal gains of 10, 5 and 1, but a noise gain of 17 — higher than any of them. So the bandwidth is GBW/17 and the offset is multiplied by 17. Adding more inputs to a summing junction quietly costs you bandwidth even if each new input is attenuated."

What they are testing: the parallel combination. It is the most commonly missed step in the whole module, and it is the reason a summing amplifier can be unstable or slow when every individual gain looks modest.
14 Trace it Nasty

An op-amp running on a single +5 V supply. Plus input tied to a 2.5 V reference. \(R_1 = 10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) from a 0–5 V input to the minus input, \(R_f = 10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) from minus to output. Sketch \(V_{out}\) as the input sweeps 0 to 5 V.

How to answer

This is the standard level-shifting inverter, and the interesting part is what it does at the ends.

\[ V_- = V_+ = 2.5\,\mathrm{V} \]
\[ \frac{V_{in}-2.5}{R_1} = \frac{2.5 - V_{out}}{R_f} \;\Rightarrow\; V_{out} = 5 - V_{in} \] With equal resistors it is an inversion about the 2.5 V reference.
\[ V_{in}: 0 \to 5\,\mathrm{V} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad V_{out}: 5 \to 0\,\mathrm{V} \]

"Ideally a clean inversion across the full rail. In practice it depends entirely on whether the part is rail-to-rail on the output — an ordinary op-amp on a single 5 V supply might only reach 1.2 V to 3.8 V, so both ends of the sweep clip flat. And the input common-mode range matters too, though here the inputs sit at 2.5 V and never move, which is the nice property of the inverting configuration on a single supply."

What they are testing: single-supply thinking. The algebra is easy; the point is whether you remember that "rail to rail" is a specification a part either has or does not, and that the inverting configuration pins the common-mode voltage where you put the reference — which is why it is preferred on a single supply.
15 Trap Nasty

A follower buffering a 10 MΩ source. The op-amp is a general-purpose bipolar part with \(I_B = 80\,\mathrm{nA}\). The measured output is 0.8 V off. Where did that come from, and what do you change?

How to answer
\[ V_{err} = I_B R_S = (80\times10^{-9})(10\times10^{6}) = 0.8\,\mathrm{V} \] The bias current has to come from somewhere, and the only path is through the source resistance.

"Input bias current through the source impedance. Three ways out, in order of how much I would like them: use a FET-input part where \(I_B\) is picodamps instead of nanoamps, which turns 0.8 V into microvolts; or match the impedance seen by the two inputs so the bias currents cancel and only the offset current \(I_{os}\) remains, which is maybe a tenth of \(I_B\); or lower the source impedance, if that is in your gift, which it usually is not."

What they are testing: whether you can spot an error mechanism from a number. 0.8 V is enormous and obviously not an offset-voltage problem, so the answer has to be a current times a resistance. This exact question — high impedance source, bipolar op-amp — is one of the most frequently asked in analog interviews, because getting it wrong in a real design is expensive and common.
16 Trap Nasty

Same follower, but now the input goes 0.5 V below the negative rail for a few milliseconds during power-up. What can happen, and how do you prevent it?

How to answer

Two distinct hazards, and a good answer separates them.

Phase inversion. On many older bipolar parts, driving an input beyond the common-mode range causes the output to flip to the opposite rail rather than saturating in the expected direction. Inside a servo loop that inverts the sign of the feedback and the loop latches. Newer parts advertise "no phase inversion" precisely because this bit so many people.

Input protection diodes. Every op-amp has ESD diodes from each input to each rail. Below the negative rail one of them conducts and the input pin becomes a low-impedance path to the supply. If the source can deliver current, you get latch-up or a destroyed input stage.

"I would put a series resistor in the input — a few kilohms — to limit the diode current to under the datasheet's input current limit, typically 10 mA. Then check the offset that resistor adds through \(I_B\), because on a bipolar part that trade is real. If the overdrive is large or sustained, external Schottky clamps to the rails carry the current instead of the internal diodes."

What they are testing: whether you think about what happens outside the operating region. A candidate who only knows the linear behaviour will say "the output saturates" and stop. Power-up, fault and out-of-range behaviour is where real designs fail, and interviewers for anything safety- or automotive-adjacent ask it deliberately.

Part 3 — Rapid fire

Short answers, thirty seconds each. These are filters: they establish whether the conversation continues at this level or drops down one.

17 Rapid

What is the gain of a voltage follower, and what is it for?

Answer

Unity. It is for impedance transformation, not gain: high input impedance, low output impedance, so a weak source can drive a heavy load without being loaded down. Saying "gain of 1" alone is not an answer to the second half.

18 Rapid

Op-amp with GBW 1 MHz. What bandwidth do you get at a gain of 100?

Answer

10 kHz. And if it is an inverting gain of 100 the noise gain is 101, so 9.9 kHz — mention it, then say the difference does not matter here.

19 Rapid

Why does a bipolar-input op-amp have so much more input bias current than a FET-input one?

Answer

A bipolar input stage is a pair of BJTs, and a BJT base requires a base current to work — \(I_B = I_C/\beta\). A MOSFET or JFET gate is an insulator or a reverse-biased junction, so what you measure is leakage, picoamps at room temperature. The trade is noise and offset: bipolar inputs are quieter and better matched.

20 Rapid

What does PSRR tell you, and why does the number on the front page of the datasheet mislead people?

Answer

How much of a supply variation appears at the output, referred to the input. The headline figure is at DC or 1 kHz, and PSRR falls with frequency — often to 20 or 30 dB by a few hundred kilohertz. So an op-amp fed from a switching regulator rejects the ripple far worse than the datasheet's first page suggests, and you have to read the PSRR-versus-frequency curve at your switching frequency.

21 Rapid

Where does the current that drives the load come from?

Answer

The power supply pins, not the input signal. It is worth saying explicitly, because it is the thing beginners have backwards: the op-amp modulates current drawn from the rails; the input just tells it how much.

22 Rapid

You need to amplify a 10 µV thermocouple signal. What specification do you look at first?

Answer

Offset voltage and its drift over temperature — because a typical op-amp's \(V_{os}\) of 1 mV is a hundred times your signal, and drift is what you cannot calibrate out. Then \(1/f\) noise, since a thermocouple signal is near DC and that is exactly where flicker noise lives. That combination is what chopper and auto-zero amplifiers exist to solve.

If you remember six things

Before anything else
Check the feedback is negative. Only then are \(V_+ = V_-\) and \(I_{in}=0\) available to you.
Noise gain, not signal gain
\(1+R_f/R_1\) sets bandwidth, offset multiplication and stability. Signal gain sets none of them.
Two bandwidth limits
GBW/noise gain for small signals, \(SR/2\pi V_{pk}\) for large ones. Whichever is lower wins.
Errors are currents times resistances
A large DC error with a high-impedance source is \(I_B R_S\) until proven otherwise.
Matching sets CMRR
A discrete difference amp is limited by its resistors, not its op-amp. 1% gives about 34 dB.
Say your assumptions
And sanity-check the answer with a degenerate case. Both are graded, both are free.
One habit worth building before an interview. Take any circuit in this module, cover the result, and derive it out loud from KCL at the summing junction. Not on paper — out loud, standing up, the way you will have to do it. The gap between "I know this" and "I can say this while someone watches" is the entire difficulty of a technical interview, and it closes with about an hour of practice.
