Industry Op-Amp Selection Guide

With thousands of op-amp options available, selecting the right one for your application requires systematic analysis of requirements, trade-offs, and practical considerations. This lesson provides a methodology-based framework for professional op-amp selection that will remain valid regardless of which specific parts are available in the market.

Note: Specific part numbers in this lesson serve as examples of each category. When designing, always search for current parts that meet your specifications—the methodology matters more than any particular IC.

The 5-Step Selection Methodology

Follow this process for any op-amp selection, regardless of application:

  1. Define Critical Requirements — What performance is non-negotiable?
  2. Identify Trade-offs — What can you sacrifice to get what you need?
  3. Choose Input Technology — Bipolar, JFET, or CMOS?
  4. Verify Secondary Specs — Power, package, temperature, stability
  5. Evaluate Availability — Multi-source, lifecycle status, lead time

Step 2: Understanding Why Parameters Matter

Before looking at numbers, understand why each parameter is critical for specific applications:

DC Accuracy Parameters — Critical for Precision Measurement

Parameter General Purpose Precision Ultra-Precision Why It Matters
VOS (max) 3-10 mV 25-250 µV 1-25 µV Direct DC error: Appears at output multiplied by closed-loop gain. A 1mV offset with gain=100 creates 100mV output error.
Drift (max) 5-20 µV/°C 0.5-2 µV/°C 0.02-0.1 µV/°C Temperature stability: In field equipment with 50°C swings, 10µV/°C drift creates 500µV error that can't be calibrated out.
IB 10-500 nA 1-10 nA 1-100 pA Source loading: Bias current × source impedance = voltage error. High-Z sensors (pH, photodiode) need pA-level IB.
Open-loop gain 80-100 dB 110-130 dB 130-140 dB Gain accuracy: the fractional gain error is 1/(1 + AOLβ) ≈ ACL/AOL, since 1/β is the closed-loop gain. A part with AOL = 106 set to a gain of 100 is 0.01% low before anything else has gone wrong — and AOL is the spec that falls fastest with frequency, so that error grows at 20 dB/decade.

AC Performance Parameters — Critical for Speed and Bandwidth

Parameter Low Speed General Purpose High Speed Why It Matters
GBW 0.5-3 MHz 3-30 MHz 100 MHz - 4 GHz Available bandwidth: GBW/ACL = your -3dB bandwidth. A 10 MHz GBW at gain=100 gives only 100 kHz bandwidth.
Slew Rate 0.3-3 V/µs 5-50 V/µs 100-5000 V/µs Large-signal response: Required SR = 2π × f × Vpk. 10Vpk at 1MHz needs 63 V/µs minimum.
Settling (0.01%) 10-100 µs 1-10 µs 10-100 ns ADC interface: SAR ADCs sample immediately after MUX switching. Slow settling causes conversion errors.

The Fundamental Trade-offs

  • Low noise vs. Low power: Noise power ∝ 1/IQ. Cutting quiescent current raises noise floor.
  • Speed vs. Precision: High-speed processes sacrifice DC matching. Chopper/auto-zero adds precision but limits bandwidth.
  • Low IB vs. Low noise: CMOS inputs have pA bias but higher voltage noise than bipolar.
  • Rail-to-rail vs. Performance: R-R output stages have crossover distortion; R-R inputs may have offset shifts at mid-supply.

Step 3: Input Technology Selection

The input stage technology fundamentally determines what an op-amp can and cannot do:

Technology IB Range Voltage Noise Best For Avoid For
Bipolar 10 nA - 10 µA 0.8 - 3 nV/√Hz Lowest voltage noise; audio, low-Z sensors, current sensing High-Z sources (photodiodes, pH electrodes, piezo)
JFET 1 pA - 100 pA 5 - 20 nV/√Hz High-Z sources; balanced noise and bias; legacy designs Ultra-low voltage noise; rail-to-rail operation
CMOS 0.1 pA - 10 pA 5 - 50 nV/√Hz Lowest bias current; single supply; rail-to-rail I/O Ultra-low noise (below ~3 nV/√Hz)
Zero-Drift/Chopper 10 pA - 1 nA 5 - 30 nV/√Hz Near-DC precision; strain gauges, RTDs, thermocouples Wideband signals; chopper artifacts in-band

Noise Considerations by Source Impedance

Total input noise depends on BOTH voltage noise (en) and current noise (in):

Total Noise = √(en² + (in × Rsource)² + 4kTRsource)

  • Low Rsource (<1kΩ): Voltage noise dominates → Use bipolar
  • Medium Rsource (1k-100kΩ): Both contribute → JFET or low-noise CMOS
  • High Rsource (>100kΩ): Current noise dominates → Use CMOS

Step 5: Sourcing and Lifecycle Strategy

Good engineering means planning for component availability over product lifetime:

Finding Second Sources

  1. Search by specification, not part number: Use parametric search on DigiKey, Mouser, or Octopart
  2. Check pin compatibility: Same pinout enables drop-in replacement
  3. Compare critical specs: Second source must meet YOUR requirements, not just be "similar"
  4. Verify package availability: Ensure the alternate is available in your footprint

Example: Finding Alternatives for a Precision Op-Amp

If your design needs: VOS <50µV, drift <1µV/°C, GBW >1MHz, SOIC-8 package

Search parameters: "Precision op-amp, Vos max 50uV, SOIC-8, in stock"

You'll find multiple manufacturers with equivalent parts—any of them work!

Lifecycle Awareness

Always check manufacturer lifecycle status before design-in. Avoid parts marked:

  • NRND: Not Recommended for New Designs
  • LTB: Last Time Buy announced
  • Obsolete: No longer manufactured

Pro tip: Major manufacturers (TI, Analog Devices, Microchip, STMicro) typically support parts for 10-15+ years. Verify longevity for industrial/automotive applications.

Worked Examples (Razavi-Style Analysis)

These problems follow the systematic approach taught in Razavi's Design of Analog CMOS Integrated Circuits, breaking down complex selections into fundamental trade-off analysis.

Example 1: Strain Gauge Amplifier Selection

Problem: Design an instrumentation amplifier for a 350Ω strain gauge bridge with 2mV/V sensitivity. The system operates from -20°C to +70°C and requires 0.1% accuracy at full scale (10mV signal).

Step 1: Define the Error Budget

Full scale signal: 10mV. Required accuracy: 0.1% → Max total error: 10µV

Step 2: Analyze Offset Error

With gain = 100 (typical for strain gauge), input-referred offset error budget:

VOS,max = 10µV / 2 = 5µV (allocating half budget to offset)

Step 3: Analyze Drift Error

Temperature range: 90°C swing. Remaining budget: 5µV

Driftmax = 5µV / 90°C ≈ 0.055 µV/°C

Step 4: Determine Op-Amp Category

Requirements: VOS <5µV, drift <0.1µV/°C

Only zero-drift (chopper/auto-zero) amplifiers meet these specs.

Typical bipolar precision (OP07-class): VOS=30µV, drift=0.3µV/°C → Fails

Zero-drift CMOS: VOS<5µV, drift=0.02µV/°C → Passes

Step 5: Check Secondary Constraints
  • Bandwidth: Strain measurements typically <10kHz → Zero-drift GBW (1-10MHz) is adequate
  • Noise: Check chopper clock frequency doesn't interfere with signal band
  • Source impedance: 350Ω bridge + balancing resistors → ~1kΩ, CMOS IB is negligible

Selection Category: Zero-drift, chopper-stabilized, instrumentation amplifier topology. Search for current offerings with VOS<5µV, drift<0.05µV/°C.

Example 2: Photodiode Transimpedance Amplifier

Problem: Design a TIA for a PIN photodiode with 50pF junction capacitance, detecting 1nA to 1µA photocurrent at 100kHz bandwidth.

Step 1: Determine Feedback Resistor

For 1µA max current and 1V output swing:

RF = Vout / Imax = 1V / 1µA = 1MΩ

Step 2: Analyze Stability (Critical!)

The photodiode capacitance (CD=50pF) with RF=1MΩ forms a pole at:

fpole = 1/(2π × RF × CD) = 1/(2π × 1MΩ × 50pF) = 3.2kHz

This pole interacts with op-amp dynamics. Need GBW such that:

GBW > (CD/CF) × f-3dB

For 100kHz BW with CF=0.5pF compensation: GBW > 10MHz minimum

Step 3: Calculate Bias Current Error

Minimum signal: 1nA. For <1% error from bias current:

IB < 0.01 × 1nA = 10pA

This eliminates bipolar op-amps (typical IB = 10nA-1µA)

Step 4: Noise Analysis

At 1MΩ feedback, Johnson noise dominates at low frequencies:

en,R = √(4kTRF) = √(4 × 4×10-21 × 1MΩ) = 128 nV/√Hz

Op-amp voltage noise contribution: en × (1 + CD/CF) at high frequency

Choose op-amp with en < 10 nV/√Hz to keep noise below resistor limit

Step 5: Select Input Technology
  • IB < 10pA → Must be CMOS or JFET
  • GBW > 10MHz → Eliminates micro-power options
  • Stable with capacitive load → Check datasheet phase margin with CD

Selection Category: FET-input (JFET or CMOS), moderate GBW (10-200MHz), low input capacitance, stable with photodiode capacitance. Many manufacturers offer "TIA-optimized" parts.

Example 3: High-Speed SAR ADC Driver

Problem: Drive a 16-bit, 1 MSPS SAR ADC with 4Vpp input range. The ADC has 30pF internal sampling capacitor.

Step 1: Settling Requirement

For 16-bit accuracy, op-amp must settle to 1 LSB = 4V/65536 = 61µV

This is 0.0015% of full scale, requiring ~12τ settling time

Available settling time: ~500ns (acquisition window at 1 MSPS)

Step 2: Required Bandwidth

f-3dB > (12 × ln2) / (2π × 500ns) ≈ 2.7 MHz minimum

For margin and capacitive load driving: GBW > 50 MHz recommended

Step 3: Output Current Capability

Charging 30pF to 4V in 100ns (during acquisition):

Ipeak = C × dV/dt = 30pF × 4V / 100ns = 1.2mA minimum

For transient headroom: Iout > 20mA recommended

Step 4: Distortion Analysis

16-bit linearity requires THD < -98 dB. Check closed-loop distortion at signal frequency.

Higher loop gain (higher GBW at lower frequencies) improves distortion.

Step 5: DC Accuracy

For 16-bit: VOS < 61µV (1 LSB). Most high-speed amps have 1-5mV offset.

Solution: Either use precision high-speed amp OR rely on ADC calibration.

Selection Category: High-speed, rail-to-rail output (for maximum dynamic range), high output current, unity-gain stable (for buffer applications). Many ADC manufacturers provide companion amplifier recommendations.
