Power Supply Rejection Ratio (PSRR)

PSRR measures how well an op-amp rejects noise and ripple on its power supply rails. Understanding PSRR is critical for designing low-noise precision circuits, especially when powered from switching regulators or shared power buses.

What is PSRR?

PSRR quantifies the op-amp's ability to prevent power supply variations from appearing at the output.

PSRR = 20 × log10(ΔVsupply / ΔVOS)

ΔVOS is the shift in the amplifier's input offset for a change ΔVsupply on one rail. It is referred to the input because that is where it behaves like a signal: whatever the amplifier then does to its input, it does to this. Precision datasheets quote the same quantity in µV/V — the OPA2277's 0.3 µV/V is 20 log10(1 / 0.3×10−6) = 130 dB.

The measurement is a unity-gain follower with its input grounded and a small AC signal added to one rail. The follower reproduces its input offset at the output, so the output ripple is ΔVOS. An open-loop amplifier with both inputs grounded cannot be used for this: its output sits against a rail on its own offset.

PSRR test: follower, input grounded, Δv on V+. The output moves by Δv / 10PSRR/20.

A PSRR of 100 dB means a 1 V supply ripple shifts the input offset by 10 µV — 10 µV at the output of a follower, and 1 mV at the output of a gain-of-100 stage.

PSRR vs Frequency

PSRR degrades at higher frequencies. This is the most critical aspect often overlooked by designers.

The Switching Regulator Problem

Switching regulators operate at 100kHz-2MHz, where op-amp PSRR may be only 40-60dB. A regulator with 20mVpp ripple can inject 20–200 µV into a precision op-amp circuit—potentially more than the op-amp's VOS!

PSRR+ vs PSRR−

The two rails reach the input through different internal paths, so they are rejected differently — but the specification table almost always gives one number for both, and the split appears only in the PSRR-versus-frequency plot. In a single-supply circuit V− is ground, and “PSRR−” becomes the question of how clean the ground is: a layout problem, not a datasheet one.

Closed-Loop PSRR

The closed-loop PSRR is modified by the noise gain of the circuit:

PSRRCL = PSRROL − 20log10(Noise Gain)

Noise gain 1 + 9k/1k = 10, so the supply-induced offset reaches the output twenty decibels larger: PSRRCL is 20 dB below the datasheet figure.

Example

An op-amp with PSRR = 100dB at DC in a gain-of-100 circuit has closed-loop PSRR = 100 - 40 = 60dB. A 10mV supply ripple becomes 10µV at the output.

Improving PSRR

1. Add Post-Regulator Filtering

Attenuation = 1 / (1 + sRFCF). With 10 Ω and 100 µF the corner is 159 Hz and the ripple falls 20 dB per decade above it. The price is IQ × RF of headroom — 40 mV for a 4 mA amplifier — which is what stops RF being larger.

One switcher, two regulators, two returns joined at a single point. The digital load's current never flows through the analog regulator's ground reference.

Practical Example: Precision ADC Reference

Design goal: Drive an ADC reference buffer with <1µV supply-induced error at 500kHz switching frequency.

Analysis

  1. SMPS ripple: 20mVpp at 500kHz
  2. Required attenuation: 20mV → 1µV = 86dB
  3. Op-amp PSRR at 500kHz: ~50dB (typical precision op-amp)
  4. Noise gain = 1 (buffer), so no degradation
  5. Shortfall: 86 - 50 = 36dB additional filtering needed

Solution

  • R-C filter: 10Ω + 100µF ceramic (fc = 160Hz)
  • Attenuation at 500kHz: 20log(500k/160) ≈ 70dB
  • Total: 50dB (op-amp) + 70dB (filter) = 120dB
  • Result: 20mV × 10-6 = 20nV (well under 1µV)

That 70 dB is the ideal capacitor's figure. A real 100 µF ceramic carries about 2 nH of lead and body inductance, which resonates with it at 356 kHz; just above that the capacitor is an inductor. At 500 kHz the net reactance happens to still be small and the attenuation holds, but at 5 MHz the ideal model says 90 dB and the real part gives 44 dB — the build below lets you put the number on it.
