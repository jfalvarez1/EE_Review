Reading an Op-Amp Datasheet

The last four lessons named every way a real op-amp departs from the ideal one: finite bandwidth, slew rate, offset voltage, bias current, finite CMRR and PSRR, noise. Each was a symbol in an equation. This lesson finds them all on a real datasheet, where they are numbers with conditions attached, and turns them into a single figure: how wrong will my circuit be?

The one thing to take away, before anything else. The typical column is not a specification. It is a description of the middle of the distribution, it is not tested on every part, and it carries no guarantee of any kind. Design against it and roughly half your production is out of spec. Only min and max are promises.

Nearly every field failure that traces back to "the datasheet said" traces to that sentence, or to the one in section 2 about test conditions.

1. The three columns, and which of them are promises

ColumnWhat it meansCan you design to it?
MIN Guaranteed. Either tested on every part, or guaranteed by design and verified statistically on samples. Yes. This is the contract.
TYP The median of a characterisation lot, at 25 °C, often years ago on a process that has since moved. No. For estimating, comparing parts, and setting expectations only.
MAX Guaranteed, same as min. Yes.
A blank max is information

When a parameter has a typical value and no min or max, the manufacturer is telling you they will not stand behind it. That is not an oversight. Offset drift on a jellybean JFET part is the classic case: 18 µV/°C typical, no maximum — so a part that drifts 60 µV/°C is within specification, because there is no specification.

If a blank max sits on a parameter your design depends on, you have three options: pick a different part, test every unit yourself, or design so the parameter stops mattering. There is no fourth.

Guaranteed over what temperature?

Most tables give a 25 °C block and a second block for the full operating range. They are different numbers, and the second one is the one your product ships against. A part with \(V_{OS} = 60\,\mathrm{\mu V}\) max at 25 °C and a \(1.3\,\mathrm{\mu V/^\circ C}\) max drift is a \(\pm 120\,\mathrm{\mu V}\) part at −40 to +85 °C. Quoting the 60 in a design review is quoting the wrong number.

2. The test conditions are part of the number

Every specification line has conditions in small type beside it. Change any of them and the guarantee is void — not degraded, void.

SpecUsual conditionWhat changes it, and by how much
\(V_{OS}\) \(T_A = 25°C\), \(V_S = \pm15\,\mathrm{V}\), \(V_{CM} = 0\) Drift with temperature; a shift with common-mode set by CMRR; a shift with supply set by PSRR; and long-term drift, often \(1\,\mathrm{\mu V}/\sqrt{\text{month}}\).
\(I_B\) \(T_A = 25°C\) Doubles every 10 °C for JFET and CMOS inputs, because it is junction leakage. A 1 pA part is about 1 nA at 125 °C — a factor of a thousand. Bipolar inputs barely move.
CMRR, PSRR DC, or 120 Hz Both fall at 20 dB/decade above the amplifier's first pole. A part with 110 dB at DC may have 60 dB at 10 kHz, which is why supply noise appears in the audio band.
\(A_{OL}\) \(R_L = 10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), stated output swing Falls with load. At \(600\,\Omega\) expect 10–20 dB less, and with it your closed-loop gain accuracy.
Slew rate One amplitude, one gain, often unity Frequently asymmetric — rising and falling differ — and the number is usually the faster edge.
GBW One gain, one load capacitance Meaningless below a decompensated part's minimum stable gain, where the part simply oscillates.
Output swing A stated load "Rail-to-rail" means to within perhaps 50 mV at \(10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), and 300 mV at \(600\,\Omega\). It is a marketing word with a datasheet footnote.
Three phrases that are not the same thing. "Single-supply operation" means the part functions from one rail. "Input common-mode range includes ground" means the inputs work at 0 V. "Rail-to-rail output" means the output approaches the rails. A part can have any one without the others, and the classic bug is a single-supply design whose input sits at 0 V driving a part whose common-mode range starts at 1.5 V — which does not fail loudly, it just gives a wrong answer near zero.

Read down the offset column: four orders of magnitude, for perhaps two orders of magnitude of money. Read down the bias-current column and notice it moves the opposite way between the bipolar and FET rows — which is the whole reason the choice is not simply "buy the precision one". At a source impedance of \(1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\), the OP07's 4 nA of bias current makes 4 mV of error, and the TL072's 200 pA makes 0.2 mV. The cheap part wins by twenty times.

Zero-drift parts are not free lunches. They achieve microvolt offset by chopping, and the chopping puts switching residue at the clock frequency and its aliases into the output, and glitch charge into the source impedance. They also have higher current noise and often worse behaviour with large source impedances. They are the right answer for DC precision at moderate impedance, and the wrong answer for a high-impedance sensor or anything near the chopping frequency.

4. The error budget, assembled

Every specification above becomes one term. For a stage of noise gain \(G_n = 1 + R_f/R_g\) — the same number for inverting and non-inverting configurations, which is why it deserves its own name:

\[ e_{V_{OS}} = V_{OS}\cdot G_n \] Offset is an input-referred voltage, so it is amplified by the noise gain, not by the signal gain.
\[ e_{drift} = \left(\frac{dV_{OS}}{dT}\right)\Delta T\cdot G_n \]
\[ e_{bias} = \bigl|I_{B+}R_{S+} - I_{B-}R_{S-}\bigr|\cdot G_n \;\to\; I_{OS}R_S\,G_n \text{ if balanced} \] Balancing the two source resistances converts an \(I_B\) error into a much smaller \(I_{OS}\) error — the reason for the otherwise pointless-looking resistor in the non-inverting input of countless circuits.
\[ e_{CMRR} = \frac{V_{CM}}{10^{\mathrm{CMRR_{dB}}/20}}\cdot G_n, \qquad e_{PSRR} = \frac{\Delta V_S}{10^{\mathrm{PSRR_{dB}}/20}}\cdot G_n \]
\[ e_{gain} = V_{out}\cdot\frac{G_n}{A_{OL}} \] From \(1/(1+A\beta)\) with \(\beta = 1/G_n\). This is the same finite-loop-gain error derived in the feedback lesson, now with a number in it.
\[ e_{noise} = e_n\sqrt{1.57\,f_{-3dB}}\cdot G_n \quad(\text{RMS, broadband}) \] The 1.57 is \(\pi/2\), the noise bandwidth of a single-pole roll-off — noise keeps arriving past the corner.
Do not simply add them. Adding every maximum gives the worst case, which no real part will ever hit — it requires every error to be at its limit and in the same direction at once. Root-sum-square is the right combination for independent random terms, and it is typically 2–3× smaller. Use worst-case for a safety-critical single unit; use RSS for a production distribution. Say which one you used.

5. Where the real information is: the curves

The specification table gives you one temperature and one set of conditions. The typical performance characteristics section — the twenty pages of graphs most readers skip — gives you your conditions. Six curves are worth finding every time.

CurveWhat it tells you that the table cannot
\(V_{OS}\) vs common-mode voltage Whether the part has two input pairs. If the curve has a step in it, it does — see below.
\(I_B\) vs temperature The doubling-per-10 °C law made concrete. On a log axis it is a straight line, and you can read your own maximum off it.
\(A_{OL}\) vs frequency and load Your actual gain error at your actual load and signal frequency, not at DC into 10 kΩ.
CMRR / PSRR vs frequency How much of the 110 dB survives at the frequency of the noise you are trying to reject. Usually much less than you hoped.
Output swing vs load current What "rail-to-rail" costs at your load. It is a straight line whose slope is the output stage's resistance.
Overshoot vs capacitive load Where the part starts ringing. Anything past a few hundred pF usually needs an isolation resistor.
The rail-to-rail input trap, which the curve reveals

Many "rail-to-rail input" amplifiers contain two input differential pairs — one PNP-based for the lower half of the common-mode range, one NPN-based for the upper — and hand over somewhere in the middle. The two pairs have unrelated offset voltages, so \(V_{OS}\) steps discontinuously as the common mode crosses the handover point, typically by a millivolt or more.

In an inverting configuration the common mode never moves and this is harmless. In a non-inverting one it is a distortion mechanism: a signal that sweeps through the handover gets a step added to it, and no amount of loop gain removes it because it is an input-referred error. The fixes are to use the part inverting, to keep the common mode on one side of the handover, or to buy a part with a charge-pump or zero-drift input stage that has only one pair.

None of this appears in the table. It appears as a step in one graph.

6. Absolute maximum ratings are not operating conditions

The two tables sit next to each other and mean opposite things.

Absolute maximum ratingsRecommended operating conditions
The point at which damage begins. Nothing is guaranteed to work here — only to survive, briefly, one stress at a time. The range over which the specifications in the table apply.
Exceeding it, even momentarily, may destroy the part or shorten its life in ways that do not show up until the field. Operating outside it is not destructive, but every number in the datasheet becomes an unsupported guess.

Three that catch people:

  • Input voltage is usually specified relative to the supplies, as \(V_- - 0.3\,\mathrm{V}\) to \(V_+ + 0.3\,\mathrm{V}\). During power-up, if a signal source is already live and the amplifier's rails are not, the input is above \(V_+\) and the ESD diode conducts. This is the single most common way op-amps die, and it happens at switch-on, not in the lab.
  • Differential input voltage is often limited to a couple of volts by back-to-back protection diodes across the inputs. A comparator application, or any circuit that can saturate, will exceed it — and then the input pair draws current through those diodes, loading the source.
  • Output short-circuit duration is frequently "continuous" only with a stated derating. Continuous into a rail from a rail-to-rail output at 85 °C often is not.

Problem Set — reading the numbers

Every one uses the representative figures in section 3.

1Level 1

A TL07x-class part is used at a noise gain of 50. What is the worst-case output offset at 25 °C from \(V_{OS}\) alone, and what would you have predicted from the typical column?

Solution

\[ e = V_{OS,max}\cdot G_n = 10\,\mathrm{mV}\times 50 = \mathbf{500\,mV} \]

From typical: \(3\,\mathrm{mV}\times 50 = 150\,\mathrm{mV}\).

A factor of 3.3 between what your bench shows and what you may ship. On a 5 V supply, half a volt of output offset is ten percent of the range — this part simply cannot be used open-loop at a gain of 50 without trimming.

2Level 1

A JFET-input amplifier is specified at 200 pA of bias current at 25 °C. What should you expect at 105 °C?

Solution

Doubling every 10 °C over an 80 °C rise is eight doublings:

\[ I_B = 200\,\mathrm{pA}\times 2^{8} = 200\,\mathrm{pA}\times 256 = \mathbf{51\,nA} \]

The part that was chosen because of its picoamp input now has more bias current than the precision bipolar it was chosen over. Across a \(1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) source that is 51 mV of error.

3Level 2

A precision bipolar part drives a \(\times 100\) stage from a \(10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) source, over 25 to 70 °C. Find the offset, drift and bias contributions to output error using maximum figures, unbalanced inputs. Then say what single change helps most.

Solution

\[ e_{V_{OS}} = 75\,\mathrm{\mu V}\times 100 = 7.5\,\mathrm{mV} \]

\[ e_{drift} = 1.3\,\mathrm{\mu V/^\circ C}\times 45°\times 100 = 5.85\,\mathrm{mV} \]

\[ e_{bias} = 4\,\mathrm{nA}\times 10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\times 100 = 4.0\,\mathrm{mV} \]

Worst case sum: 17.4 mV. RSS: \(\sqrt{7.5^2+5.85^2+4.0^2} = 10.3\,\mathrm{mV}\).

The single most effective change is balancing the source resistances — adding \(10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) in the other input's path converts the \(I_B\) term into an \(I_{OS}\) term, \(0.8\,\mathrm{nA}\) instead of 4, dropping 4.0 mV to 0.8 mV. It costs one resistor and a little noise. Trimming the offset would remove more error but does not survive the drift, which is the second-largest term and cannot be trimmed at one temperature.

4Level 2

An amplifier claims 110 dB CMRR. Your common-mode signal is 2 V of 50 Hz mains pickup, and you need the resulting error below 10 µV referred to the input. Does it pass? What if the interference is at 5 kHz and the part's first pole is at 5 Hz?

Solution

At DC: \(2\,\mathrm{V}/10^{110/20} = 2/316000 = 6.3\,\mathrm{\mu V}\). Passes, with little margin.

At 5 kHz, CMRR has been falling at 20 dB/decade since 5 Hz — three decades, so 60 dB gone:

\[ \mathrm{CMRR}(5\,\mathrm{kHz}) \approx 110 - 60 = 50\,\mathrm{dB} = 316 \]

\[ e = 2\,\mathrm{V}/316 = 6.3\,\mathrm{mV} \]

A thousand times over budget. The lesson is that CMRR is a DC specification and interference is not a DC problem — which is why common-mode chokes, twisted pairs and input filters exist. You cannot buy your way out of this with a better CMRR number, because the roll-off starts at the same place.

5Level 3

You need a \(\times 1000\) amplifier for a thermocouple (\(\approx 40\,\mathrm{\mu V/^\circ C}\), source resistance \(20\,\Omega\)) with a total error below 1 °C over 0–60 °C ambient. Which class of part, and why do the usual arguments about bias current not apply?

Solution

1 °C is \(40\,\mathrm{\mu V}\) at the input. Budget everything input-referred:

Precision bipolar: \(75\,\mathrm{\mu V}\) offset alone blows the budget nearly twice over before drift is counted. Drift adds \(1.3\times 60 = 78\,\mathrm{\mu V}\). Fails.

Zero-drift: \(5\,\mathrm{\mu V}\) offset, \(0.02\times 60 = 1.2\,\mathrm{\mu V}\) drift. Total about \(6\,\mathrm{\mu V}\) — comfortably inside \(40\).

Bias current does not matter here because the source is \(20\,\Omega\): even 800 pA makes \(16\,\mathrm{nV}\), which is six orders of magnitude below the budget. The usual objection to chopper amplifiers — that their bias and current noise are poor — is an objection about high source impedance, and a thermocouple has none. Match the part to the impedance, not to its reputation.

What would still need attention: the cold-junction compensation, which is a far larger error than any of this, and the thermocouple's own tolerance.

6Level 4

A non-inverting \(\times 2\) buffer using a rail-to-rail-input part on a 5 V supply shows 0.4% total harmonic distortion on a 4 V peak-to-peak sine, but is clean at 0.5 V peak-to-peak. The output is nowhere near the rails and the slew rate is not the limit. Explain, and give two fixes that do not change the part.

Solution

In a non-inverting configuration the common-mode voltage equals the input signal, so a 4 Vpp signal sweeps the common mode across most of the supply. A rail-to-rail input stage with two differential pairs hands over somewhere in the middle, and \(V_{OS}\) steps at the crossover point — typically a millivolt or two.

A step of \(\sim 1\,\mathrm{mV}\) added to a 4 Vpp signal, at the gain of 2, is roughly \(2\,\mathrm{mV}\) on \(8\,\mathrm{Vpp}\)... but the step is a sharp event, so its energy spreads across many harmonics, and a few tenths of a percent THD is entirely consistent. At 0.5 Vpp the common mode never reaches the handover, and the distortion vanishes — which is the diagnostic signature.

Two fixes with the same part:

1. Use it inverting. The common mode is then pinned at the reference voltage and never moves, so the handover is never crossed. The cost is a low input impedance set by \(R_g\) and a sign change.

2. Bias the whole signal so the common mode stays on one side of the handover — for instance reference the signal to 1 V rather than mid-supply, if the handover is above 3 V. This costs headroom.

A third option, if the supply allows: run the part on a wider supply so the signal occupies only the region below the handover.

Lab — measure a datasheet, and find one number that is wrong for you

Time 60 min Skill offset and bias measurement; reading against spec; distribution thinking Parts 5× TL072 (or any cheap part, several of them), resistors, DMM, freezer spray or a hair dryer

The point is not to check whether the manufacturer told the truth — they did. It is to see the distribution the typical column describes, and to find a condition your datasheet does not cover.

Circuit. A gain-of-1000 offset amplifier: the device under test in a non-inverting configuration with \(R_g = 10\,\Omega\), \(R_f = 10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), input grounded. The output is \(1000\times V_{OS}\), which a DMM reads directly. Use ±15 V rails.

  1. Measure the output for each of five parts. Divide by 1000. Record all five values with their signs — offset is as often negative as positive.
  2. Compare against the typical and maximum in the datasheet. Compute your sample's mean and spread. How many of your five exceed the typical value?
  3. Warm one part with a hair dryer while watching the output, and estimate its temperature with a thermocouple or by feel and time. Compute \(dV_{OS}/dT\) in µV/°C.
  4. Compare your drift to the datasheet's typical. Then look for a maximum drift figure. On most jellybean parts there is none, which is the point of the exercise.
  5. Now measure bias current: replace the ground at the non-inverting input with a \(10\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) resistor to ground. The output changes by \(1000\times I_B\times 10\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\). Compute \(I_B\).
  6. Repeat step 5 with the part warmed. Check the doubling-per-10 °C law.

What you should find. Offsets scattered on both sides of zero, most of them better than typical — a characterisation median is conservative for a mature process — and none near the maximum. That is exactly the trap: five good parts feel like evidence, and they are not. Five parts tell you almost nothing about the tail of a distribution that the maximum is guarding. Drift within a factor of two or three of the typical figure, in either direction, since it has no maximum to constrain it.

Where it goes wrong, and why that is the interesting part. Two things will contaminate the measurement, and separating them is the real exercise. First, at a gain of 1000 with \(R_g = 10\,\Omega\), any thermocouple junction in your wiring generates its own offset: a copper-to-solder junction is a few µV/°C, and your fingers near the board are a temperature gradient. Watch the reading drift as you move your hand and you have just measured a thermal EMF, not the amplifier. Second, in step 5 the \(10\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) resistor is a beautiful antenna and a leakage path; a fingerprint across it can pass more current than the input does.

Both of these are the reason precision DC circuits use guard rings, short symmetric leads and thermal shielding — and the reason a datasheet's test conditions include things like "in a still-air enclosure". You have just reproduced why that phrase is there.
