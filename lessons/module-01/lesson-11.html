Cascode Amplifier

The cascode configuration stacks a common-emitter stage with a common-base stage to achieve high gain AND wide bandwidth - solving the Miller effect problem that limits CE amplifier speed.

Key Benefit
Bandwidth increases by factor of ~Av
Trade-off
Needs more voltage headroom
Industry Use: Op-amp internal gain stages, RF LNAs, precision current mirrors, high-speed video amplifiers.

The Miller Effect Problem

In a CE amplifier, the base-collector capacitance Cbc appears multiplied at the input:

Miller Capacitance
Cin,Miller = Cbc × (1 + |Av|)
Example
Cbc=2pF, Av=100 → Cin=202pF

This massive capacitance limits bandwidth severely. The cascode eliminates this by keeping the voltage swing at Q1's collector near zero.

Cascode Circuit

How It Works

  1. Q1 (CE): Input signal drives base. Collector current flows but voltage stays nearly constant (≈ VBIAS - VBE).
  2. Q2 (CB): Base is AC-grounded via bypass cap. Passes Q1's collector current to RC, creating output voltage swing.
  3. Miller Eliminated: Q1's collector doesn't swing → Cbc isn't Miller-multiplied.
Headroom: Need VCC > 2×VCE,sat + output swing + VE. Typically VCC ≥ 8-10V minimum.

Cascode Equations

Voltage Gain
Av = -gm × RC
Same as CE
But with much higher bandwidth!
Output Impedance
Zout ≈ β × ro2 || RC
Bandwidth
f-3dB ≈ fT / (1 + low gain)
Key Insight: Q1 operates with gain ≈ 1 (collector swing is only re × IC), so Miller capacitance = Cbc × 2 instead of Cbc × 100+.

MOSFET Cascode

Even more important in CMOS - boosts output impedance from ro to gmro²:

Simple Mirror Zout
ro ≈ 50kΩ (65nm CMOS)
Cascode Mirror Zout
gmro² ≈ 5MΩ

This is why all modern op-amps use cascode topologies internally.
