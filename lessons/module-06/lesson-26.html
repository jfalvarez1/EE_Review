Interview Prep — MOSFETs

MOSFET interviews split cleanly into two conversations that share a symbol and almost nothing else. Small-signal questions are about \(g_m\), gain and the square law, and sound like the BJT material. Power switching questions are about charge, losses and heat, and have nothing to do with amplification at all.

Work out which one you are in within the first sentence. "What is the gain of this common-source stage" and "why is my FET getting hot" are asked by different interviewers looking for different people. If it is ambiguous, ask — asking is a better signal than guessing.
Square law (saturation)
ID = ½k'(W/L)(VGS−Vth)²
Transconductance
gm = 2ID/Vov
Triode / on-resistance
RDS(on) ≈ 1/[k'(W/L)Vov]
Switching loss
P = ½V·I·(tr+tf)·fsw

Part 1 — Small signal

1 Derive Warm-up

Derive \(g_m\) for a MOSFET in saturation. Give it three ways, and say which one you would use.

How to answer
\[ I_D = \tfrac{1}{2}k'\tfrac{W}{L}V_{ov}^2, \qquad V_{ov} \equiv V_{GS}-V_{th} \]
\[ g_m = \frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_{GS}} = k'\frac{W}{L}V_{ov} \]
\[ = \frac{2I_D}{V_{ov}} = \sqrt{2k'\tfrac{W}{L}I_D} \] Three equivalent forms. The middle one is the useful one, because \(I_D\) and \(V_{ov}\) are both things you choose.

"I use \(2I_D/V_{ov}\), because it makes the design trade explicit: for a given current, \(g_m\) is inversely proportional to overdrive. Small overdrive gives you gain and costs you headroom and speed; large overdrive gives you speed and linearity and costs you gain. And note it only goes as \(\sqrt{I_D}\), where a BJT's \(g_m\) is linear in \(I_C\) — so a MOSFET needs far more current to reach the same transconductance."

What they are testing: the square root. The comparison "\(g_m\) goes as \(\sqrt{I_D}\) here and as \(I_C\) for a bipolar" is the single sentence that explains why bipolar input stages still exist, and interviewers ask this expecting it.
2 Derive Standard

Common-source stage, \(I_D = 1\,\mathrm{mA}\), \(V_{ov} = 200\,\mathrm{mV}\), \(R_D = 5\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). Gain? Now compare it to a BJT at the same current and load.

How to answer
\[ g_m = \frac{2(1\,\mathrm{mA})}{0.2\,\mathrm{V}} = 10\,\mathrm{mA/V} \]
\[ A_v = -g_mR_D = -(0.01)(5000) = -50 \]
\[ \text{BJT at 1 mA: } g_m = 38.5\,\mathrm{mA/V} \Rightarrow A_v = -192 \] Nearly four times the gain for the same current and the same load.

"To match the bipolar I would need \(V_{ov} = 2I_D/g_m = 52\,\mathrm{mV}\), which is barely above the subthreshold region — where the MOSFET stops obeying the square law and starts behaving exponentially, like a BJT, and gets slow. That is the fundamental trade: the MOSFET's \(g_m\) per amp is worse, and you buy it back with area or with current, neither of which is free."

What they are testing: whether you can hold both device families in your head at once. Any question phrased as "compare" is really asking whether you know the physical reason for the difference, not just the numbers.
3 Trap Harder

Your square-law hand calculation says \(g_m = 10\,\mathrm{mA/V}\) and the simulator says 6.5. Who is wrong?

How to answer

Neither. The square law is a long-channel approximation and real short-channel devices do not obey it.

  • Velocity saturation. Above roughly \(10^4\,\mathrm{V/cm}\) carriers stop accelerating. \(I_D\) becomes closer to linear in \(V_{ov}\) than quadratic, so \(g_m\) approaches a constant \(\approx W C_{ox} v_{sat}\) and stops rising with overdrive at all.
  • Mobility degradation. The vertical field from the gate pulls carriers against the oxide interface and scatters them, so \(\mu\) itself falls as \(V_{GS}\) rises.
  • Channel-length modulation. \(I_D\) keeps rising with \(V_{DS}\) in saturation, giving finite \(r_o\) — the MOSFET's Early effect.

"So I would use the square law to get the design in the right decade and pick a topology, then trust the simulator with the real model for the numbers. In a modern process the hand calculation can be out by a factor of two, and knowing which direction it errs — always optimistic on \(g_m\) — is what makes it still useful."

What they are testing: intellectual honesty about models. The wrong answers are "the simulator is wrong" and "the hand calculation is useless". The right one names a mechanism. Anyone who has taped out silicon has had this exact argument.
4 Derive Harder

Why does a source follower on the same die have a gain noticeably below one, when a bipolar emitter follower is much closer to unity?

How to answer

Two reasons, and the second one is the one they want.

\[ A_v = \frac{g_mR_S}{1+g_mR_S} \] Already below one, and \(g_m\) is small, so \(g_mR_S\) is not large. That is the ordinary reason.
\[ A_v = \frac{g_mR_S}{1 + (g_m+g_{mb})R_S} \] The BODY EFFECT. In a follower the source moves with the signal while the body stays tied to the rail, so \(V_{SB}\) modulates and the back gate fights the front gate.
\[ g_{mb} = \chi g_m, \quad \chi \approx 0.1\text{–}0.3 \;\Longrightarrow\; A_v \lesssim 0.8 \]

"The body is a second gate you did not ask for. In a process with isolated wells you tie the body to the source and the problem disappears — which is exactly what a PMOS in an n-well lets you do, and one of the reasons PMOS source followers turn up where you would not otherwise expect them."

What they are testing: the body effect is the one MOSFET phenomenon with no bipolar analogue, so it is a reliable way to find out whether you learned MOSFETs or just relabelled your BJT knowledge.

Part 2 — Power switching

A different vocabulary. Nobody here cares about \(g_m\); they care about charge, time and joules.

5 Estimate Standard

A MOSFET switches 20 A at 48 V, 100 kHz. \(R_{DS(on)} = 5\,\mathrm{m}\Omega\), rise and fall 20 ns each, \(Q_g = 60\,\mathrm{nC}\) at 10 V gate drive, duty 50%. Total losses?

How to answer
\[ P_{cond} = I^2R_{DS(on)}D = (20)^2(0.005)(0.5) = 1.0\,\mathrm{W} \]
\[ P_{sw} = \tfrac{1}{2}VI(t_r+t_f)f = \tfrac{1}{2}(48)(20)(40\times10^{-9})(10^5) = 1.92\,\mathrm{W} \] Bigger than conduction, and this is the term people forget.
\[ P_{gate} = Q_gV_{gs}f = (60\times10^{-9})(10)(10^5) = 0.06\,\mathrm{W} \] Small here, but it is dissipated in the DRIVER, not the FET — and at 1 MHz it would be 0.6 W and would matter.
\[ P_{total} \approx 2.98\,\mathrm{W} \]

"Switching loss is twice conduction loss here, so the obvious move — buying a lower \(R_{DS(on)}\) part — is the wrong one. A bigger die has more gate charge and switches slower, so it would probably make this worse. The lever is the gate driver: faster edges cut the 1.9 W directly, bounded by how much ringing and EMI the layout tolerates."

What they are testing: whether you compute all three terms and then say which one to attack. The trap is the reflex "lower \(R_{DS(on)}\)"; recognising that \(R_{DS(on)}\) and \(Q_g\) trade against each other is the whole content of power MOSFET selection.
6 Trap Harder

A high-side N-channel switch in a 48 V buck. Why can't you just drive the gate to 10 V?

How to answer

Because the gate voltage is referred to the source, and in a high-side switch the source is the switch node — which flies up to 48 V when the FET turns on.

\[ V_{GS} = V_G - V_S = 10 - 48 = -38\,\mathrm{V} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{hard off} \]
\[ \text{Need } V_G = V_S + 10 = 58\,\mathrm{V}, \text{ above the supply} \]

"Three ways to get there. A bootstrap capacitor is the usual one: it charges to 10 V through a diode while the switch node is low, then rides up with the source and holds the gate 10 V above it. It fails if the duty cycle approaches 100%, because the cap never gets a chance to recharge — so you either add a refresh or use a charge pump, which works at 100% duty. Or you use a P-channel high-side FET, referred to the rail instead, and pay for it in \(R_{DS(on)}\) because holes are less mobile than electrons."

What they are testing: whether \(V_{GS}\) being source-referred is something you actually internalised. This is the number one power-electronics interview question and the 100% duty cycle limitation is the follow-up that separates the top answers.
7 Trap Nasty

A half-bridge destroys both FETs on the first switching edge. The gate drive timing and the dead time are correct. What happened?

How to answer

Cdv/dt-induced turn-on — also called Miller turn-on, and it is shoot-through that no amount of dead time prevents.

When the high-side device turns on, the switch node slews at perhaps 20 V/ns. That \(dV/dt\) is applied across the low-side FET's \(C_{GD}\), which injects current into its gate node:

\[ I_{inj} = C_{GD}\frac{dV}{dt} \]
\[ C_{GD} = 100\,\mathrm{pF},\ \frac{dV}{dt} = 20\,\mathrm{V/ns} \;\Rightarrow\; I = 2\,\mathrm{A} \]
\[ V_{gs} = I \cdot R_{g,\,\text{off}} = 2 \times 2\,\Omega = 4\,\mathrm{V} \] If that exceeds \(V_{th}\) — and 4 V does, comfortably — the low-side device turns partially on while the high side is already on. Both conduct, the supply is shorted, and it is over in nanoseconds.

"So the fix is not dead time, it is holding the off-state gate impedance low. A separate low-impedance turn-off path — a small PNP or a dedicated sink pin — so \(R_{g,\text{off}}\) is a fraction of an ohm; a negative off-state gate bias, minus two or minus five volts, on GaN and SiC especially; a device with a better \(C_{GD}/C_{GS}\) ratio; or slowing the edge, which trades the problem for switching loss."

What they are testing: serious power electronics knowledge. Everyone knows about dead time; far fewer know that the fastest devices fail from a mechanism dead time cannot address, and that it is why gate drivers have separate source and sink pins.
8 Trap Harder

Two MOSFETs in parallel to share 40 A. Is that safe?

How to answer

For steady-state conduction, yes, and it self-balances. For switching, no, and it does not.

  • Conduction shares itself. \(R_{DS(on)}\) has a positive temperature coefficient, roughly doubling from 25 to 125 °C. The FET taking more current gets hotter, its resistance rises, and it sheds current to the other. Negative feedback, and the reason paralleling works at all.
  • Switching does not. \(V_{th}\) has a negative tempco, so the hotter device turns on earlier and takes more of the switching transient, which heats it further. Positive feedback. Add a few nanohenries of difference in gate loop inductance between the two and one device can take essentially the whole switching loss.

"So I would parallel them, but with individual gate resistors — 5 to 10 ohms each, right at each gate — to decouple the gate loops and damp the LC formed with the package inductance. Symmetrical layout so both see the same gate and power loop, ideally the same package on the same copper pour so they thermally couple, and same date code if it is anything critical."

What they are testing: whether you distinguish static from dynamic sharing. "Yes, because positive tempco" is a half answer that misses the failure mode that actually kills paralleled devices.
9 Rapid

What is the body diode, and when does it bite you?

Answer

An unavoidable parasitic from the source-body short to the drain region. It conducts in reverse and is essential in synchronous rectification and inductive loads. It bites in hard-switched bridges because it is slow: its reverse recovery charge \(Q_{rr}\) dumps through the opposite device at turn-on, adding loss and a current spike. That single parameter is why GaN — which has no body diode and therefore zero \(Q_{rr}\) — wins in hard-switched topologies.

10 Rapid

Why is the SOA curve on a datasheet not just a constant power hyperbola?

Answer

Because of thermal instability in the linear region. At low \(V_{GS}\) the drain current has a positive tempco — a hot spot draws more current, gets hotter, and runs away before the package average temperature says anything is wrong. So the SOA curve falls below the constant-power line at high \(V_{DS}\). This is why a MOSFET chosen for switching can be destroyed by using it as a linear pass element or a hot-swap controller well inside its nominal power rating, and why parts intended for that duty are specifically characterised for linear-mode SOA.

If you remember five things

\(g_m\) goes as \(\sqrt{I_D}\)
Not linear like a BJT. That one fact explains most MOSFET-vs-BJT choices.
\(2I_D/V_{ov}\)
The form that makes the gain-versus-headroom trade visible.
\(V_{GS}\) is source-referred
Which is the entire reason bootstraps and charge pumps exist.
Three loss terms
Conduction, switching, gate. Compute all three before choosing a part.
The body is a second gate
No bipolar analogue, and it is what limits a source follower.
