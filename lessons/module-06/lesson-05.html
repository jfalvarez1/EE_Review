The Common-Gate Amplifier

The third and last way to use a three-terminal device: signal in at the source, gate held at AC ground, output at the drain. Common-source grounds the source, common-drain grounds the drain, and this grounds the gate. There is no fourth configuration, because there is no fourth terminal to ground.

It is the least intuitive of the three and the one with the most distinctive properties: a low input impedance on purpose, a non-inverting gain, and — the reason it matters most — no Miller effect at all, which makes it the fastest of the three by a wide margin.

1. The circuit

R1/R2 set the gate's DC voltage; CG holds it at AC ground, which is what makes this a common-gate stage rather than a degenerated common-source one. The signal is injected into the source, and RS both biases the device and provides a DC path.

CG is not optional, and it is the whole configuration. Remove it and the gate follows whatever the divider does, the signal appears as \(\vgs\) in the ordinary way, and you have built a common-source stage with source degeneration instead. The identity of this circuit rests entirely on one capacitor holding one node still.

2. Input impedance, derived — and why it is low

In the other two configurations you drive the gate, which draws no current, so the input impedance is whatever the bias network happens to be. Here you drive the source, and the source is where all the drain current comes out. That changes everything.

\[ \text{Apply } v_x \text{ at the source; the gate is at AC ground.} \] The standard test: drive the port with a voltage, measure the current it takes, divide.
\[ \vgs = v_G - v_S = 0 - v_x = -v_x, \qquad v_{bs} = -v_x \] Raising the source while the gate stays put reduces \(\vgs\). The body is also grounded, so it sees the same swing.
\[ i_x = -\left(\gm \vgs + g_{mb} v_{bs}\right) = (\gm + g_{mb})\,v_x \] The controlled sources pull current out of the source terminal in proportion to \(v_x\). The sign works out so that pushing the source up makes the source draw current from you.
\[ \boxed{\;Z_{in} = \frac{v_x}{i_x} = \frac{1}{\gm + g_{mb}}\;} \] Neglecting \(\ro\), which is legitimate while \(\gm \ro \gg 1\) — true for essentially any device biased in saturation. The exact result including \(\ro\) and the drain load is \(Z_{in} = (R_D + \ro)/(1 + (\gm+g_{mb})\ro)\), which reduces to the boxed form when \(R_D \ll \gm \ro^2\).

With \(\gm = 7.07\,\mathrm{mS}\) and \(\chi = 0.2\), that is \(1/8.48\,\mathrm{mS} = \mathbf{118\,\Omega}\). Low, and deliberately so.

3. Gain, and the sign that surprises people

\[ i_d = (\gm + g_{mb})\,v_{in} \] The same current computed above, now viewed as flowing through the device toward the drain.
\[ v_{out} = i_d \cdot R_D = (\gm + g_{mb})R_D\,v_{in} \] Raising the source raises the current, which pulls the drain down… but \(v_{in}\) was applied at the source, and the sign of the current reverses relative to the common-source case. The two sign flips cancel.
\[ \boxed{\;A_v = +(\gm + g_{mb})R_D\;} \] Positive. Common-gate is the only one of the three configurations with a non-inverting voltage gain and a gain magnitude greater than one.

But that voltage gain is only achieved from an ideal voltage source. Drive it from a real source of impedance \(R_{sig}\) and the low \(Z_{in}\) forms a divider:

\[ \frac{v_{out}}{v_{sig}} = \frac{(\gm+g_{mb})R_D}{1 + (\gm+g_{mb})R_{sig}} \;\xrightarrow[\ R_{sig} \gg Z_{in}\ ]{}\; \frac{R_D}{R_{sig}} \]

which contains no device parameter at all. That limit is the tell: a common-gate stage is really a current buffer. It accepts a current at a low impedance, passes it through essentially unchanged (\(A_i \approx 1\), since the same current that enters the source leaves the drain), and delivers it into a high impedance. In the four-amplifier-type table from the very first lesson, it is the current amplifier row.

4. Why it is fast: no Miller effect

This is the property that earns common-gate its place, and it follows from one observation about where \(C_{gd}\) is connected.

Common-sourceCommon-gate
Gate nodethe input, swingingAC ground, still
\(C_{gs}\) seesinput to ground — plain input capacitanceground to input — plain input capacitance
\(C_{gd}\) seesinput to output — multiplied by \((1+|A_v|)\)ground to output — a plain load capacitance
Result\(C_{in} = C_{gs} + C_{gd}(1+|A_v|)\)\(C_{in} = C_{gs}\), and that is all

The Miller effect needs a capacitance bridging input and output, so that a swing at one end drives current in at the other. Ground the gate and \(C_{gd}\) no longer bridges anything — it becomes an ordinary capacitor from the output to ground. With \(A_v = 10\) and \(C_{gd} = 5\,\mathrm{pF}\), a common-source input sees \(5 \times 11 = 55\,\mathrm{pF}\) of Miller capacitance that the common-gate simply does not have.

The consequence you will actually use

Put a common-gate stage on top of a common-source stage and you get a cascode: the lower device provides transconductance while the upper device holds its drain nearly still, so the lower device's \(C_{gd}\) never sees any gain to multiply. That is the entire idea of the cascode, and it is why the next lesson follows this one.

5. Worked example: a 50 Ω RF input stage

An antenna feeds a \(50\,\Omega\) coaxial line. If the amplifier's input is not \(50\,\Omega\), part of the signal reflects back down the cable and returns as an echo. Here impedance matching genuinely matters — unlike the signal-transfer case in the very first lesson, where matching threw away 6 dB for nothing.

\[ Z_{in} = \frac{1}{\gm + g_{mb}} = 50\,\Omega \;\Longrightarrow\; \gm + g_{mb} = 20\,\mathrm{mS} \]
\[ \gm(1+\chi) = 20\,\mathrm{mS},\ \chi = 0.2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \gm = 16.7\,\mathrm{mS} \]
\[ \id = \frac{\gm^2}{2k} = \frac{(0.0167)^2}{2(0.025)} = 5.6\,\mathrm{mA} \] Inverting \(\gm = \sqrt{2k\id}\). The match is set by the bias current, which is a pleasant property: you tune the input impedance with a current source rather than a component.
\[ A_v = (\gm+g_{mb})R_D = 0.020 \times 1500 = 30 \quad (29.5\,\mathrm{dB}) \]

What this costs. 5.6 mA is a real current for a receiver front end, and it is set by the match rather than by the gain you wanted. That is the standing objection to common-gate as an RF input: the matching condition and the noise-optimum bias are two different currents, and you cannot satisfy both. The alternatives — an inductively degenerated common-source stage, or a shunt-feedback stage — exist to break that coupling.

The pattern worth carrying away. Each configuration grounds one terminal, and whichever terminal you ground determines everything else. Ground the source and you get voltage gain with a Miller penalty; ground the drain and you get a buffer; ground the gate and you get a current buffer with no Miller. The bipolar versions — common-emitter, common-collector, common-base — map onto these one for one, with \(\beta\) and finite input resistance as the differences.

Problem Set

1Level 1

A common-gate stage has \(\gm = 10\,\mathrm{mS}\), \(\chi = 0.2\), \(R_D = 2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\). Find \(Z_{in}\) and \(A_v\) from an ideal source.

Solution

\[ g_{m,\text{tot}} = 10(1.2) = 12\,\mathrm{mS} \]

\[ Z_{in} = 1/0.012 = \mathbf{83.3\,\Omega}, \qquad A_v = 0.012 \times 2000 = \mathbf{+24} \]

Note the sign: a common-source stage with the same \(\gm\) and \(R_D\) would give \(-20\) (using \(\gm\) alone, since the body effect does not contribute when the source is grounded).

2Level 2

The same stage is driven from a source of \(R_{sig} = 1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\). What gain do you measure, and what has happened?

Solution

\[ \frac{v_{out}}{v_{sig}} = \frac{g_{m,\text{tot}}R_D}{1+g_{m,\text{tot}}R_{sig}} = \frac{24}{1 + 12} = \mathbf{1.85} \]

The gain collapsed from 24 to under 2. The \(83\,\Omega\) input against a \(1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) source is a 12:1 divider, and it throws away almost all of the signal before the transistor sees it.

This is the loading theorem from the first lesson, applied to a stage whose input impedance is deliberately low — and it is why a common-gate stage must be fed from something with a low output impedance, or from a current source, and never from a high-impedance node.

3Level 3

A photodiode with \(2\,\mathrm{pF}\) of junction capacitance feeds a common-gate stage biased at \(1\,\mathrm{mA}\) (\(k = 25\,\mathrm{mA/V^2}\), \(\chi=0.2\)). Estimate the input pole, and compare with the same photodiode feeding a \(100\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) transimpedance node.

Solution

\(\gm = \sqrt{2(0.025)(0.001)} = 7.07\,\mathrm{mS}\), so \(g_{m,\text{tot}} = 8.48\,\mathrm{mS}\) and \(Z_{in} = 118\,\Omega\).

\[ f_p = \frac{1}{2\pi Z_{in}C} = \frac{1}{2\pi(118)(2\times10^{-12})} = \mathbf{674\,MHz} \]

Against a \(100\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) node:

\[ f_p = \frac{1}{2\pi(10^5)(2\times10^{-12})} = 796\,\mathrm{kHz} \]

Nearly a thousand times faster, because the diode's capacitance is looking into 118 Ω instead of 100 kΩ. This is the standard trick for fast optical front ends: the common-gate stage holds the photodiode at a low, fixed impedance so its capacitance cannot form a slow pole, and the current is converted to a voltage further downstream where the node is no longer capacitive.

4Level 4

Explain why stacking a common-gate stage on a common-source stage (a cascode) gives high gain and high bandwidth, when each configuration alone sacrifices one for the other.

Solution

The bandwidth limit of a common-source stage is Miller: \(C_{gd}\) is multiplied by \((1+|A_v|)\), and \(|A_v|\) is large precisely because the drain swings a lot.

Put a common-gate device above it and the lower transistor's drain now looks into \(Z_{in} = 1/g_{m,\text{tot}}\) — about a hundred ohms. The lower device's local voltage gain collapses to \(-\gm/(g_{m,\text{tot}}) \approx -0.83\), so its Miller multiplier is about 1.8 instead of 11 or 100. Its \(C_{gd}\) has nothing to amplify.

The signal is not lost, because the lower device is a current source: it delivers \(\gm v_{in}\) as a current regardless of how little its drain moves, and the common-gate stage passes that current on with \(A_i \approx 1\) into a high-impedance load where the voltage gain is finally developed.

So the gain happens at a node with no capacitance bridging it, and the Miller capacitance sits at a node with no gain. The cascode separates the two jobs that were fighting each other — and that separation is why nearly every wideband and every high-gain integrated stage is cascoded.
