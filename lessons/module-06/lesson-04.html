The Source Follower (Common-Drain)

The FET's buffer: input at the gate, output at the source, gain of about one. It is the counterpart of the emitter follower, and it exists for the same reason — to turn a high-impedance node into one you can actually drive.

It also has a defect its bipolar cousin does not, and the defect is the interesting part: a source follower cannot reach a gain of one, no matter how much current you spend. The body effect stops it, and this lesson works out exactly where the ceiling is.

1. The circuit

The drain sits at the supply, which is AC ground, so the signal current flows gate→source→RS. The output follows the input, one \(\vgs\) below it.

2. Gain, derived — including the term that ruins it

The body terminal is tied to ground, but the source moves with the signal. So \(v_{bs} = -v_{out}\), and the back gate injects its own current \(g_{mb}v_{bs}\) — opposing the one you want.

\[ v_{gs} = v_{in} - v_{out}, \qquad v_{bs} = -\,v_{out} \] Both gate-source and body-source voltages are referenced to a source that is moving. This is the whole difficulty.
\[ \gm v_{gs} + g_{mb}v_{bs} = \frac{v_{out}}{R_S} \] KCL at the source node: the two controlled currents must equal the current in \(R_S\).
\[ \gm(v_{in}-v_{out}) - g_{mb}v_{out} = \frac{v_{out}}{R_S} \]
\[ \gm v_{in} = v_{out}\!\left(\gm + g_{mb} + \frac{1}{R_S}\right) \] Collect \(v_{out}\). Note all three terms are conductances in parallel at the source node — which is exactly what they are.
\[ \boxed{\;A_v = \frac{\gm}{\gm + g_{mb} + 1/R_S}\;} \]

Now take the limit that should give a perfect buffer. Let \(R_S \to \infty\), or replace it with an ideal current source, so the \(1/R_S\) term vanishes:

The ceiling \[ A_{v,\max} = \frac{\gm}{\gm + g_{mb}} = \frac{1}{1+\chi}, \qquad \chi \equiv \frac{g_{mb}}{\gm} \approx 0.1\text{–}0.3 \]

With a typical \(\chi = 0.2\), the best a source follower can do is \(\mathbf{0.833}\). Spending more current raises \(\gm\), but it raises \(g_{mb}\) in the same proportion, so the ratio does not move. You cannot buy your way to unity gain.

The fix, when you need it. Tie the body to the source instead of to ground. Then \(v_{bs}=0\), \(g_{mb}\) contributes nothing, and the gain limit becomes 1. On a discrete part the body is the source — a packaged MOSFET has three terminals precisely because the manufacturer already did this. On an integrated NMOS in a common p-substrate you cannot: every device shares the substrate, and \(\chi\) is a fact of life. It is one reason PMOS source followers appear in n-well processes, where each PMOS can have its own well.

3. Output impedance — the reason you built it

\[ Z_{out} = \frac{1}{\gm + g_{mb}} \;\Big\|\; R_S \] Ground the input and drive the source: the transistor presents \(1/(\gm+g_{mb})\), in parallel with the bias resistor.
\[ \gm = 7.07\,\mathrm{mS},\ \chi=0.2,\ R_S=1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}: \quad Z_{out} = \frac{1}{8.48\,\mathrm{mS}}\ \|\ 1\,\mathrm{k} = 118\ \|\ 1000 = 105\,\Omega \] A hundred ohms out, essentially infinite in. That is the transformation you paid a transistor for.

Compare with the emitter follower, which achieves \(Z_{out} \approx r_e + R_s/\beta\) — typically a few ohms. The bipolar wins here too, and for the same reason as everywhere else: its \(\gm\) is several times larger at the same current.

4. What bites you

  • The DC level shift is not small. \(V_{out} = V_{in} - \vgs\), and \(\vgs = \vth + \vov\) can be 1–4 V on a discrete part with a wide threshold spread. A follower is a buffer for AC and a subtraction for DC, and the subtraction varies part to part.
  • It cannot pull down. An NMOS follower sources current through the transistor and sinks it only through \(R_S\). Drive a capacitive load with a falling edge and the slew rate is \(V/(R_S C)\) — nothing to do with the transistor. Followers are asymmetric, which is why push-pull output stages exist.
  • It can oscillate. A source follower driving a capacitive load through gate inductance is a Colpitts oscillator in disguise: the device provides gain, and \(C_{gs}\) with the load capacitance forms the divider. A small series gate resistor, tens to hundreds of ohms, is the standard cure and costs almost nothing.
  • \(\chi\) is not a constant. Body effect depends on \(V_{SB}\), which the signal is modulating. So \(\chi\) moves with the output swing, the gain moves with it, and that is second-harmonic distortion.

Problem Set

1Level 1

A source follower has \(\gm = 5\,\mathrm{mS}\), \(\chi = 0.15\) and \(R_S = 2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\). Find \(A_v\) and \(Z_{out}\).

Solution

\[ A_v = \frac{5}{5 + 0.75 + 0.5} = \frac{5}{6.25} = \mathbf{0.80} \]

\[ Z_{out} = \frac{1}{5.75\,\mathrm{mS}}\ \Big\|\ 2\,\mathrm{k} = 174\ \|\ 2000 = \mathbf{160\,\Omega} \]

2Level 2

Your follower measures \(A_v = 0.80\) and you need \(0.95\). You try doubling the drain current. Does it work?

Solution

No, and only slightly. Doubling \(\id\) raises \(\gm\) by \(\sqrt2\), which helps the \(1/R_S\) term but not the \(g_{mb}\) term — that scales with \(\gm\), so the ratio \(\chi\) is unchanged. With \(\chi=0.2\) the hard ceiling is \(1/1.2 = 0.833\), so 0.95 is unreachable at any current.

The routes that do work: tie the body to the source if the process allows (ceiling becomes 1.0); use a bipolar emitter follower instead; or put the whole thing inside a feedback loop and let loop gain force the transfer to unity. Recognising that a specification is unreachable with a topology — rather than grinding at the bias point — is the skill here.

3Level 3

A follower biased at \(1\,\mathrm{mA}\) through \(R_S = 1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) drives a \(1\,\mathrm{nF}\) load. Estimate the maximum output slew rate in each direction.

Solution

Rising: the transistor can source far more than the bias current, so the limit is set by how hard the gate is driven — typically tens of V/µs, not the bottleneck.

Falling: the transistor can only turn off. All the discharge current comes from \(R_S\), and at best that is the bias current:

\[ \frac{dV}{dt} = \frac{I}{C} = \frac{1\,\mathrm{mA}}{1\,\mathrm{nF}} = 1\,\mathrm{V/\mu s} \]

A factor of tens between the two edges. The waveform is visibly asymmetric, and the fix is more standing current or an active pull-down — which is a push-pull stage, arrived at from necessity rather than from a textbook.
