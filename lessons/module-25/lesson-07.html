LED Driver Design

LEDs require constant current drive for consistent brightness and long life. This lesson covers LED driver topologies from simple linear regulators to high-efficiency switch-mode drivers for automotive, display, and general lighting applications.

Industry Reference: Based on TI LED Driver Design Guide, Analog Devices LED Driver Fundamentals, and Diodes Inc. Application Notes.

Section 1: LED Characteristics

LEDs are current-driven devices with an exponential V-I relationship:

Red (AlGaInP) 1.8 - 2.2V 20mA - 1A 50-100 lm/W
Green (InGaN) 2.9 - 3.5V 20mA - 1A 100-150 lm/W

Temperature Coefficient: LED VF decreases ~2-4mV/°C. This means constant voltage drive causes thermal runaway! Always use constant current drive.

What to run: a transient run of 1 ms in steps of 100 ns. At 1 µs you would get six samples per switching cycle, which draws the ripple as noise.

What to watch: I(L1), V(led_cathode), V(sw), V(pwm).

This is a hysteretic regulator rather than a fixed-frequency one, which is what most LED drivers actually are: the two comparators watch the sense resistor against a 450–550 mV band and the latch remembers which fired last. Across 286 mΩ that band is 1.573 to 1.923 A, so 1.748 A average with 350 mA of ripple, and the current is regulated directly — there is no error amplifier and nothing to compensate.

The price is that the frequency is an outcome rather than a choice. ton = ΔI L/(VIN − VLED) = 2.52 µs and toff = ΔI L/VLED = 3.47 µs, giving 167 kHz — but raise VIN and ton shortens while toff does not, so the frequency climbs. A hysteretic converter trades a predictable spectrum for a loop that cannot be unstable, which is a fine trade for a lamp and a bad one for anything that has to pass conducted emissions.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.
