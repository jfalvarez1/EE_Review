Based on classical control theory and Evans' root locus method

Introduction

The root locus is a graphical technique that shows how the closed-loop poles move in the s-plane as a system parameter (usually gain) varies. It provides intuitive insight into how feedback affects system stability and transient response.

Why Root Locus?

Root locus analysis reveals:

  • How closed-loop poles move as gain increases from 0 to ∞
  • What gain values cause instability (poles crossing into RHP)
  • How damping and natural frequency change with gain
  • The effect of adding poles or zeros to compensation

Closed-Loop Pole Locations

For a system with loop gain T(s) = KG(s), the closed-loop transfer function is:

Closed-Loop Poles

H(s) = KG(s) / (1 + KG(s))

The closed-loop poles are the roots of:

1 + KG(s) = 0, or equivalently, KG(s) = -1

This means the poles occur where:

  • |KG(s)| = 1 (magnitude condition)
  • ∠G(s) = 180° + n×360° (angle condition)

Root Locus Construction Rules

The root locus can be sketched using these rules (derived from the angle condition):

Basic Root Locus Rules

  1. Number of branches: Equal to the number of poles of G(s)
  2. Starting points (K=0): Open-loop poles
  3. Ending points (K=∞): Open-loop zeros (or infinity)
  4. Real axis segments: To the left of an odd number of real poles and zeros
  5. Asymptotes: If n poles and m zeros, (n-m) branches go to infinity at angles θ = (180° + k×360°)/(n-m)
  6. Asymptote centroid: σ = (Σpoles - Σzeros)/(n-m)
  7. Breakaway/break-in points: Where multiple branches meet/separate on real axis

Interpreting the Root Locus

Stability Boundary

The system becomes unstable when any closed-loop pole crosses into the right-half plane (Re > 0). The gain at this crossing is the critical gain or ultimate gain.

Damping and Natural Frequency

For complex conjugate poles, the location in the s-plane determines system behavior:

Pole Location and Response

For a pole at s = -σ + jωd:

  • Natural frequency: ωn = √(σ² + ωd²)
  • Damping ratio: ζ = σ/ωn = cos(θ) where θ is angle from negative real axis
  • Damped frequency: ωd = ωn√(1-ζ²)

Damping Interpretation

ζ > 1Overdamped (real poles)
ζ = 1Critically damped
0 < ζ < 1Underdamped (complex poles)
ζ = 0Marginally stable (poles on jω axis)
ζ < 0Unstable (poles in RHP)

Effect of Adding Poles and Zeros

Adding a Pole

Adding a pole to G(s):

  • Pulls the root locus toward the RHP
  • Reduces stability margins
  • May cause the locus to cross into instability at lower gain

Adding a Zero

Adding a zero to G(s):

  • Pulls the root locus toward the LHP
  • Improves stability
  • Can be used for compensation (lead compensator)

Compensation Insight

A lead compensator adds a zero closer to the origin than its pole, pulling nearby root locus branches toward the LHP and improving damping.

Root Locus for Common Systems

Two Real Poles

G(s) = 1/[(s+p₁)(s+p₂)]

  • Locus starts at -p₁ and -p₂
  • Branches meet at breakaway point, become complex
  • For p₁ ≠ p₂: breakaway at s = -(p₁+p₂)/2
  • Goes to infinity along asymptotes at ±90°
  • Never crosses into RHP → always stable!

Three Real Poles

G(s) = 1/[s(s+p₁)(s+p₂)]

  • Two branches eventually go to infinity in RHP
  • System becomes unstable at critical gain
  • Asymptotes at 60°, 180°, -60°

Complex Poles Plus Real Pole

G(s) = 1/[(s+a)(s²+2ζωns+ωn²)]

  • Complex poles start away from real axis
  • Real pole starts on real axis
  • Branches may migrate toward each other

Worked Example: Op-Amp with Dominant Pole Compensation

Problem

An op-amp has open-loop transfer function A(s) = 10⁵/[(s/10 + 1)(s/10⁵ + 1)(s/10⁶ + 1)]

Analyze the root locus as feedback gain β varies from 0 to 1.

Step 1: Identify Poles

Open-loop poles at: s = -10, -10⁵, -10⁶ rad/s

DC gain A₀ = 10⁵ (100 dB)

Step 2: Loop Gain

T(s) = βA(s) = β × 10⁵/[(s/10 + 1)(s/10⁵ + 1)(s/10⁶ + 1)]

As β varies from 0 to 1, the effective gain varies from 0 to 10⁵

Step 3: Sketch Root Locus

  • Three branches starting at -10, -10⁵, -10⁶
  • Real axis segments: from -∞ to -10⁶, from -10⁵ to -10
  • Asymptotes at 60°, 180°, -60° from centroid at σ = (-10 - 10⁵ - 10⁶)/3 ≈ -0.37×10⁶

Step 4: Find Critical Gain

Using Routh-Hurwitz or simulation, find the gain where poles cross jω axis.

For this system, critical gain Kcrit ≈ 1.1×10¹¹

Since maximum loop gain is 10⁵, the system is always stable with any β from 0 to 1.

Conclusion

The dominant pole at -10 rad/s ensures stability by pushing the asymptote centroid far into the LHP. This is the principle behind dominant pole compensation.

Root Locus and Bode Plot Connection

Root locus and Bode analysis are complementary:

Variable Gain K Frequency ω
Shows Closed-loop pole locations Open-loop magnitude and phase

Practical Design Considerations

Art of Electronics Wisdom

  • Parasitic poles: Real systems have more poles than models suggest. High-frequency poles can destabilize systems at high gain.
  • Nonlinearities: Saturation effectively reduces gain, moving poles along the locus toward lower-K positions.
  • Parameter variations: Pole locations shift with temperature, aging, and component tolerances.
  • Complex zeros: Can create unusual locus shapes, including branches that curve back toward the RHP.
