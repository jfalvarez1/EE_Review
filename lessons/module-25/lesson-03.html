Stability Analysis: Bode Plots and Phase Margin

Feedback systems can oscillate if not properly designed. This lesson develops the stability analysis techniques using Bode plots, phase margin, and gain margin - the practical tools every analog designer must master.

Art of Electronics Warning: "Every feedback circuit is a potential oscillator. The question is not whether it might oscillate, but whether you've designed it so that it won't."

The Stability Problem

Recall the closed-loop transfer function:

ACL = A / (1 + Aβ)

What happens when 1 + Aβ = 0?

This means Aβ = -1, which requires:

  • |Aβ| = 1 (loop gain magnitude = 1)
  • ∠Aβ = -180° (loop gain phase = -180°)

When both conditions are met, the system oscillates!

Barkhausen Criterion

A feedback system will oscillate if, at some frequency:

  1. Loop gain |Aβ| ≥ 1
  2. Phase shift of Aβ = -180° (total phase = 360° around the loop)

For stability, we need to violate at least one condition at all frequencies.

Bode Plot Fundamentals

The Bode plot shows magnitude (in dB) and phase versus frequency on a log scale.

Key Relationships

Pole/Zero Magnitude Effect Phase Effect
Simple pole at fp -20 dB/decade after fp -45° at fp, -90° ultimately
Simple zero at fz +20 dB/decade after fz +45° at fz, +90° ultimately
Double pole at fp -40 dB/decade after fp -90° at fp, -180° ultimately
RHP zero at fz +20 dB/decade -45° at fz (phase decreases!)

The 45° Rule (Razavi)

Phase change of ±45° occurs per decade, centered at the pole/zero frequency:

  • At f = fp/10: phase shift is -5.7°
  • At f = fp: phase shift is -45°
  • At f = 10fp: phase shift is -84.3°

In practice, significant phase shift occurs over about two decades centered on the pole.

Phase Margin and Gain Margin

Definitions

  • Unity-gain crossover frequency (fc): Where |Aβ| = 1 (0 dB)
  • Phase margin (PM): 180° + ∠Aβ at fc
  • Gain margin (GM): 1/|Aβ| at the frequency where ∠Aβ = -180°

Design Guidelines

Phase Margin Behavior Recommendation
< 0° Unstable (oscillates) Never acceptable
0-30° Severe ringing, near-oscillation Marginal, add compensation
30-45° Some overshoot, acceptable for fast systems Acceptable for speed-critical
45-60° Good damping, slight overshoot Recommended minimum
60-90° Well-damped, no overshoot Excellent

Worked Example: Op-Amp Stability (Razavi Style)

Problem

An op-amp has:

  • DC gain A0 = 100,000 (100 dB)
  • Two poles: fp1 = 10 Hz, fp2 = 1 MHz

It's used in a unity-gain buffer (β = 1). Is it stable?

Solution

Step 1: Find the unity-gain crossover frequency

For a two-pole system with well-separated poles:

|A(f)| ≈ A0 / [(1 + f/fp1)(1 + f/fp2)]

At high frequencies (f >> fp1 but f << fp2):

|A(f)| ≈ A0 × fp1 / f = GBW / f

where GBW = A0 × fp1 = 100,000 × 10 Hz = 1 MHz

Unity-gain crossover: fc = GBW = 1 MHz

Step 2: Calculate phase at crossover

∠A(fc) = -tan-1(fc/fp1) - tan-1(fc/fp2)
∠A(1MHz) = -tan-1(1M/10) - tan-1(1M/1M)
∠A(1MHz) = -89.999° - 45° = -135°

Step 3: Calculate phase margin

PM = 180° + (-135°) = 45°

Conclusion: PM = 45° is marginally acceptable. There will be some overshoot (~16%) in the step response, but the system is stable.

Why Op-Amps Are Internally Compensated

Most general-purpose op-amps have a dominant pole at very low frequency (10-100 Hz) to ensure stability at unity gain.

The Compensation Trade-off

Problem: A multi-stage amplifier naturally has multiple poles. Without compensation, the second pole would be within the unity-gain bandwidth, causing instability.

Solution: Add a "dominant pole" at very low frequency, pushing unity-gain crossover below where other poles contribute significant phase shift.

Cost: Reduced bandwidth! The op-amp is slower than it needs to be for higher gains.

Decompensated Op-Amps

Some op-amps are intentionally decompensated for use at higher gains:

  • Unity-gain stable: Guaranteed PM > 45° at G = 1
  • Gain of 2 stable: Only stable for G ≥ 2 (e.g., OP37)
  • Gain of 10 stable: Only stable for G ≥ 10

Using a decompensated op-amp at too low a gain will cause oscillation!

The Effect of Capacitive Loads

Capacitive loads add a pole to the loop gain, reducing phase margin:

Art of Electronics: "Capacitive Load Catastrophe"

The output impedance of an op-amp (typically 50-100Ω at high frequency) combined with a load capacitor CL creates a pole at:

fp,load = 1 / (2π × Zout × CL)

This additional pole can steal 90° of phase margin!

Solutions for Capacitive Loads

Technique How It Works Trade-off
Isolation resistor R in series with output, moves load outside loop Slight output impedance increase
"Snubber" (R-C) Adds zero to cancel load pole Requires tuning
Feedback capacitor Cf across feedback resistor adds lead compensation Reduces high-frequency gain
Use stable op-amp Some op-amps are designed for capacitive loads Usually lower bandwidth

Step Response and Stability

Phase margin directly affects transient response:

Phase Margin Damping Ratio ζ Overshoot Settling Time
30° 0.30 37% Long (many cycles)
45° 0.45 23% Moderate
60° 0.58 10% Good
76° 1.0 0% Critically damped
90° >1 0% Overdamped

Razavi's Approximation

For a second-order system with phase margin PM:

ζ ≈ tan(PM) / 2 (for PM < 60°)
Overshoot ≈ exp(-πζ/√(1-ζ²)) × 100%
