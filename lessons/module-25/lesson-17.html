Flyback Converter & Transformer Design

The flyback converter is the workhorse of isolated power supplies under 100W. From USB chargers to telecom power modules, flyback topologies dominate due to their simplicity, low component count, and inherent isolation. This lesson covers the complete design flow from specification to working prototype.

Industry Applications

  • USB-C/PD Chargers - 5V to 20V multi-output adapters
  • LED Drivers - Isolated constant-current supplies
  • Auxiliary Power - Bias supplies for gate drivers, controllers
  • Telecom Equipment - -48V to 3.3V/5V isolated converters
  • Industrial I/O - Isolated sensor power modules

1. Flyback Operating Principle

Unlike a true transformer that transfers energy instantaneously, the flyback "transformer" is actually a coupled inductor that stores energy in its core during the ON time and releases it to the secondary during OFF time.

Energy Transfer Phases

ON Time (MOSFET Conducting)

  • Current ramps up in primary: \( I_p = \frac{V_{in} \cdot t}{L_p} \)
  • Energy stored in magnetic field: \( E = \frac{1}{2}L_p I_{pk}^2 \)
  • Secondary diode reverse-biased
  • Output capacitor supplies load

OFF Time (MOSFET Off)

  • Core field collapses, inducing secondary voltage
  • Secondary diode forward-biased
  • Energy transfers to output: \( E_{out} = \frac{1}{2}L_s I_{s,pk}^2 \)
  • Current ramps down: \( I_s = I_{s,pk} - \frac{V_{out} \cdot t}{L_s} \)

Fundamental Flyback Relationships

Turns Ratio: \( n = \frac{N_p}{N_s} \)

Reflected Voltage: \( V_R = \frac{V_{out} + V_f}{n} \) (Vf = diode forward drop)

MOSFET Voltage Stress: \( V_{DS,max} = V_{in,max} + V_R + V_{spike} \)

Duty Cycle (CCM): \( D = \frac{V_{out}}{V_{out} + n \cdot V_{in}} \)

2. DCM vs CCM Operation

Flyback converters can operate in two modes, each with distinct characteristics that affect control loop design and component stress.

Parameter DCM (Discontinuous) CCM (Continuous)
Inductor Current Returns to zero each cycle Never reaches zero
Peak Current Higher (2× average) Lower (closer to average)
MOSFET/Diode Stress Higher peak, lower RMS Lower peak, higher RMS
Control Dynamics Single pole, easier to stabilize RHP zero, more complex compensation
Efficiency at Light Load Better (ZVS possible) Worse (continuous losses)
Transformer Size Larger (higher peak flux) Smaller (lower peak flux)
Typical Power Range < 50W 50W - 150W

Critical Conduction Mode (CrCM / BCM)

Operating at the DCM/CCM boundary offers benefits of both modes. Controllers like the UCC28C42 can implement this mode with variable frequency, achieving ZVS while minimizing peak currents.

Step 2: Core Selection

The transformer core must handle the required energy storage without saturating. Key parameter is the Area Product (Ap):

\[ A_p = A_e \times A_w = \frac{L_p \cdot I_{pk}^2}{K_u \cdot B_{max} \cdot J} \times 10^8 \text{ (cm}^4\text{)} \]

Where: Ae = core cross-section, Aw = window area, Ku = window utilization (0.3-0.4), Bmax = max flux (0.25-0.3T for ferrite), J = current density (400-600 A/cm²)

Step 3: Turns Calculation

Primary Turns

\[ N_p = \frac{L_p \cdot I_{pk}}{B_{max} \cdot A_e} \times 10^6 \]

Secondary Turns

\[ N_s = \frac{N_p}{n} = \frac{N_p \cdot (V_{out} + V_f)}{V_R} \]

Auxiliary Winding (for controller bias)

\[ N_{aux} = \frac{N_s \cdot V_{aux}}{V_{out} + V_f} \]

Step 4: Gap Calculation

Flyback transformers require an air gap to store energy and prevent core saturation:

\[ l_g = \frac{\mu_0 \cdot N_p^2 \cdot A_e}{L_p} \times 10^3 \text{ (mm)} \]

For EE/EI cores, the gap is typically ground in the center leg. The actual spacer thickness is lg/2 since flux crosses two gaps.

Gap Fringing Considerations

  • Fringing flux near gap increases effective area by ~10-20%
  • Keep windings away from gap to reduce eddy current losses
  • Distributed gap (low-perm material) reduces fringing but limits inductance control
  • Verify Bmax doesn't exceed 0.3T at Vin,min and max Ipk

4. Leakage Inductance & Snubber Design

Leakage inductance is unavoidable in any transformer and causes voltage spikes when the MOSFET turns off. Proper snubber design is critical for reliability.

Snubber Design Equations

Leakage Energy per Cycle

\[ E_{lk} = \frac{1}{2} L_{lk} \cdot I_{pk}^2 \]

Clamp Voltage Selection

Choose Vclamp such that: \( V_{DS,max} = V_{in,max} + V_{clamp} < V_{DS,rating} \times 0.8 \)

Typical: \( V_{clamp} = 1.5 \times V_R \) to \( 2.0 \times V_R \)

Clamp Resistor

\[ R_{clamp} = \frac{V_{clamp}^2}{P_{lk}} = \frac{V_{clamp}^2}{E_{lk} \cdot f_{sw}} \]

Clamp Capacitor

\[ C_{clamp} \geq \frac{V_{clamp}}{R_{clamp} \cdot \Delta V \cdot f_{sw}} \]

Where ΔV is acceptable ripple (typically 5-10% of Vclamp)

Snubber Best Practices

  • Use fast recovery or ultrafast diodes (≤75ns trr)
  • Cclamp should be film or ceramic (low ESR)
  • Rclamp power rating: use 2× calculated Plk for margin
  • Consider TVS diode across MOSFET for extra protection
  • Route snubber directly to MOSFET drain, not through long traces

5. Component Selection

Primary MOSFET Selection

Parameter Requirement Typical Selection
VDS Rating ≥ 1.25 × (Vin,max + Vclamp) 600V or 650V for universal input
ID Rating ≥ 2 × Ipk,pri (for thermal margin) 2-10A depending on power level
RDS(on) Low for efficiency, but secondary to Coss 0.5-2Ω at 100°C
Coss Low for switching loss reduction <100pF at 400V
Qg Low for fast switching and low drive power <30nC for good efficiency

Output Diode Selection

Diode Voltage Rating

\[ V_{RRM} \geq 1.25 \times \left( V_{out} + \frac{V_{in,max}}{n} \right) \]

Diode Current Rating

\[ I_{F,avg} = I_{out} \cdot (1-D_{min}) \]

\[ I_{F,peak} = I_{sec,pk} = n \cdot I_{pri,pk} \]

Diode Types:

  • Schottky - Low Vf (~0.3-0.5V), fast, but limited to ~100V. Best for low Vout.
  • Ultrafast - Higher Vf (~0.8-1.2V), but available to 600V. Use for Vout > 24V.
  • SiC Schottky - Best of both worlds, but expensive. Use for high-efficiency designs.

Output Capacitor Selection

Capacitance for ripple:

\[ C_{out} \geq \frac{I_{out} \cdot D_{max}}{f_{sw} \cdot \Delta V_{out}} \]

ESR for ripple:

\[ ESR \leq \frac{\Delta V_{out}}{I_{sec,pk}} \]

Use low-ESR electrolytics or polymer caps. Parallel multiple caps to reduce ESR and increase ripple current rating.

6. Practical Winding Techniques

Winding Order (Interleaved)

Winding Best Practices

  • Interleaving reduces leakage inductance by 4× compared to non-interleaved
  • Triple-insulated wire for secondary eliminates tape layers (safety rated)
  • Margin tape on bobbin edges ensures creepage distance (4-8mm for reinforced)
  • Litz wire reduces skin effect losses above 100kHz
  • Bank winding (wound in same direction each layer) minimizes capacitance

Safety Requirements (IEC 62368-1)

Insulation Type Creepage Clearance Tape Layers
Basic (250Vac) 2.5mm 2.0mm 2 layers
Reinforced (250Vac) 5.0mm 4.0mm 6 layers or TIW

8. Design Checklist

Pre-Design

  • ☐ Define input voltage range (consider rectified AC ripple)
  • ☐ Define output voltage, current, and ripple requirements
  • ☐ Choose DCM or CCM based on power level
  • ☐ Select switching frequency (higher = smaller magnetics, more switching loss)

Transformer Design

  • ☐ Calculate Lp for chosen operating mode
  • ☐ Select core based on Area Product
  • ☐ Calculate turns ratio for target reflected voltage
  • ☐ Calculate primary and secondary turns
  • ☐ Calculate gap length
  • ☐ Verify Bmax < 0.3T at worst case
  • ☐ Choose wire gauge for acceptable fill factor
  • ☐ Plan interleaving for low leakage

Power Stage

  • ☐ MOSFET voltage rating > Vin,max + Vclamp with margin
  • ☐ MOSFET current rating > 2× peak current
  • ☐ Output diode voltage rating verified
  • ☐ Output capacitor ripple current rating adequate
  • ☐ Snubber designed for measured leakage

Control & Safety

  • ☐ Feedback loop compensated for target bandwidth
  • ☐ Optocoupler CTR variation accounted for
  • ☐ Creepage/clearance requirements met
  • ☐ Y-capacitor value appropriate for leakage current
