The Ideal Amplifier

An amplifier is a specification before it is a circuit. Long before you know whether it is built from BJTs, FETs or an op-amp, you can say what it must do, and four numbers say it: gain, input impedance, output impedance, and bandwidth.

This lesson is about the first three. They are not decoration on top of gain — they are the reason a gain of 10 built by a careful person measures 9.09 on the bench.

Why this comes first: every configuration in the next lesson (inverting, non-inverting, buffer, summing, difference) is solved with two rules and no knowledge of transistors whatsoever. You can design working circuits for months before you open the box. The box gets opened later in the path, as the answer to “how was that built”, not as a prerequisite.

1. Why there are exactly four kinds of amplifier

An amplifier's input is either a voltage or a current. Its output is either a voltage or a current. Two choices, two ports, four combinations — and that is the whole list. There is no fifth kind.

The impedance columns are not arbitrary conventions to memorise. Each one follows in one line from what the amplifier is trying not to disturb, and section 2 derives them.

Where this returns: those same four combinations reappear later as the four feedback topologies — series-shunt, series-series, shunt-shunt, shunt-series. That is not a coincidence. Feedback is how an engineer builds a chosen row of this table on purpose, by raising or lowering each impedance deliberately.

2. The derivation: why Zin must be large

Nothing you measure is an ideal source. A real signal source is a source plus a series impedance Rs — a microphone's coil resistance, a sensor bridge's arm, the output impedance of the stage before yours. Connect your amplifier and you have built a voltage divider whether you meant to or not:

Vin = Vs · Zin / (Rs + Zin)
fractional loss = 1 − Zin/(Rs+Zin) = Rs / (Rs + Zin) ≈ Rs/Zin   when Zin ≫ Rs

That last approximation is the useful form: the fractional error is roughly the ratio of the two impedances. Want to lose less than 0.1%? Make Zin at least 1000× Rs. That is the entire content of “input impedance should be high”, and it tells you how high, which the slogan does not.

The output does the same thing against the load, in mirror image:

Vload = AvVin · RL / (Zout + RL)

So Zout must be small compared to the load, for exactly the same reason with the roles swapped. And now the other three rows of the table follow without any new ideas: if the input is a current, you want to steal all of it, so Zin must be small (a short circuit takes the whole current). If the output is a current, it must not care what load you hang on it, so Zout must be large. Four rows, one argument.

3. Worked example: the gain of 10 that measures 9.09

A sensor with an open-circuit output of 100 mV and a source resistance of 100 kΩ drives a non-inverting amplifier configured for a gain of 10, built on an LM358. You expect 1.000 V and measure 0.909 V. Nothing is broken.

LM358 input resistance: about 1 MΩ (bipolar input stage)
Vin = 100 mV × 1 M / (100 k + 1 M) = 100 mV × 0.909 = 90.9 mV
Vout = 10 × 90.9 mV = 0.909 V   — a 9.1% error

The amplifier's gain was exactly 10. The system gain was 9.09, and the missing 9.1% never entered the amplifier at all. Swap to an OPA1656 (CMOS input, input resistance around 1013 Ω) and the same calculation gives an error of 10−8 — utterly negligible, and the measurement reads 1.000 V.

The part number was the fix. No topology change, no extra components: the bipolar input stage of the LM358 draws base current and presents megohms, the FET input of the OPA1656 presents teraohms. This is what “pick the right part” means in practice, and it is why the datasheet lesson later in the path exists.

Input impedance is set by what the input transistors are, which is why it clusters by technology rather than varying smoothly:

Read that last row carefully. At 3 fA the amplifier has stopped being the limit — a fingerprint across the PCB conducts more than the input pin does. Choosing a better part past this point buys you nothing; the design problem has moved to guard rings and cleaning. Knowing when a spec stops mattering is as much of the skill as reading it.

5. What bites you

  • The 10× probe that changes the circuit. A 1× scope probe is 1 MΩ shunted by ~100 pF. On a high-impedance node that capacitance is the dominant load: at 100 kHz, 100 pF is 16 kΩ. The circuit that oscillates only when you probe it is not a mystery — you added the load that caused it.
  • DC input impedance is not the number you need above DC. Every datasheet's teraohms is a DC figure. In parallel sits Cin, a few pF, and that is what your source sees at frequency. A 1013 Ω input with 8 pF is 200 kΩ at 100 kHz.
  • Bias current through a large resistor is an offset. A TL072's 65 pA into 10 MΩ is 0.65 mV of DC error — larger than the op-amp's own offset voltage. This is why the DC-errors lesson treats Vos, Ib and Ios together: which one dominates depends entirely on your source impedance.
  • Cascading multiplies the losses. Three stages each losing 9% do not lose 27%, they deliver 0.9093 = 75% — and the error compounds silently because every stage looks fine on its own.

Check yourself

1. A photodiode produces 10 µA into a transimpedance amplifier. Why does this amplifier want Zin → 0 when everything above argued for large input impedance?

Answer

Because the input is a current, not a voltage. The two cases are the same argument, not opposite ones: you want the source to see the load it prefers. A current source wants a short (all its current flows into you and none is lost to the diode's own shunt resistance); a voltage source wants an open. Row 3 of the table, derived rather than memorised.

2. A 600 Ω microphone drives an amplifier whose input impedance is 10 kΩ. How much signal is lost, and is that acceptable for audio?

Answer

Loss = 600/(600+10 000) = 5.7%, which is 0.5 dB. For audio that is inaudible and entirely normal — microphone inputs are deliberately built as “bridging” inputs at roughly 10× the source impedance rather than matched to it. Matching (10 k into 10 k) would throw away half the signal, 6 dB. Impedance matching is for power transfer and transmission lines, not for signal transfer — a distinction that costs people a lot of needlessly noisy front ends.
