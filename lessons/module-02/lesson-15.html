Interview Prep — Filters, TIAs, Instrumentation and Noise

This module is where op-amp knowledge turns into signal-chain knowledge, and interviews follow it there. The questions stop being "what is the gain" and start being "what limits this", which is a different skill and a harder one to fake.

Noise questions are the ones people fumble. Not because the mathematics is hard — it is one square root — but because you have to keep four different mechanisms straight and know which dominates where. Get the numbers below into your head before an interview and most noise questions become arithmetic.
Resistor thermal noise
4 nV/√Hz for 1 kΩ at 300 K
Scaling
×√R — 100 kΩ is 40 nV/√Hz
Noise bandwidth
1.57 × f−3dB for one pole
Shot noise
in = √(2qI) — 0.57 pA/√Hz at 1 µA

Part 1 — Derive it

1 Derive Standard

Sallen-Key low-pass. Where do \(f_0\) and \(Q\) come from, and what does \(Q\) physically do?

How to answer
\[ f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{R_1R_2C_1C_2}} \] The geometric mean of the two RC products. Nothing surprising.
\[ Q = \frac{\sqrt{R_1R_2C_1C_2}}{R_1C_1 + R_2C_2 + R_1C_2(1-K)} \] Where \(K\) is the amplifier's gain. The \((1-K)\) term is the interesting one: the amplifier is feeding energy BACK into the RC network through \(C_1\), and that positive feedback is what creates a Q above 0.5.
\[ \text{Unity-gain form } (K=1): \quad Q = \frac{\sqrt{R_1R_2C_1C_2}}{R_1C_1+R_2C_2} \]

"Q is how much the response peaks at \(f_0\) and how much it rings in time. Two poles of a passive RC cascade can only give \(Q = 0.5\); anything sharper needs an active element putting energy back. That is the entire reason the topology exists, and it is also why high-Q Sallen-Key sections are sensitive — a \(Q\) of 10 means component tolerances get multiplied by roughly 10, and the amplifier's own bandwidth starts to matter."

What they are testing: whether you understand Q as controlled positive feedback rather than as a number in a table. The follow-up is usually "so what happens at high Q?" and the answer is sensitivity — to components, and to the op-amp's finite GBW, which is why you cascade low-Q sections and put the highest-Q one last.
2 Derive Standard

Three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier. Derive the gain, and explain why its CMRR is so much better than a one-op-amp difference amplifier's.

How to answer
\[ A_{stage1} = 1 + \frac{2R_1}{R_G} \] Differential gain only. The two input followers force \(V_{RG} = V_1 - V_2\) across \(R_G\), so the current through it is differential.
\[ A_{stage1,\,CM} = 1 \] THE key result. A common-mode input puts no voltage across \(R_G\), so no current flows and both outputs move together with a gain of exactly one.
\[ A_{total} = \left(1+\frac{2R_1}{R_G}\right)\frac{R_3}{R_2} \]

"So the first stage amplifies the difference by, say, 100 while passing common mode at unity — the ratio has already improved by 100 before the difference amplifier does any rejecting at all. The output stage's resistor matching still limits things, but now it is limiting a signal that is already 40 dB better off. That is why an INA gets 100 dB of CMRR from the same 0.1% resistors that would give a bare difference amp about 54 dB."

What they are testing: the unity common-mode gain of the input stage. If you can say why no current flows in \(R_G\) for a common-mode input, you have understood the topology; if you only recite the gain formula, you have not.
3 Derive Harder

A 1 MΩ transimpedance amplifier, bandwidth 100 kHz. What is the output noise, and what dominates it?

How to answer
\[ e_{n,R_f} = \sqrt{4kTR_f} = 4\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}}\times\sqrt{1000} = 127\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}} \] Scale from the 1 kΩ number by \(\sqrt{R}\) — no calculator needed.
\[ \text{BW}_{noise} = 1.57 \times 100\,\mathrm{kHz} = 157\,\mathrm{kHz} \]
\[ V_{n,out} = 127\times10^{-9}\sqrt{157000} = 50\,\mu\mathrm{V\,RMS} \]
\[ i_{n,\,\text{equiv}} = \frac{50\,\mu\mathrm{V}}{1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega} = 50\,\mathrm{pA\ RMS} \]

"At this bandwidth the feedback resistor dominates, which is the good case — it is the irreducible floor for a given \(R_f\). But the op-amp's voltage noise is amplified by the noise gain, and the noise gain of a TIA rises with frequency because of the photodiode capacitance: \(1 + Z_f/Z_{in}\) where \(Z_{in}\) is the diode capacitance. So above the zero at \(1/2\pi R_f C_d\) the amplifier's \(e_n\) climbs and eventually takes over. Push the bandwidth up and \(e_n C_d\) becomes the dominant term, not the resistor."

What they are testing: that TIA noise is frequency-dependent in a way most noise problems are not. Saying "the resistor dominates" and stopping is a partial answer; naming the \(e_nC_d\) term and where it takes over is the complete one, and it connects straight to the stability question in module 3.
4 Derive Harder

Why is \(\sqrt{4kTR}\) the noise of a resistor but a capacitor is noiseless — and yet an RC filter has \(kT/C\) noise on it?

How to answer

Both statements are true and they are the same statement.

\[ \text{A capacitor is lossless, so it dissipates nothing and generates nothing.} \]
\[ v_n^2 = \int_0^\infty 4kTR\,\left|\frac{1}{1+j2\pi fRC}\right|^2 df = 4kTR\cdot\frac{1}{4RC} = \frac{kT}{C} \] The noise comes entirely from the RESISTOR. The capacitor only sets the bandwidth over which you integrate it.
\[ \text{R cancels: bigger R makes more noise density and less bandwidth, exactly offsetting.} \]
\[ C = 1\,\mathrm{pF}: \quad v_n = \sqrt{\frac{1.38\times10^{-23}\cdot300}{10^{-12}}} = 64\,\mu\mathrm{V\ RMS} \]

"Which is why sample-and-hold noise is quoted as \(kT/C\) and why the only way to reduce it is a bigger capacitor — the switch resistance genuinely does not matter. A 12-bit converter with a 1 V range has an LSB of 244 µV, so 64 µV of \(kT/C\) from a 1 pF hold capacitor is already a quarter of an LSB, and this is why sampling capacitors in precision converters are picofarads rather than femtofarads."

What they are testing: a conceptually clean question that catches people who have memorised formulas without understanding where noise comes from. "R cancels" is the punchline and it should be delivered as one.

Part 2 — Diagnose it

5 Trap Standard

An instrumentation amplifier with a gain of 1000 measuring a bridge. The output is railed. The bridge output is 2 mV. What is wrong?

How to answer

Almost certainly no return path for the input bias currents.

An INA's inputs need a DC path to its reference. A floating source — a bridge with no ground connection, a thermocouple, an isolated transformer winding — provides none, so the bias currents have nowhere to go and charge the inputs until they leave the common-mode range and the output slams to a rail.

"The fix is a pair of resistors from each input to the reference node, high enough not to load the source — 10 M for a bias current in the picoamps, 1 M or lower for nanoamps. It has to be BOTH inputs and they should match, because mismatched bias resistors turn matched bias currents into a differential offset. It is the single most common instrumentation amplifier support call there is, and it is the first thing I would check before suspecting the part."

What they are testing: practical experience. The mathematics is fine; the circuit does not work; only someone who has wired one up knows why. This exact failure has its own section in every INA datasheet.
6 Trap Harder

You cascade five identical unity-gain Sallen-Key low-pass sections, each with \(f_0 = 10\,\mathrm{kHz}\), expecting a sharp 10 kHz filter. What do you get?

How to answer

A much lower cutoff and a soggy response.

\[ |H| = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^5 = 0.177 \;\Rightarrow\; -15\,\mathrm{dB\ at\ } 10\,\mathrm{kHz} \] Each section is already 3 dB down at its own \(f_0\), and five of those stack.
\[ f_{-3\mathrm{dB,\ total}} \approx f_0\sqrt{2^{1/5}-1} = 0.386\,f_0 = 3.86\,\mathrm{kHz} \]

"Identical sections is the wrong construction. A real tenth-order filter has five sections with different \(f_0\) and \(Q\), taken from the Butterworth or Chebyshev pole positions — some sections peak, some roll off early, and the product is flat to the corner and then falls off a cliff. Cascading identical stages gives you the roll-off rate without the corner, which is the worst of both."

What they are testing: whether you know that filter tables exist and why. A candidate who thinks "more identical sections = sharper filter" has never designed one. Bonus: the highest-Q section goes LAST in the cascade, so its peaking does not overload the stages behind it.
7 Trap Nasty

A precision DC measurement drifts and wanders at about 0.1 Hz. The op-amp's broadband noise is 5 nV/√Hz, which should be nothing. What is going on and what do you do?

How to answer

Flicker noise, and possibly thermocouples.

\[ e_n(f) = e_{n,wb}\sqrt{1 + \frac{f_c}{f}} \] Below the corner frequency \(f_c\) the noise density rises as \(1/\sqrt{f}\). A 5 nV/√Hz part with a 10 Hz corner is 50 nV/√Hz at 0.1 Hz — ten times worse, and it keeps rising as you average longer.
\[ \text{0.1–10 Hz noise} \approx e_{n,wb}\sqrt{f_c\ln\!\frac{f_2}{f_1}} \] The band everyone quotes for precision parts, because it is where DC measurements live.

And check for thermocouples before blaming the amplifier. Every junction of dissimilar metals on the board is a thermocouple — solder to copper is about 3 µV/°C, kovar leads to copper can be 40 µV/°C. A draught across the board produces exactly this kind of slow wander and it is not noise at all.

"So: a chopper or auto-zero amplifier, which modulates the input up past the flicker corner, amplifies, and demodulates back down — leaving essentially no \(1/f\) at all. Then thermal symmetry in the layout so any remaining thermocouples come in pairs that cancel, and a lid over the sensitive nodes to stop air currents."

What they are testing: whether you know the broadband number is the wrong one for a DC measurement, and whether you consider non-electronic causes. The thermocouple answer is what marks someone who has chased a real drift problem rather than simulated one.
8 Rapid

Why do noise sources add in quadrature, and what does that mean practically?

Answer

They are uncorrelated, so powers add rather than amplitudes: \(e_{tot} = \sqrt{e_1^2 + e_2^2}\). Practically it means the largest source dominates hard and everything else is nearly free: 10 nV and 3 nV combine to 10.4 nV, so the 3 nV source contributed 4%. Chase the biggest contributor and ignore anything under about a third of it — that is how a noise budget is actually used.

9 Rapid

Where should the gain in a signal chain go, and why?

Answer

As early as possible. Everything after the first stage has its noise divided by the gain preceding it, so the first stage sets the noise figure of the whole chain — that is Friis's formula. The constraint pulling the other way is headroom and overload: too much gain up front and a large input clips before it reaches the filter that would have removed it.
