Noise: the Floor You Cannot Design Below

Every other limit in this course is a design choice you could revisit with a better part or more current. Noise is not. A resistor at room temperature generates a voltage across itself because its electrons are warm, and no amount of money changes that. What you get to choose is how much you add on top, and whether the thing you are measuring is above the floor at all.

This lesson is a budget, not a theory. The aim is that you can look at a schematic and say, within a factor of two and without a simulator, what its noise floor is and which component set it. That is a fifteen-minute skill and it is the difference between choosing parts and guessing at them.
Why noise adds the way it does

Noise sources are like people talking in a room, all saying different things at once. Two conversations at the same volume do not make one twice as loud — the sounds are uncorrelated, so the powers add and the amplitude only goes up by √2.

The practical consequence is the useful part. A source at a third of the dominant one adds 0.45 dB. At a fifth, 0.17 dB. So a noise budget is not a list of things to minimise — it is a search for the one or two that matter, after which everything else is free. Chase the loudest voice; the rest of the room is a rounding error.

Where it breaks: two correlated sources do add in amplitude, and that happens for real — the same interference coupling into two inputs, or one supply's noise reaching two stages. Quadrature is for uncorrelated sources only, and interference is not noise in this sense at all: it has a cause you can go and remove.

1. Where it comes from — three mechanisms

Thermal (Johnson–Nyquist): every resistor, always

\[ e_n = \sqrt{4kTR} \quad [\mathrm{V}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}] \] Charge carriers are in thermal motion, so a resistor has a fluctuating voltage across it even with no current flowing. It depends on temperature and resistance, and on nothing else — not the material, not the construction, not the price.
\[ \boxed{4\,\mathrm{nV}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}} \text{ for } 1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \text{ at room temperature}} \] The number to memorise. Everything else scales as \(\sqrt{R}\), so you never need the constants again.
\[ 100\,\Omega: 1.3 \qquad 10\,\mathrm{k}: 12.9 \qquad 100\,\mathrm{k}: 40.7 \qquad 1\,\mathrm{M}: 129 \]

Shot: current is made of electrons

\[ i_n = \sqrt{2qI} \quad [\mathrm{A}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}] \] Charge is discrete, so a DC current crossing a barrier arrives as countable events with Poisson statistics. It needs a barrier — a junction — so a plain resistor has thermal noise but not shot noise.
\[ 1\,\mu\mathrm{A}: 0.57\,\mathrm{pA}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}} \qquad 1\,\mathrm{mA}: 17.9 \] Note it goes as \(\sqrt I\): the noise current grows, but relative to the signal current it shrinks. More current is a better signal-to-noise ratio, which is why low-noise front ends burn current.

Flicker (1/f): the one that ruins DC measurements

\[ e_n(f) = e_{n,wb}\sqrt{1 + \frac{f_c}{f}} \] Below the corner frequency \(f_c\), noise density rises as \(1/\sqrt f\). Caused by carriers being trapped and released at surface states, so it depends on the process and the device — bipolar inputs have a corner of a few hertz, CMOS a few kilohertz.
\[ \text{Band-limited: } e_{rms} = e_{n,wb}\sqrt{f_c\ln\frac{f_2}{f_1}} \] The logarithm is why every decade contributes equally, and why "0.1 to 10 Hz noise" is the figure precision datasheets quote — averaging for longer does not help.
Which one you are fighting

High source impedance → thermal, from the source itself. Low impedance and wide band → the amplifier's voltage noise. Near DC → flicker, and the broadband number on the datasheet's front page is the wrong one. Photodiodes and anything with a bias current → shot.

2. The amplifier's two numbers, and the impedance that decides between them

A noise datasheet gives you \(e_n\) in nV/√Hz and \(i_n\) in pA/√Hz. They are not two ways of saying the same thing — which one hurts depends entirely on what is connected to the input.

\[ e_{total} = \sqrt{\underbrace{4kTR_s}_{\text{source}} + \underbrace{e_n^2}_{\text{voltage}} + \underbrace{(i_nR_s)^2}_{\text{current}}} \] Three terms. The current noise only becomes a voltage by flowing through the source resistance, which is why it is harmless at low impedance and fatal at high.
\[ R_{opt} = \frac{e_n}{i_n} \] Where the two amplifier terms are equal, and where that amplifier is at its best. Below it, voltage noise dominates; above it, current noise does.
"Low-noise op-amp" on its own is not a specification. A FET-input part with forty times less current noise is worse than a bipolar one by 12 dB at a 150 Ω source, and better by a mile at 80 kΩ. The first question in any noise design is always: what is the source impedance?

3. Noise gain is not signal gain

This is the idea the rest of the module keeps using, and it is worth stating on its own.

\[ \text{Noise gain} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1} \qquad\text{always} \] Whatever appears at the op-amp's own input — its \(e_n\), its offset — is amplified by this. It does not care which end of \(R_1\) the signal came in.
\[ \text{Non-inverting: signal gain} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_1} \quad \text{— the same} \]
\[ \text{Inverting: signal gain} = -\frac{R_f}{R_1} \quad \text{— NOT the same} \]
\[ \text{Inverting unity gain: signal} \times 1, \text{ noise} \times 2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \textbf{6 dB worse} \] An inverting buffer and a follower have the same signal gain and differ by 6 dB in output noise. If you do not need the inversion, that is 6 dB paid for nothing.

4. Bandwidth: noise is not band-limited where you think

\[ V_{rms} = e_n\sqrt{\mathrm{BW}_{noise}} \] Density times the square root of bandwidth. Halving the bandwidth buys you only \(\sqrt2\), which is why filtering is a weak lever compared with fixing the dominant source.
\[ \mathrm{BW}_{noise} = \frac{\pi}{2}f_{-3\mathrm{dB}} = 1.57\,f_{-3\mathrm{dB}} \quad\text{(one pole)} \] A single-pole filter keeps letting noise through above its corner, so the equivalent brick-wall bandwidth is 57% wider than the corner. Forgetting this understates the noise by 25%.
\[ \text{2 poles: } 1.11f \qquad \text{3: } 1.05f \qquad \text{4: } 1.03f \] A sharper filter converges on the corner frequency, which is one of the quieter arguments for a higher-order anti-alias filter.

A gain-of-10 inverting stage uses \(R_1 = 10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), \(R_f = 100\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). A colleague suggests 1 k and 10 k instead. Same gain — what does it buy?

  1. Nothing. The gain is a ratio, so the noise is the same.
    The gain is a ratio and the noise is not. Thermal noise depends on the resistance itself, not on the ratio.
  2. About 2.8× less input-referred noise
    Right, and it is worth being able to see why without computing. \(R_1\)'s thermal noise scales as \(\sqrt R\) and is amplified by the signal gain; the op-amp's current noise through \(R_f\) scales as \(R\) outright. Dropping both by ten takes the input-referred noise from 17.4 to 6.2 nV/√Hz. The cost is that the op-amp now has to drive 1 kΩ rather than 10 kΩ, and the source has to drive a 1 kΩ input impedance — which is exactly the trade, and the reason you do not simply keep going.
  3. Less noise, but at the cost of gain accuracy
    Accuracy is set by the resistor tolerance, which is a percentage and does not care about the absolute value.
  4. Nothing useful — the op-amp's \(e_n\) dominates either way
    Worth checking rather than assuming, and here it is false: at 10 k/100 k the op-amp's voltage noise is only 6% of the total power. \(R_1\) is 55% and the current noise through \(R_f\) is 33%. Set the interactive to those values and look.

Use the lowest resistor values the source and the op-amp's output can drive. That single habit is worth more than most part substitutions.

Read the two share columns against each other

Same circuit, same gain, same op-amp. In the left-hand design the amplifier and the resistors matter about equally, so a better op-amp would buy you something. In the right-hand one the op-amp contributes 6% of the noise power — buying a quieter part would change essentially nothing, and the money should go into lower resistor values instead.

That is what a noise budget is for. Not to compute a number, but to tell you which change is worth making.

6. Measuring it, and the mistakes that make it look wrong

  1. Short the input to ground through the source impedance you will actually use, not with a wire. A shorted input removes the source's own thermal noise and flatters the result.
  2. Measure at the output and divide by the gain. Trying to measure nanovolts directly is why the gain is there.
  3. Band-limit deliberately, and know the order of the filter you are measuring through — the 1.57 factor is a 25% error if you assume a brick wall.
  4. Use a true-RMS meter or the scope's RMS measurement over many acquisitions. Peak-to-peak on a scope is roughly 6–8× RMS for Gaussian noise and depends on how long you looked, so it is a poor number to quote.
  5. Check for interference before believing anything. Mains harmonics, switching supplies and nearby digital clocks are not noise: they have a cause, they are removable, and they will dominate a measurement while telling you nothing about the design.
If the measurement is far worse than the budget, it is almost never the noise theory. It is interference, a ground loop, or a measurement artefact. Look at the spectrum before touching the circuit: real noise is broad and featureless, interference has lines in it.

Build it in Circuit Toy

Circuit Toy has a Noise Source part, which makes this one of the few places you can watch a budget behave.

  1. Build the gain-of-10 inverting stage with 1 k and 10 k. Inject a noise source at the input and measure the output spectrum.
  2. Change to 10 k and 100 k. The gain is identical and the output noise is nearly three times larger — the checkpoint above, measured.
  3. Now build the same gain as a non-inverting stage and compare. Same signal gain, and for the inverting version the noise gain is 11 against 11 — equal here. Then do it at unity gain, where the inverting version's noise gain is 2 and the follower's is 1, and find the 6 dB.
  4. Open Op-Amp Error Sources alongside for the offset and bias-current terms, which sit on top of everything here.
