Interview Prep — Output Stages

Output stages are where an interview stops being about small signals and starts being about heat, survival and the things that happen at the edges of the operating range. The questions are practical almost without exception, and they are excellent at revealing whether someone has built hardware.

Every output stage question is one of three questions. How does it hand over between devices? Where does the heat go? And what happens when the load does something you did not plan for — a short, an inductive kick, a capacitive load? If you cannot immediately see which one is being asked, ask.

Part 1 — Derive and design

1 Derive Standard

Class A, B and AB. Derive the maximum theoretical efficiency of the first two and say where AB sits.

How to answer
\[ \text{Class A: } P_{dc} = V_{CC}I_Q \text{ always, } P_{out,max} = \frac{V_{CC}I_Q}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2} \] The device conducts for the whole cycle and draws its quiescent current whether there is a signal or not.
\[ \eta_{A,max} = 25\% \text{ (resistive load)}, \ 50\% \text{ (transformer or inductor loaded)} \]
\[ \text{Class B: } \eta_{max} = \frac{\pi}{4} = 78.5\% \] Each device conducts half the cycle and draws nothing at idle, so there is no standing loss to pay for.
\[ P_{diss,max}\big|_{class\ B} = \frac{2}{\pi^2}\cdot\frac{V_{CC}^2}{R_L} \approx 0.203\frac{V_{CC}^2}{R_L} \quad\text{at } V_{out} = \frac{2V_{CC}}{\pi} \] Worst-case dissipation is NOT at full output. It is at about 63% of full swing, and heatsinks must be sized for that point.

"Class AB is class B with a small idle current — typically enough to keep both devices barely conducting through the handover — so it is a few percent below class B in efficiency and enormously better in distortion. Essentially every linear output stage in production is class AB, and the design question is how small you can make that idle current before crossover distortion becomes audible or measurable."

What they are testing: the worst-case dissipation point. Almost everyone sizes a heatsink for full output; the correct answer is 63% of full swing, and being the person who knows that is memorable.
2 Derive Harder

Design the bias for a class AB stage. Why is a \(V_{BE}\) multiplier better than two diodes, and where does it have to be physically?

How to answer
\[ V_{bias} = V_{BE}\left(1 + \frac{R_1}{R_2}\right) \] A single transistor with a divider across its base-emitter. \(R_1/R_2\) is adjustable, so the bias voltage is adjustable — two diodes give you exactly 1.2 V and no say in it.
\[ \text{Need } \approx 2V_{BE} + 2I_QR_E \text{ across the two bases} \]

Why the multiplier wins on both counts:

  • Adjustable. Output devices are not the same as small-signal diodes; their \(V_{BE}\) at the idle current is whatever it is, and a trimmable multiplier lets you set the idle current instead of accepting it.
  • Trackable. The tempco scales with the multiplication factor, so you can match it to the output devices' \(-2\,\mathrm{mV/°C}\) times two rather than being stuck with a fixed \(-4\,\mathrm{mV/°C}\) from two diodes.

"And it has to be mounted on the output devices' heatsink, in thermal contact. If the bias reference stays cool while the output transistors heat up, their \(V_{BE}\) falls, the bias voltage does not follow, the idle current rises, the dissipation rises, and they get hotter still. That is thermal runaway and it destroys output stages within seconds. The emitter resistors — a fraction of an ohm — provide local degeneration that slows it down, but thermal coupling of the bias transistor is the actual fix."

What they are testing: the physical mounting requirement. It is not visible on a schematic and it is the difference between a design that works and one that fails in the field. Saying it unprompted is a strong signal.
3 Design Harder

Add short-circuit protection to that stage. Then tell me why simple current limiting is not enough.

How to answer
\[ I_{limit} = \frac{V_{BE}}{R_{sense}} = \frac{0.7}{0.22} = 3.2\,\mathrm{A} \] A sense resistor in the emitter with a transistor across it that steals base drive once the drop reaches a \(V_{BE}\). Two components.
\[ \text{But into a dead short: } P = V_{CC}\times I_{limit} = 40 \times 3.2 = 128\,\mathrm{W} \] The current is limited and the VOLTAGE across the device is now the full rail, so dissipation is far higher than at any normal operating point. The limiter has converted an overcurrent failure into a thermal one.

"That is why you use foldback: sense the output voltage as well as the current, and reduce the current limit as the output collapses. Into a short you might allow only 0.5 A instead of 3.2, so dissipation is 20 W rather than 128. The cost is that foldback can fail to start into a nonlinear load — a lamp, or a downstream converter with a big input capacitance — because the load looks like a short during start-up and the supply latches into the folded-back state. So you either add a timer, or use hiccup mode, which retries periodically and keeps the average dissipation low."

What they are testing: whether you know that a current limit does not limit power, and that foldback has a start-up failure mode of its own. Both directions of that trade being present in the answer is what makes it a design answer rather than a textbook one.

Part 2 — What goes wrong

4 Trap Harder

A class AB stage in a well-designed amplifier fails after twenty minutes at moderate power driving a loudspeaker. It survives a resistive load indefinitely at the same wattage. Why?

How to answer

The loudspeaker is reactive, and a reactive load moves the operating point outside the safe operating area even though the average power is unchanged.

With a resistive load, voltage and current are in phase: when the output is near a rail the current is high but \(V_{CE}\) is low, and dissipation is modest. With a reactive load they are not in phase, so the device can be carrying high current while also standing off most of the supply voltage. The instantaneous \(V\!\cdot\!I\) product is far higher and the load line becomes an ellipse that can pass outside the SOA curve.

"And for bipolar output devices the mechanism is secondary breakdown, which the SOA curve shows as a slope steeper than constant power at high \(V_{CE}\): current crowding into a hot spot that then draws more current locally. The protections are a proper SOA-shaped limiter rather than a fixed current limit, more devices in parallel with emitter ballasting, or MOSFET outputs, which have a positive tempco of \(R_{DS(on)}\) and no secondary breakdown — though they have a linear-mode SOA problem of their own."

What they are testing: reactive load lines and secondary breakdown. "Same average power, different instantaneous stress" is the sentence they want, and this question is a standard one for anyone interviewing on audio, motor drive or any inductive load.
5 Trap Harder

An H-bridge driving a motor destroys its high-side FET on deceleration. The current limit never tripped.

How to answer

Regenerative energy with nowhere to go.

A decelerating motor is a generator. Its kinetic energy comes back through the body diodes into the DC bus, and if nothing absorbs it the bus voltage rises until something breaks down. The current limit sees nothing wrong because the current is going the other way and is not excessive.

\[ E = \tfrac{1}{2}J\omega^2 \quad\text{(rotational)} \;\longrightarrow\; \Delta V_{bus} = \sqrt{\frac{2E}{C_{bus}}} \]
\[ E = 5\,\mathrm{J},\ C = 470\,\mu\mathrm{F}: \quad \Delta V = \sqrt{\frac{10}{470\mu}} = 146\,\mathrm{V} \] A 48 V bus becomes nearly 200 V. Every device on it is now outside its rating.

"So you need somewhere for that energy to go: a brake chopper — a transistor and a power resistor across the bus that switch in above a threshold — or enough bus capacitance to absorb it within the voltage rating, or a regenerative front end that returns it to the line. And I would check the inductive spike separately: at turn-off the motor's leakage inductance produces its own \(L\,di/dt\), which is a different and faster mechanism that the body diodes normally handle but which needs a low-inductance path to do so."

What they are testing: whether you think about energy flow in both directions. Motor drive interviews use this constantly, and "where does the energy go?" is the question to ask out loud.
6 Rapid

Why are the emitter resistors in a class AB output stage there, and why are they so small?

Answer

Local negative feedback on the idle current: if one device starts taking more, the drop across its emitter resistor rises and reduces its own \(V_{BE}\). That is what makes paralleled devices share and slows thermal runaway. They are 0.1–0.5 Ω because bigger values would waste output swing and dissipate real power at full current — 0.22 Ω at 3 A is 2 W — so you use the smallest value that still stabilises the bias.

7 Rapid

Why does a rail-to-rail output stage use common-emitter devices rather than followers?

Answer

A follower always loses a \(V_{BE}\), so it can never get closer than about 0.7 V to the rail. A common-emitter output can swing to within \(V_{CE(sat)}\) — a couple of hundred millivolts, and less at light loads. The price is that the output impedance is now high and load-dependent, so the loop gain changes with load, which makes rail-to-rail parts harder to stabilise and is why their datasheets specify performance against a particular load.

If you remember four things

Worst heat is at 63% swing
Not at full output. Size the heatsink there.
Bolt the bias to the heatsink
If it does not track the output devices, you get thermal runaway.
Current limiting does not limit power
Into a short, \(V_{CC} \times I_{limit}\) is worse than any normal condition. Foldback.
Reactive loads move the load line
Same average power, far higher instantaneous \(V\!\cdot\!I\). That is what SOA is for.
