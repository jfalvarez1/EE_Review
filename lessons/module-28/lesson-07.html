Design: A Headphone Amplifier

A small amplifier with a hard requirement: it has to drive anything plugged into it. Headphones run from 16 Ω in-ear monitors to 600 Ω studio cans — a 37:1 range — and the two ends want opposite things. Getting that right is most of the design; the gain is trivial.

1. What the two ends of the range actually demand

Headphone sensitivity is quoted two ways — dB per milliwatt for low-impedance models, dB per volt for high-impedance ones — and that difference is itself the clue.

Read the two right-hand columns together

The amplifier must be able to produce 5 V and, separately, 20 mA — but never both at once, because the load that needs the voltage does not need the current and vice versa. So the rails are set by the 250 Ω case and the output current by the 32 Ω case, and the worst point for power is somewhere in the middle.

That is why an op-amp with ±12 V rails and 30 mA of output is enough for essentially every headphone made, and why a headphone amplifier is a small circuit that people nevertheless get wrong.

Why the range is the problem

Driving headphones is like a tap that has to fill both a thimble and a bathtub, and you do not find out which is under it until someone connects one. A tap sized for the bathtub overwhelms the thimble; one sized for the thimble takes all day on the tub.

The resolution is that a voltage source does not care: it offers a pressure and lets each vessel take the flow it wants. That is the whole design goal — not "enough power" but low enough output impedance that the amplifier behaves like a voltage source to anything you connect.

Where it breaks: a real headphone is not a fixed vessel — its impedance changes with frequency, which is what makes output impedance matter in the specific way section 2 describes. A thimble that changed size depending on the note being played would make the analogy work harder than it can.

2. Output impedance: the specification that is usually wrong

A headphone's impedance is not flat. A dynamic driver peaks at its resonance; a multi-driver IEM with a passive crossover can swing 4:1 across the audio band. Put that variable impedance in series with a non-zero output impedance and you have a voltage divider whose ratio changes with frequency — which is a tone control you did not ask for.

\[ \frac{V_{load}}{V_{amp}} = \frac{Z_{load}(f)}{Z_{load}(f) + Z_{out}} \]
\[ \text{Response error} = 20\log_{10}\frac{Z_{max}/(Z_{max}+Z_{out})}{Z_{min}/(Z_{min}+Z_{out})} \] The difference between the loudest and quietest points of the divider, in dB. Zero only when \(Z_{out}\) is zero.

Where the impedance comes from is the interesting part. An op-amp inside a feedback loop has an output impedance of milliohms, so the amplifier itself is almost never the problem. The problem is what is outside the loop: the series resistor added to keep the op-amp stable into cable capacitance, the current-limit resistor, the jack, and the switch inside it. A design with a 10 Ω series resistor "for protection" has thrown away the very specification that mattered, and that resistor is why so many portable devices measure badly here.

A portable player sounds bass-heavy with one pair of in-ear monitors and neutral with another. The same file, the same volume. What is the most likely cause?

  1. The first pair is simply voiced with more bass
    Possible, and it is the answer people reach for. But it does not explain why the same IEMs sound neutral from a different source, which is the observation that usually comes next.
  2. The player's output impedance interacting with a non-flat load impedance
    Right. A multi-driver IEM with a passive crossover can swing 4:1 in impedance, peaking at the bass driver's resonance. Feed it from a 10 Ω output and the divider delivers nearly 3 dB more where the impedance is high — which is exactly where the bass is. A single-driver IEM with a flatter impedance is barely affected by the same source, which is why one pair changes and the other does not. The fix is at the amplifier: get \(Z_{out}\) below about an eighth of the minimum load.
  3. The player is clipping on bass transients
    Clipping would be level-dependent and would sound like distortion rather than tonal balance. And an IEM needs a fraction of a volt, so a player capable of driving anything is not close to clipping.
  4. Insufficient output current for the low impedance
    A 16 Ω IEM at 110 dB needs about 14 mA, which any headphone output can supply. Running out of current would also be level-dependent, and this is not.

Set the output impedance slider to 10 Ω and watch the error figure. That number is a frequency-response deviation, not a subtlety, and it is why output impedance belongs in a headphone amplifier's specification alongside power.

3. The circuit, and where the current comes from

The whole design is an op-amp with enough output current, and every decision is about what to do when that is not true.

ApproachUse whenCost
Op-amp alone The op-amp can supply the current at the lowest load you support. Many can do 30–40 mA, which covers everything. None. This is the right answer far more often than the hobbyist literature suggests.
Op-amp + buffer inside the loop You want more current, or you want the op-amp chosen for noise rather than drive. The buffer's delay is inside the loop, so it eats phase margin. Use a fast one.
Paralleled op-amps Cheap extra current from parts you already have. Each needs a small series resistor to stop them fighting over offset — and those resistors are outside the loop, so they add directly to \(Z_{out}\).
Discrete class AB output Amps of output, or a specific sonic intent. All of module 7's bias, thermal and protection problems, for a load that needs 100 mW.
\[ \text{Gain} = \frac{5\,\mathrm{V}}{2\,\mathrm{V}} = 2.5 \;\rightarrow\; \text{use } 3 \] Enough to reach full output from line level with a little margin, and no more — every dB of unnecessary gain is a dB of extra noise and a volume control living at the bottom of its travel.
\[ \text{Non-inverting, so the source is not loaded} \]
\[ R_{series} = 0 \text{ to } 1\,\Omega, \text{ INSIDE the loop if possible} \] If a series resistor is needed for stability into cable capacitance, take the feedback from AFTER it at DC and before it at high frequency — the standard isolation network. That keeps \(Z_{out}\) low where it matters and stability where it matters.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse Jack inserted while playing The plug's sleeve shorts tip to ring to sleeve in turn as it slides home. The output is momentarily shorted to ground, and momentarily driving both channels into each other. The op-amp's own short-circuit protection handles it, and this is the reason that protection exists. The real casualty is the listener if the headphone is already on their head — which is why a well-designed amplifier mutes on jack detect, and why doing so is a safety feature rather than a convenience.
Abuse Output shorted continuously The op-amp current-limits at perhaps 40 mA with the full rail across it: \(12 \times 0.04 = \mathbf{0.5\ W}\) in a small package rated for maybe 0.6 W. It gets hot and thermal-shutdowns, then oscillates in and out of shutdown. Survivable, ugly, and it is the one place a small series resistor earns its keep — but keep it inside the feedback loop so it does not become \(Z_{out}\).
Abuse A 16 Ω IEM at full volume after a 600 Ω setting The volume control is where it was left. 5 V into 16 Ω is 1.6 W — about 135 dB SPL, if the driver survives that long. Nothing electrical fails. The listener is injured and the driver may be destroyed, and this is the most serious failure in this lesson. A gain switch, a mute on jack insertion, or simply not providing more gain than the highest-impedance load needs. Excess gain is not headroom here; it is a hazard.
Abuse Long cable, high capacitance A few nanofarads across the op-amp's output, inside the feedback loop. The output impedance and that capacitance form a pole that costs phase margin. Ringing or oscillation — the follower problem from module 1, arriving in a product where long cables are normal. The isolation network in section 3 is the fix, and it is why that network is drawn the way it is rather than as a plain series resistor.
The gain row is the one to take seriously. Every other abuse case in this course ends with a component failing. This one ends with hearing damage, and the mechanism is nothing more exotic than a volume control left where the previous headphone needed it. Designing for the highest-impedance load and no further is a safety decision, not a performance compromise.

Build it in Circuit Toy

  1. Build a non-inverting stage of gain 3 from Non-Inv Amp, load it with 32 Ω, and confirm it holds its output.
  2. Add a series resistor between the op-amp and the load, and sweep it from 0 to 32 Ω while measuring the voltage at the load. That divider is the output-impedance problem, and the table in section 2 falls straight out of it.
  3. Now make the load frequency-dependent — an inductor in series, or an LC to mimic a driver resonance — and run an AC sweep at each series resistance. The response error appears as a real bump rather than a number.
  4. Put 5 nF across the output and watch the step response ring. Then move the feedback take-off to before the series resistor and watch it settle, which is the isolation network doing its job.
