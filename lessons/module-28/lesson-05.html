Design: A 50 W Class AB Power Amplifier

A complete channel, from the brief to a heatsink specification. This is the longest design in the course because a power amplifier is where every earlier module has to hold at once: a differential pair, a Miller-compensated VAS, an output stage with a thermal feedback loop, and a stability argument that decides whether the whole thing sings or oscillates.

The order the decisions have to be made in. Each one constrains the next, and doing them out of order means going round again. Rails come from the output power and the load. Devices and heatsink come from the rails. Bias comes from the output devices. Front end comes from the required loop gain and the stability limit. Protection comes last, and is designed against the failures rather than the specification.

1. Rails, from the output power

\[ \hat{V}_{out} = \sqrt{2PR_L} = \sqrt{2(50)(8)} = 28.3\,\mathrm{V} \]
\[ \hat{I}_{out} = \frac{28.3}{8} = 3.54\,\mathrm{A} \]
\[ V_{CC} = \hat{V}_{out} + V_{drop} \approx 28.3 + 4 = 32.3 \;\rightarrow\; \pm 35\,\mathrm{V} \] The 4 V covers two \(V_{BE}\) drops through the driver and output devices, the emitter resistor, and enough \(V_{CE}\) to keep the output transistor out of saturation. Round up, because an unregulated supply sags under load and the rated power must be available at the sagged voltage.
\[ \text{Available: } \frac{(35-4)^2}{2(8)} = 60\,\mathrm{W} \quad \text{— 20\% margin} \]
Into 4 Ω the same amplifier tries to make 120 W, which needs 7.75 A peak and doubles the device dissipation. That is why "50 W into 8 Ω" and "survives a 4 Ω load" are two separate specifications, and why the protection circuit in section 5 exists.

2. Output devices and the heatsink

\[ P_{diss,max} = \frac{2V_{CC}^2}{\pi^2R_L} = \frac{2(35)^2}{\pi^2(8)} = 31.0\,\mathrm{W} \] Total, at 64% of full swing — not at full output. Sizing here is the difference between an amplifier that survives a test tone and one that does not.
\[ 15.5\,\mathrm{W} \text{ per device} \]
\[ \theta_{JA} \leq \frac{T_{J,max} - T_a}{P} = \frac{150 - 50}{15.5} = 6.45\,\mathrm{°C/W} \]
\[ \theta_{SA} \leq 6.45 - \underbrace{1.0}_{\theta_{JC}} - \underbrace{0.5}_{\theta_{CS}} = 4.95\,\mathrm{°C/W\ per\ device} \] \(\theta_{JC}\) from the datasheet for a TO-247; \(\theta_{CS}\) is the mica washer and thermal compound, and it is not negligible.
\[ \text{Both devices on one heatsink: } \theta_{SA} \leq 2.5\,\mathrm{°C/W} \] They share it, so the sink must be twice as good. A 2.5 °C/W extrusion is a substantial piece of aluminium — roughly 150 mm of a medium profile — and this number is what decides the size of the product.

Device selection. \(V_{CEO}\) must exceed the rail-to-rail 70 V with margin, so a 100 V or 150 V part. \(I_C\) must exceed 3.54 A peak, and into 4 Ω nearly 8 A, so a 15 A device. And the specification that matters most is the SOA curve: at 35 V across the device and 3.5 A through it, the operating point must sit inside the second-breakdown region of the curve, not merely inside the constant-power hyperbola.

3. Bias: the number with a genuine optimum

Quiescent current in a class AB stage is not "as little as you can get away with". Too little gives crossover distortion; too much gives a different distortion as the two devices both conduct and their transconductances add. There is a distinct minimum in between.

\[ \text{Emitter resistors } R_E = 0.22\,\Omega \text{ per device} \] Local feedback that stabilises the bias against temperature and lets paralleled devices share. Larger wastes output swing; smaller stops stabilising.
\[ \text{Optimum: } \approx 11\,\mathrm{mV} \text{ across each } R_E \;\Longrightarrow\; I_q = \frac{0.011}{0.22} = 50\,\mathrm{mA} \] The classic result for a complementary emitter-follower output stage. It is a genuine minimum in the distortion-versus-bias curve, and it moves very little with device type — which is why 50 mA per device appears in so many designs that look otherwise unrelated.
\[ P_{idle} = 2V_{CC}I_q = 2(35)(0.05) = 3.5\,\mathrm{W} \] The price of class AB over class B: three and a half watts to produce silence. Against class A's 153 W for the same rails, it is a bargain.
\[ V_{spreader} = 4V_{BE} + 2I_qR_E = 2.6 + 0.022 = 2.62\,\mathrm{V} \] Four junctions for a driver-plus-output (EF2) stage. This is what the \(V_{BE}\) multiplier must produce.
The mounting requirement that is not on the schematic

The \(V_{BE}\) multiplier transistor must be bolted to the heatsink, in contact with the output devices. If it stays cool while they heat up, their \(V_{BE}\) falls, the spreader voltage does not follow, the quiescent current rises, the dissipation rises, and they get hotter. That is thermal runaway, and it destroys output stages in seconds.

The emitter resistors slow it down — they are local negative feedback on \(I_q\) — but thermal coupling of the bias reference is the actual fix. A schematic cannot show it, so it belongs in the assembly drawing and in the design review.

4. The front end, and where the bandwidth comes from

Three stages: a degenerated differential pair, a Miller-compensated voltage amplifier, and the output stage already designed. The two numbers that come out of it are the loop crossover frequency and the slew rate, and they are set by different things — which is the useful part.

\[ \text{Closed-loop gain } A_{cl} = 1 + \frac{R_f}{R_g} = 1 + \frac{21\mathrm{k}}{1\mathrm{k}} = 22 \] Gives full output from 0.91 V RMS in, which is +1.4 dBu — comfortably driven by any line-level source.
\[ r_e = \frac{V_T}{I_{tail}/2} = \frac{26\,\mathrm{mV}}{2\,\mathrm{mA}} = 13\,\Omega \]
\[ G_m = \frac{1}{r_e + R_{degen}} \] Degeneration resistors in the input pair's emitters. This is the design knob.
\[ f_{crossover} = \frac{G_m}{2\pi A_{cl}C_{dom}} \]
\[ \mathrm{SR} = \frac{I_{tail}}{C_{dom}} = \frac{4\,\mathrm{mA}}{100\,\mathrm{pF}} = 40\,\mathrm{V/\mu s} \] Slew rate is set by the tail current and the compensation capacitor — and NOT by the degeneration.

Why the undegenerated version is unusable

A 5.6 MHz loop crossover sounds like a fast amplifier and is in fact a broken one. The output stage — a driver and an output device, both with their own capacitances, driving a load that may be a capacitive cable — has poles in the low megahertz. Cross over up there and those poles are inside the loop at unity gain, phase margin disappears, and the amplifier oscillates.

Degeneration fixes it, and note the last column: slew rate is unchanged throughout. Degeneration lowers the small-signal transconductance without touching the maximum current the tail can deliver, so it improves the slew rate relative to the bandwidth. An undegenerated pair reaches its slewing limit at a much lower input level, which is one of the classic causes of amplifiers that measure well on a sine and sound poor on music.

Your 50 W design is finished. Marketing asks for 100 W into the same 8 Ω. What is the largest consequence?

  1. The output devices need twice the current rating
    Peak current only rises by √2, from 3.54 A to 5.0 A, because power goes as the square of voltage. Real, and not the biggest problem.
  2. The heatsink has to be twice as good, because dissipation scales with \(V_{CC}^2\)
    Right, and it is worse than it sounds. Doubling the power needs √2 times the rails, and \(P_{diss} = 2V_{CC}^2/\pi^2R_L\) scales with the square of that — so dissipation doubles to 62 W while the allowed thermal resistance halves to about 1.2 °C/W. That is the difference between an extrusion on the back panel and forced air or a much larger chassis, and it usually decides the product's size and price. It is also why designers reach for a second output pair here: two pairs share the dissipation and each sees half.
  3. The compensation must be redesigned
    The front end barely notices. Loop crossover and slew rate are set by \(G_m\), \(C_{dom}\) and the closed-loop gain, none of which changed.
  4. The slew rate requirement doubles
    It rises by √2 with the peak voltage, from 3.55 to 5.0 V/µs — against 40 V/µs available. Not close to binding.

Set the calculator to 100 W and watch the heatsink number, then add a second output pair and watch it become tractable again. That sequence is how the decision is actually made.

5. Protection, designed against the failures

Four things go wrong with power amplifiers, and each needs its own answer. None of them appears in the performance specification.

FailureWhat happensThe answer
Output short Current limited only by \(R_E\) and the device; dissipation becomes \(V_{CC} \times I\), far above any normal operating point A VI limiter that senses both the voltage across the device and the current through it, so the limit follows the SOA curve rather than a fixed current
Reactive load Voltage and current out of phase, so the load line becomes an ellipse that can leave the SOA at the same average power The same VI limiter, plus honest SOA margin. A fixed current limit does not protect against this.
DC on the output A failed output device puts a rail across the speaker and destroys the voice coil in seconds A DC detector driving a relay that disconnects the speaker, with a few hundred milliseconds of delay so music does not trip it
Switch-on thump Rails come up at different rates and the output slams to one of them The same relay, held open for a second or two after power-up
The VI limiter has a cost worth naming. It sits in the signal path and, if it engages on a difficult but legitimate load, it produces distortion far worse than anything the amplifier does normally. Set it too tight and the amplifier sounds bad on real speakers; too loose and it does not protect. This is the single most argued-about circuit in power amplifier design, and the reason some high-end designs omit it and use large SOA margin and fuses instead.

6. Build it in Circuit Toy

Build it in pieces, in the order it was designed. Each stage is a gallery circuit already.

  1. Open Complementary push-pull output stage. Drive it with a 1 kHz sine at a few hundred millivolts and find the crossover notch. Then add the bias spreader and watch it close.
  2. Sweep the bias from zero upward and plot distortion. You should find a minimum, not a monotonic improvement — that is the optimum in section 3, measured.
  3. Open Differential Pair and add emitter degeneration. Measure \(G_m\) by driving a small differential signal and reading the output current, and confirm \(G_m = 1/(r_e+R_E)\).
  4. Open Two-Stage CE Amplifier and add a Miller capacitor between the VAS collector and base. Watch the open-loop response tilt to a single −20 dB/decade slope, which is what compensation is for.
  5. Then Loop Stability & Phase Margin to see the same thing as a phase margin number, and The same stage with and without compensation for the comparison side by side.

Also relevant: Darlington Follower for the EF2 output topology, and Gain-bandwidth and slew rate against infinity, which is the interaction in section 4 made visible.

Abuse — what happens when someone does the wrong thing

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse Output shorted at full drive The output tries to source \(V_{CC}/R_E\) = 35/0.22 = 159 A. It will not get there, but current is limited only by the device's own capability while the full rail sits across it — instantaneous dissipation in the low kilowatts. VI limiter engages within microseconds and holds the operating point inside the SOA. Without it the output device fails by second breakdown in under a millisecond — far faster than any fuse.
Abuse 4 Ω load when rated for 8 Peak current doubles to 7.1 A; worst-case dissipation doubles to 62 W. The junction rises to \(50 + 31 \times 6.45 = 250\,\mathrm{°C}\) with the original heatsink. Either the heatsink is sized for 4 Ω from the start, or a thermal cut-out mutes the amplifier before the junction reaches 150 °C. Silently overheating is not an acceptable outcome.
Abuse Input driven to 10 V when it expects 1 The amplifier clips hard and the feedback loop cannot hold \(V_- = V_+\). The full input then appears across the input pair's base-emitter junctions, and a reverse-biased B-E junction avalanches at about 6–7 V. Back-to-back diodes across the input pair, or a series input resistor that limits the avalanche current to under a milliamp. Without them, avalanching permanently degrades \(\beta\) and the noise figure — the amplifier still works and is quietly worse forever, which is the most insidious failure of the set.
Abuse Speaker disconnected while playing Nothing. A voltage amplifier is happy into an open circuit — dissipation falls to the quiescent value. No action needed. Worth stating explicitly, because the intuition that "no load is dangerous" comes from valve amplifiers, where an output transformer with no load really does arc over.
Abuse One rail fails (fuse, rectifier, or dry joint) The output slams to the surviving rail. The speaker sees 35 V DC across 8 Ω = 4.4 A, which is 153 W into a voice coil rated for a few watts of continuous dissipation. The DC-detect relay opens within a few hundred milliseconds. Without it the voice coil burns out, and the customer replaces a speaker rather than a fuse.
Abuse Power cycled rapidly Rails come up before the protection has released and before the input source is settled; the output can slam to a rail for tens of milliseconds. The mute relay stays open until the rails are up and the DC detector is happy. A thump is a symptom of the relay timing, not of the amplifier.
Abuse Driven into clipping continuously A clipped sine has roughly 1.5× the RMS of an unclipped one at the same peak, so speaker dissipation rises sharply — and the harmonics land in the tweeter. The amplifier survives; the speaker may not. This is the most common way amplifiers destroy loudspeakers, and it is why an undersized amplifier is more dangerous to a speaker than an oversized one.
The pattern worth extracting. In five of those seven rows the amplifier is fine and something else dies — the speaker, the input transistor's \(\beta\), the customer's confidence. A protection circuit is not there to protect the amplifier from the world. It is mostly there to protect the world from the amplifier, and designing it requires asking what is downstream, not what is inside.
