Design: A Class D Amplifier

The same 50 W into 8 Ω as the class AB design, built the other way. The output devices are never in between — they are on or off — so the 31 W the linear amplifier turned into heat becomes about 1.8 W. What you buy that with is an inductor, a filter, and an EMI problem.

Why switching wastes nothing

A linear amplifier is a tap you hold half open: the water you do not pass still pushes against the valve, and that struggle is the heat. A class D amplifier opens the tap fully, then shuts it fully, very fast, and controls the average by how long it spends open.

A valve that is fully open has no pressure across it. A valve that is fully shut passes no flow. Either way the product of pressure and flow — which is the power it wastes — is nearly zero. The waste is only in the moments of transition, which is why switching loss exists and why it scales with how often you switch rather than with how loud the music is.

Where it breaks: the picture says nothing about why you then need a filter. Water has inertia and averages itself; voltage does not, so the chopped output has to be averaged deliberately by an LC, and that filter is most of the design work this lesson is about.

1. The three blocks

BlockDoesThe decision in it
Modulator Turns the audio into a duty cycle. A comparator against a triangle carrier: where the audio sits relative to the ramp sets the pulse width. Switching frequency, and whether the loop closes before or after the output filter.
Power stage A half bridge or a full bridge of FETs, driven with dead time between them. Half versus full bridge, and how little dead time you dare use.
Output filter An LC that passes audio and removes the carrier. Cut-off frequency and Q — and the fact that the load is part of the filter.
\[ D = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{v_{audio}}{V_{tri}}\right) \] Natural-sampling PWM. The average of the switched output over one carrier period is linear in the input, which is the whole reason this works at all.
\[ \bar{v}_{out} = (2D - 1)V_{CC} = V_{CC}\frac{v_{audio}}{V_{tri}} \] Exactly proportional. The distortion comes not from the modulation but from everything that makes the real pulse differ from the commanded one — dead time, rise times, rail sag.

2. Half bridge or full bridge

\[ \hat{V}_{out} = \sqrt{2PR_L} = \sqrt{2(50)(8)} = 28.3\,\mathrm{V}, \quad \hat{I} = 3.54\,\mathrm{A} \]
\[ \text{Half bridge: } \pm 35\,\mathrm{V} \text{ rails, one FET pair} \] Needs a split supply, and the output has a DC component that must be blocked or centred.
\[ \text{Full bridge (BTL): } +35\,\mathrm{V} \text{ single rail, two FET pairs} \] The load sees the difference between two half-bridges swinging in opposite directions, so the swing DOUBLES for the same rail — which is FOUR TIMES the power. That is why anything battery-powered is bridged.
The bridge also cancels the common mode. Both halves switch at the same instant, so the common-mode step appears on both terminals and the load sees only the difference. That helps EMI a great deal — and it is why a bridged output must never have one side grounded, which is the commonest way people destroy a class D amplifier by connecting it like a class AB one.

3. The output filter, and the fact that the speaker is part of it

\[ H(s) = \frac{1}{1 + sL/R_L + s^2LC} \] A series inductor and shunt capacitor, damped by the load. Note where \(R_L\) appears — in the damping term only.
\[ f_c = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}, \qquad Q = \frac{R_L}{2\pi f_c L} = R_L\sqrt{\frac{C}{L}} \]
\[ f_c = 40\,\mathrm{kHz},\ Q = 0.707,\ R_L = 8\,\Omega \;\Longrightarrow\; L = 45\,\mu\mathrm{H},\ C = 352\,\mathrm{nF} \]
\[ \text{Standard values } 47\,\mu\mathrm{H} \text{ and } 330\,\mathrm{nF}: \quad f_c = 40.4\,\mathrm{kHz},\ Q = 0.67 \]
Now change the speaker

\(Q\) is proportional to \(R_L\), and a loudspeaker's impedance is not 8 Ω — it is a nominal figure that varies by 4:1 across the band, rising at resonance and again at high frequency.

LoadQWhat the response does
4 Ω0.34Overdamped: the response droops before the corner
8 Ω0.67What it was designed for
16 Ω1.34Peaks — several dB of lift near 40 kHz

So a class D amplifier's frequency response depends on what you plug into it, in a way a class AB one's does not. That is why datasheets specify performance into a stated load, why some designs add a Zobel network to present a constant impedance to the filter, and why a no-load condition — \(Q \to \infty\) — can ring hard enough to matter.

\[ \text{Attenuation at } f_{sw}: \; 40\log_{10}\frac{f_{sw}}{f_c}\ \mathrm{dB} \] Two poles, so 40 dB per decade.
\[ 200\,\mathrm{kHz}: 28\,\mathrm{dB} \qquad 400\,\mathrm{kHz}: 40\,\mathrm{dB} \qquad 800\,\mathrm{kHz}: 52\,\mathrm{dB} \] Switching faster buys filtering — and costs switching loss and EMI in exactly the band that is hardest to pass. That trade is the central decision of the design.

Your class D amplifier measures 0.02% THD at 1 W and 0.5% at 100 mW. Distortion getting worse at low level — what is it?

  1. The output filter's Q is wrong
    Filter Q shapes the frequency response and does not depend on level at all. A level-dependent defect needs a level-dependent mechanism.
  2. Crossover distortion, as in a class AB stage
    The right instinct — worse at small signals is the signature of a handover problem — but a class D output stage has no linear region and no crossover in the class AB sense. Both devices are always fully on or fully off.
  3. Dead time: a fixed timing error is a bigger fraction of a small pulse
    Right. During the dead time neither FET drives, and the output is set by which way the inductor current happens to be flowing — so the delivered pulse differs from the commanded one by roughly the dead time, and the error flips sign at the current zero crossing. That is a fixed absolute error, so at 100 mW it is a far larger fraction of the output. Class D's version of crossover distortion, arriving by a completely different route and with the same signature. Cut the dead time to the minimum the FETs allow, or close the feedback loop after the filter so the loop corrects it.
  4. The supply is sagging at low output
    Backwards: a supply sags at HIGH output, not low. Rail sag is a real class D distortion mechanism and it gets worse as you turn it up.

Move the dead-time slider above and watch the distortion figure. Then note the trade: too little dead time and both FETs conduct at once, which is a short across the rail.

4. Losses, against the linear amplifier

\[ P_{cond} = 2I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)} = 2(2.50)^2(0.05) = 0.63\,\mathrm{W} \]
\[ P_{sw} = \tfrac{1}{2}V_{CC}\hat{I}(t_r+t_f)f_{sw} = 0.99\,\mathrm{W} \]
\[ P_{gate} = 2Q_gV_{gs}f_{sw} = 0.19\,\mathrm{W} \]
\[ \text{Total } 1.8\,\mathrm{W} \;\Longrightarrow\; \eta = 96.5\% \]
\[ \text{Class AB at the same output: } \frac{2V_{CC}^2}{\pi^2R_L} = 31\,\mathrm{W} \] Seventeen times the heat. That is the entire argument, and it is why a 500 W class D amplifier fits in a box a 50 W class AB one needs.
Notice which loss scales with what. Conduction follows the signal; switching and gate losses follow the frequency and are there whether or not anyone is listening. So a class D amplifier has a standing idle loss a class AB one does not have at zero bias — small, but it is why battery-powered designs drop the switching frequency or go into a burst mode at low levels.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse One output terminal grounded on a bridged amplifier That half-bridge is now switching into a short. Current is limited only by \(R_{DS(on)}\) and the inductor: \(35/0.05 = \mathbf{700\ A}\) if nothing stops it, rising for the whole on-time. Cycle-by-cycle current limit must catch it within one switching period. Without it the FETs fail in microseconds. This is the single most common way a bridged amplifier is destroyed, because it is exactly how you would legitimately wire a class AB one.
Abuse Speaker disconnected while playing Unlike class AB, this is not harmless. \(Q = R_L\sqrt{C/L}\) goes to infinity with no load, so the LC rings hard at 40 kHz with nothing damping it. The inductor and capacitor see far more voltage than at rated load. The Zobel network — a resistor and capacitor across the output — provides damping at high frequency regardless of the load. Without one the filter capacitor can be overvolted, and the ultrasonic ringing radiates. Note this is the opposite of the class AB answer, where no load was completely safe.
Abuse Dead time set to zero Both FETs conduct together during the transition — a direct short across the rail through two devices, once per switching edge, 800,000 times a second. Nothing acceptable happens. Shoot-through destroys the bridge in milliseconds, and the failure is thermal so it looks like an overload rather than a timing fault. Dead time is the one parameter in this design where the safe direction and the low-distortion direction are opposite, which is why it is set once, carefully, and left alone.
Abuse A long speaker cable The cable adds capacitance across the filter output and inductance in series with the load. Both move the filter's corner and Q, and the cable becomes an antenna driven by whatever carrier residue survives. Usually fine for audio, and it is the EMI that fails first — a cable radiating at 400 kHz and its harmonics is the classic class D compliance failure. Ferrites at the output terminals, and enough filtering that the residue is small before it reaches the cable.
Two of these invert the class AB answers. No load was perfectly safe for a linear amplifier and is a hazard here; a grounded output terminal is normal there and destructive here. Carrying intuition across from one topology to the other is how experienced people break class D amplifiers, and it is worth saying explicitly rather than assuming the reader will notice.

6. Build it in Circuit Toy

Start from Buck Converter — a class D half bridge is a synchronous buck whose duty cycle is modulated by audio rather than held constant by a regulator. That equivalence is worth seeing directly.

  1. Open Buck Converter and replace the fixed PWM with a PWM Source whose duty is swept. Watch the output follow the duty cycle linearly — that is the modulator equation in section 1.
  2. Set the LC to 47 µH and 330 nF and load it with 8 Ω. Run an AC sweep and confirm the corner near 40 kHz.
  3. Change the load to 16 Ω and re-run. The peak that appears is \(Q\) rising to 1.34 — the load-dependence in section 3, measured rather than asserted.
  4. Then remove the load entirely and watch it ring. Add a Zobel — 10 Ω in series with 100 nF across the output — and watch the ringing damp with the audio response unchanged.
  5. Finally open Bootstrap High-Side Drive for the gate drive this needs, and CCM vs DCM for what the inductor does at low output.

What to carry forward

Loss follows frequency, not volume
Switching and gate losses are there at idle. Conduction is the only one that follows the signal.
The speaker is part of the filter
\(Q = R_L\sqrt{C/L}\). Response depends on the load in a way a linear amp's does not.
Bridging is 4× the power
Double the swing from the same rail — and never ground one side.
Dead time is crossover distortion
A fixed timing error, so worst at small signals. Same signature, different mechanism.
Faster switching buys filtering
40 dB/decade of separation, paid for in switching loss and EMI.
No load is now dangerous
Opposite of class AB. \(Q\) runs away and the filter rings. Fit a Zobel.
