Design: A Microphone Preamplifier

The hardest amplifier in an audio system, and the one where the design procedure is most explicitly a budget. You are not choosing a topology from taste — you are working out how close to the physics you can get, and what each component costs you against that floor.

1. The floor, before you have chosen anything

Start with what is impossible. The microphone's own source resistance generates thermal noise, and no amplifier can do better than pass it through.

\[ e_n = \sqrt{4kTR} = 4.07\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}}\times\sqrt{R/1\mathrm{k}} \] Scale from the 1 kΩ number; a 150 Ω source is \(\sqrt{0.15}\) of it. The figure everyone quotes is 4 nV/√Hz, and that is the 290 K value; at 300 K it is 4.07. The 2% matters only because the rest of this page carries three figures.
\[ e_n(150\,\Omega) = 4.07 \times 0.387 = 1.58\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}} \]
\[ V_n = 1.58\,\mathrm{nV}\times\sqrt{20\,000} = 223\,\mathrm{nV\ RMS} \]
\[ 20\log_{10}\frac{223\times10^{-9}}{0.7746} = \boxed{-130.8\ \mathrm{dBu}} \] The theoretical floor. A preamp quoting −128 dBu is within 3 dB of physics, which is why that number appears on so many datasheets — there is nowhere else to go.
What "noise figure" is measuring

Noise figure is how much you added to a hiss that was already there. The microphone arrives with its own irreducible hiss, and the only question is whether your amplifier's contribution is audible on top of it.

Because noise adds in quadrature, this is far more forgiving than it sounds: an amplifier whose own noise equals the source's adds only 3 dB. One at a third of the source's noise adds 0.5 dB and is effectively free. That is why chasing the last 0.5 dB of noise figure is usually the wrong use of a design day.

Where it breaks: the source hiss is only irreducible for a given source resistance. Halve the microphone's impedance and the floor drops — which is a real design option for a transformer-coupled input, and one the analogy hides by treating the source as fixed.

2. The three noise terms, and which one you can change

\[ e_{total} = \sqrt{\underbrace{4kTR_s}_{\text{source}} + \underbrace{e_{n,amp}^2}_{\text{voltage noise}} + \underbrace{(i_{n,amp}R_s)^2}_{\text{current noise}}} \] Three contributions, and the source term is the one you are trying to reach.
\[ \mathrm{NF} = 20\log_{10}\frac{e_{total}}{\sqrt{4kTR_s}} \]
\[ R_{opt} = \frac{e_{n}}{i_{n}} \] The source resistance at which a given amplifier is at its best. Below it, voltage noise dominates; above it, current noise does. This is the single most useful expression in low-noise design.

Read that table twice

The FET part has forty times less current noise and is the worst choice here, by 12 dB. Low noise is not a property of a part — it is a property of a part and a source impedance. The FET amplifier that is hopeless at 150 Ω is the right answer at 80 kΩ, where the bipolar's current noise would dominate completely.

This is why "low-noise op-amp" on its own is not a specification, and why the first question in any noise design is always what is the source impedance.

3. Phantom power, and the objection that turns out to be wrong

A condenser microphone needs 48 V, delivered through two 6.81 kΩ resistors, one to each signal leg. The obvious worry: those resistors are in the signal path, and a 6.81 kΩ resistor generates 10.6 nV/√Hz — nearly seven times the microphone's own noise. Does phantom power wreck the noise figure?

\[ \text{The two resistors appear in series across the input: } 13.6\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \] The 48 V supply is an AC ground, so from the signal's point of view the pair is one resistor bridging the two legs.
\[ e_n(13.6\,\mathrm{k}\Omega) = 15.0\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}} \]
\[ \text{But it is loaded by the microphone: } \frac{150}{150+13620} = 0.0109 \] The resistor's noise voltage is divided down by the microphone's much lower impedance before it reaches the amplifier input. This is the step everybody skips.
\[ 15.0 \times 0.0109 = 0.16\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}} \]
\[ \sqrt{1.576^2 + 0.164^2} = 1.585 \text{ vs } 1.576\,\mathrm{nV/\sqrt{Hz}} \] A degradation of 0.05 dB. Phantom power is essentially free, and it is free BECAUSE the source impedance is low.
What phantom does cost you is elsewhere. The two resistors must be matched — a 1% mismatch turns 48 V of common mode into a differential offset and degrades CMRR directly. Use 0.1% parts, or a matched pair. And they must be rated for the fault case of a shorted or hot-plugged microphone, where 48 V appears across one of them.

4. The topology, and why the gain control is in the middle

The classic balanced preamp: two amplifiers in non-inverting configuration sharing one gain-setting resistor between their inverting inputs, followed by a difference stage. It is the first two-thirds of an instrumentation amplifier, and the gain pot is the \(R_G\).

\[ A_{diff} = 1 + \frac{2R_f}{R_G} \] Differential gain, set by one variable resistor.
\[ A_{CM} = 1 \] A common-mode input puts no voltage across \(R_G\), so no current flows and both outputs move together at unity. That is where the CMRR comes from, and it costs nothing.
\[ R_G \to \infty \Rightarrow A = 1; \qquad R_G \to 0 \Rightarrow A \to \infty \] One pot spans the whole range. Put a fixed resistor in series with it to bound the maximum gain, or the noise at full rotation becomes unpleasant.

Why the pot goes there and not at the input: a gain control at the input is an attenuator, and attenuating before the first amplifier throws away signal-to-noise you can never get back. A gain control inside the first stage's feedback changes the gain without ever attenuating the source. That single placement decision is most of what makes a preamp quiet at low gain settings.

A preamp measures −128 dBu EIN with a 150 Ω source. A customer complains it is noisy with their 600 Ω ribbon microphone. Is something broken?

  1. Yes — EIN should not depend on the source
    EIN is defined with a stated source impedance, which is why every datasheet quotes the impedance alongside the number. Change the source and the figure changes, by definition.
  2. Yes — the input stage is probably oscillating with the higher impedance
    Worth checking, but there is a simpler explanation that requires nothing to be wrong.
  3. No — a 600 Ω source has 6 dB more thermal noise of its own
    Right. \(e_n \propto \sqrt{R}\), so four times the resistance is twice the noise voltage: the floor moves from −130.8 to −124.8 dBu before the preamp contributes anything. The preamp is behaving identically; the customer is hearing the microphone. The honest answer is to quote EIN at both impedances, and to note that a ribbon's low output makes this worse still.
  4. No — but only because ribbon mics are inherently noisy
    The right conclusion for the wrong reason. It is not a property of ribbons as such; it is the source resistance and the low output level, both of which are measurable and both of which follow from the physics above.

Move the source-resistance slider in the calculator above from 150 to 600 Ω and watch the floor rise 6 dB while the noise figure actually improves — because the amplifier's fixed \(e_n\) is now smaller relative to a larger source. Two different numbers, moving in opposite directions, and quoting only one of them is how this argument starts.

Build it in Circuit Toy

Start from Three op-amp instrumentation amplifier in the gallery — it is this topology with a fixed \(R_G\).

  1. Replace \(R_G\) with values giving 20, 40 and 60 dB and confirm \(A = 1 + 2R_f/R_G\) at each.
  2. Drive both inputs together with a 1 V source — a pure common-mode input — and confirm the output barely moves. Then mismatch one difference-stage resistor by 1% and watch CMRR collapse, which is the reason this topology puts its gain in the first stage rather than the second.
  3. Add two 6.81 kΩ resistors from each input to a 48 V source and confirm the signal is unaffected — the phantom calculation above, on a solver.
  4. Then mismatch those two by 1% and watch a differential offset appear from nothing but the common-mode supply.
