The Audio Signal Chain

Audio is a good vehicle for learning amplifier design because the whole chain is visible, every block has a reason to exist, and the requirements span a range no single technique covers — from a microphone's microvolts to a speaker's tens of volts, a span of about 140 dB.

Levels, which are the first thing to get straight. Audio engineering quotes everything in dB relative to a reference, and confusing the references is the commonest source of a factor-of-two error in a gain calculation.

Why the order is not negotiable

The chain is a relay race where each runner can only shout as loudly as they were shouted at. The first runner hears a whisper, and whatever they mishear is passed on as fact — no later runner can recover the original words, however clearly they speak.

That is Friis's formula and it has a hard consequence: the microphone preamplifier is the most demanding amplifier in an audio system, and the power amplifier — the big expensive one with the heatsinks — is one of the least demanding on noise, because by the time the signal reaches it, it is a volt and the noise floor is a settled question.

Where it breaks: a runner who shouts too loudly is just loud, whereas an overdriven stage clips and produces harmonics that were not in the original at all. The picture covers noise correctly and says nothing about headroom, which is the other half of gain structure.

2. Gain structure: the one calculation that matters

Every stage has a floor set by its noise and a ceiling set by its rails. The distance between them is that stage's dynamic range, and gain structure is the business of keeping the signal in the middle of every stage's window at once.

\[ \text{Headroom} = 20\log_{10}\frac{V_{clip}}{V_{nominal}} \] How far above the working level you can go before clipping. Pro audio conventionally leaves 18–20 dB.
\[ \text{SNR} = 20\log_{10}\frac{V_{nominal}}{V_{noise}} \]
\[ \text{Dynamic range} = \text{headroom} + \text{SNR} = 20\log_{10}\frac{V_{clip}}{V_{noise}} \] Fixed by the stage. Where you sit the nominal level inside it is your choice, and it is the only choice you have.
The rule that follows

Put the gain as early as the largest signal will allow, and put the volume control as late as clipping will allow. A volume control early in the chain attenuates the signal and then everything after it adds noise to a smaller signal; a volume control late in the chain means the stages before it must handle full level without clipping. Almost every real design puts it after the preamp and before the power amp, which is the compromise that satisfies both.
