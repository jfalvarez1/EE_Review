Amplifier Classes A to H

The class letter answers exactly one question: what fraction of the cycle does each output device conduct for? Everything else — efficiency, distortion, heatsink size, cost, whether it can be used for radio — falls out of that single choice.

The whole subject in one picture

An output stage is a tap feeding a sink, and the class describes how the tap is operated.

Class A leaves the tap half open all the time and modulates around that. The water is always flowing, so the response is perfectly smooth — and you pay for the water you did not use. Class B shuts it fully between uses: nothing is wasted, but there is a moment of nothing at the start of each pour while the valve crosses from shut to open. Class AB leaves it barely cracked, so the flow is already established when you need it, for a small standing cost. Class D gives up on the analogue tap entirely and switches a full-bore valve on and off very fast, then averages the result — which wastes almost nothing, because a valve that is either fully open or fully shut dissipates nothing either way.

Where it breaks: water in a pipe has momentum and a transistor does not, so the picture says nothing about why class D needs an output filter or why its switching edges cost energy. It is right about where the waste goes and wrong about the dynamics.

2. Where 25% and 78.5% come from

Both numbers are worth deriving once, because they are the entire argument for and against class A and they are asked in interviews.

\[ \textbf{Class A: } P_{DC} = V_{CC}I_Q \quad\text{always, signal or not} \] The device conducts for the whole cycle, so the supply current is constant. That is the definition and it is also the problem.
\[ P_{out,max} = \frac{(V_{CC}/2)^2}{2R_L}, \qquad I_Q = \frac{V_{CC}}{2R_L} \] Best case: bias at mid-supply, swing the full half-rail as a sine.
\[ \eta = \frac{V_{CC}^2/8R_L}{V_{CC}^2/2R_L} = \frac{1}{4} = \textbf{25\%} \] And that is the ceiling, reached only at full output. At a tenth of full power it is 2.5%.
\[ \textbf{Class B: } P_{DC} = \frac{2V_{CC}\hat{V}}{\pi R_L}, \qquad P_{out} = \frac{\hat{V}^2}{2R_L} \] Supply current is now a rectified sine, whose average is \(2/\pi\) of its peak — so the supply draws only what the signal needs.
\[ \eta = \frac{\pi}{4}\cdot\frac{\hat{V}}{V_{CC}} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{\pi}{4} = \textbf{78.5\%} \text{ at } \hat{V} = V_{CC} \]
\[ P_{diss,max} = \frac{2V_{CC}^2}{\pi^2R_L} \quad\text{at}\quad \hat{V} = \frac{2V_{CC}}{\pi} = 0.637\,V_{CC} \] The number that sizes the heatsink, and it is NOT at full output. Differentiate \(P_{DC}-P_{out}\) and set it to zero; the worst case is at 64% of full swing.
The consequence people miss

A class A amplifier is hottest when idle and its dissipation falls as you turn it up, because output power comes out of a fixed supply draw. A class B or AB amplifier is coolest when idle and hottest at about two-thirds of full output. They are opposite machines, and a heatsink sized by intuition for one will be wrong for the other.

You are sizing the heatsink for a class AB amplifier. At what output level do you calculate the dissipation?

  1. At full rated output — the worst case
    At full output the device has the most current through it but the least voltage across it, because the output is near the rail. \(V\!\cdot\!I\) is not maximised there.
  2. At idle, since that is where class A is worst
    True for class A, and backwards for AB. An AB stage draws only its small quiescent current at idle and dissipates almost nothing.
  3. At about 64% of full swing
    Right. \(P_{diss} = P_{DC} - P_{out}\), and differentiating gives a maximum at \(\hat V = 2V_{CC}/\pi = 0.637V_{CC}\), where dissipation is \(2V_{CC}^2/\pi^2R_L\). Both device current and device voltage are substantial there. Size the heatsink for that point, and then check that a continuous sine at that level is a load case you actually care about — for music it is pessimistic, for a test-tone burn-in it is exactly right.
  4. At whatever level the datasheet quotes
    The datasheet quotes the device's capability, not your circuit's dissipation. The two are only related through the calculation above.

3. The three that are not just "more conduction angle"

Class C, and why it only works with a tank

A class C stage conducts for well under half a cycle, so its output is a train of narrow pulses that looks nothing like the input. On its own it is useless. Put a parallel resonant tank on the output and the tank rings at its own frequency, driven once per cycle by each pulse — and the result is a clean sine at the tank frequency.

\[ Q = \frac{f_0}{\mathrm{BW}}, \qquad \text{the tank both filters and stores} \]

That is why class C is an RF-only technique: it needs a narrowband load to reconstruct the waveform, and audio is not narrowband. It is also why class C cannot be used for anything amplitude-modulated without care — the output amplitude is set by the tank and the supply, not by the input.

Class G: switch the rails

Music has a high crest factor — the peaks are ten or twenty times the average. A class AB amplifier sized for the peaks spends nearly all its time running from rails far higher than the signal needs, dissipating the difference. Class G runs from a low rail normally and switches to a high one only when the signal demands it.

\[ P_{diss} \approx (V_{rail} - |v_{out}|)\,|i_{out}| \] Lower the rail when the signal is small and the dissipation falls in direct proportion.

The cost is the transition: a device switching between rails produces an artefact, and a badly designed one produces it right in the middle of the signal's dynamic range. Done well the artefact is below the noise floor.

Class H: make the rail follow the signal

The same idea taken continuously. Instead of two or three fixed rails, a tracking supply keeps the rail a fixed headroom above the instantaneous output. Efficiency approaches class D while the output stage stays linear, so there is no switching residue in the audio path at all — the switching is in the supply, where a filter is easier.

The same technique under a different name is envelope tracking in RF power amplifiers, where it is the main reason a modern phone can transmit an LTE waveform without flattening its battery.

4. Choosing, in practice

If the requirement is…UseBecause
Under a watt, quality matters, heat does notClass A No handover to get wrong, and at that power the 25% ceiling costs you a few hundred milliwatts
1–100 W of audio, mains poweredClass AB The mature default. Predictable, quiet, and the heatsink is affordable at this size
Battery powered, or above ~100 WClass D Efficiency stops being a nicety and becomes the reason the product exists
Very large, and linear quality is contractualClass G or H Most of class D's efficiency without any switching residue in the signal path
Narrowband RF powerClass C, E or F A tuned load reconstructs the waveform, so the device never has to be linear
A small-signal gain stage anywhereClass A At milliamps the efficiency question does not arise, and linearity is free
The most common mistake is choosing class D for a small amplifier. Below a watt or two the switching losses, the output filter, the EMI work and the extra parts cost more than the heat you saved. Efficiency matters when the waste is large in absolute terms — a 90% efficient 1 W amplifier saves 0.35 W over a class AB one, which is not worth an inductor and a compliance test.
