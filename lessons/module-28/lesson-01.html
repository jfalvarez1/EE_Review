The Amplifier Map

"Amplifier" names about forty different circuits that share a word and very little else. A photodiode front end, a 200 W subwoofer channel and a satellite receiver's first stage are all amplifiers, and knowing one of them well tells you almost nothing about the others.

This lesson is the map. It exists so that every later design lesson has somewhere to hang: when you meet a new requirement, the first job is to work out which amplifier you are being asked for, and that is a question with a procedure rather than a matter of taste.

Everything below follows from one question: what does the source look like, and what does the load need? Not "what gain do I want" — gain is the easy part and it is nearly always available. The hard part is impedance, noise and power, and those are decided at the two ends, not in the middle.

1. There are only four amplifiers

Strip away the implementation and every amplifier senses either a voltage or a current, and delivers either a voltage or a current. Two choices, two ends, four combinations. That is the whole taxonomy, and the rest is engineering.

Why the impedances come out that way

Think of an amplifier's input as a listener and its output as a speaker, and ask what each has to do not to interfere with the thing it is connected to.

To measure a voltage without changing it, you must draw no current — so a voltage-sensing input is high impedance. To measure a current without changing it, you must develop no voltage — so a current-sensing input is low impedance, ideally a short. The same logic at the output: to deliver a voltage that does not sag, be a stiff low-impedance source; to deliver a current that does not care what the load does, be a high-impedance one.

So the four impedance combinations are not four conventions to memorise. They are forced, two at a time, by what you are sensing and what you are delivering.

Where it breaks: "high" and "low" are relative to the source and load, not absolute. A 1 kΩ input is high impedance to a 10 Ω source and a short circuit to a 10 MΩ one. Always ask high compared with what.

2. The same four, sorted by how they are built

The table above is about what an amplifier is. This one is about what you actually put on the board, which is a different question with a different answer.

The decision, in the order it should be made

1. Source and load pick the type — one of the four in the first table. This is forced, not chosen.

2. Signal size picks the noise strategy. Below about a millivolt you are designing a noise budget, and the first stage decides it. Above about a volt, noise is usually somebody else's problem.

3. Bandwidth and power pick the family. Under a few MHz and under 50 mA, an op-amp. Beyond either, you are discrete, or power, or RF.

4. Only now, gain. Which is two resistors, and is the part everybody starts with.

3. Gain distribution: where to put it, and why not all in one place

\[ F_{total} = F_1 + \frac{F_2 - 1}{G_1} + \frac{F_3 - 1}{G_1G_2} + \cdots \] Friis. Every later stage's noise is divided by all the gain in front of it, so the FIRST stage sets the noise figure of the whole chain and nothing downstream can recover it.
\[ \text{So: gain early.} \]
\[ \text{But } V_{out} = A\,V_{in} \leq V_{rail} \] And gain early means an early stage clips on a large input, before any filter downstream has had a chance to remove the thing that was large.
Gain distribution

Amplifying a signal is like photocopying a document. Enlarging early makes the faint pencil marks legible — but it also enlarges the coffee stain, and once the stain runs off the edge of the page you cannot crop it out later.

So you enlarge as much as you can before the stain reaches the edge, then clean the page, then enlarge again. That is exactly a gain stage, a filter, and a second gain stage — and it is why almost every real signal chain alternates between the two rather than putting all the gain in one block.

Where it breaks: a photocopier's noise is added at each copy in the same proportion; a real amplifier's noise is dominated by the first stage in a way that does not have a copying analogue. The picture is right about the clipping trade and understates how much the first stage matters.

A sensor gives 100 µV of signal with 50 mV of 60 Hz interference on top. You need 60 dB of gain and a ±5 V output. Where does the gain go?

  1. All 60 dB up front, then filter
    1000 × 50 mV is 50 V of interference. The first stage saturates and the signal is gone before the filter sees it — and a saturated stage does not merely clip, it also takes time to recover.
  2. About 20–26 dB, then the 60 Hz notch, then the rest
    Right. Ten to twenty times gain puts the interference at 0.5–1 V, comfortably inside the rails, while lifting the 100 µV signal to 1–2 mV where the second stage's noise no longer matters. Then remove the interference, then take the remaining 34–40 dB. The first stage still sets the noise figure because it still has gain — you have not given that up.
  3. Filter first, then all the gain
    Safe against clipping, and it costs you noise: the filter's own resistors and the second stage's input noise now sit at the same level as a 100 µV signal with no gain in front of them. Friis says this is the expensive choice.
  4. Split evenly, 30 dB and 30 dB
    30 dB is 31.6×, putting the interference at 1.6 V — workable, but it is a guess rather than a calculation. The right split is set by the largest interferer and the rail, and here that permits about 20 dB.

The general rule: take as much gain as the largest thing present will allow, then remove that thing, then take more. The interferer, not the signal, sets the first stage's gain.
