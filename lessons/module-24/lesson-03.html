Hot-Swap & Inrush Limiting

Hot-swap circuits allow safe insertion of boards into powered backplanes. They limit inrush current to prevent supply dips and connector damage.

Key Insight: When a capacitive load is suddenly connected, the capacitors look like a short circuit. The hot-swap controller limits current during startup and protects against sustained faults.

Hot-Swap Controller Features

Feature Purpose
Soft-Start Slowly turns on FET to limit dV/dt
Current Limit Prevents excessive current during faults
SOA Timer Shuts off if FET exceeds safe area too long
Power Good Output signal when startup is complete
Retry/Latch Behavior after fault condition
FET Selection: During soft-start, the FET operates in linear region with high VDS and significant current. SOA (Safe Operating Area) must allow this for the startup duration.

Startup control is not fault protection

VDRIVE represents an ideal floating gate supply, 10 V above the source. RGATE and CGATE set a 10 ms gate-to-source time constant. Start a transient with both capacitors discharged; a simulator’s precomputed DC state starts fully on and hides inrush. The W/L = 10000 generic uses Kp = 110 µA/V², Vth = 0.7 V and λ = 0.04 V−1.

RSENSE only measures current. Nothing in this table feeds its voltage back to the gate, so it cannot enforce a 2 A limit. The gate RC does not guarantee a linear output ramp either. Record peak V(in,sense)/10 mΩ and 10–90% output rise time; repeat with half the transient step. A nonlinear transient solver is required for these readings; the DC checks below establish only the fully-on and disabled conditions.

For a hypothetical linear 0–12 V output ramp in 10 ms, a 1 mF capacitor needs 1.2 A. The 24 Ω load adds up to 0.5 A, so a controller needs at least 1.7 A just before ramp completion. This calculation specifies a controller requirement; it does not predict this RC gate waveform.

Bench acceptance: measure VDS, shunt voltage and VGS together during startup. Compare the resulting current/voltage/time trajectory with the chosen MOSFET’s SOA curve at the intended temperature. Capacitor energy alone cannot certify SOA. A real controller also needs a fault timer, current or power limiting, and evaluated start-into-short and hot-short behavior.

Reference: TI LM5069, sections 9.1 and 9.2.1.2 describes those design checks. This lab is a deliberately incomplete gate-drive experiment, not an LM5069 implementation.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.
