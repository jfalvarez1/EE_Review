Overvoltage & Crowbar Protection

Overvoltage events can destroy sensitive electronics. Protection circuits clamp transients or short the supply to blow a fuse.

TVS Diode
Fast clamp (~1ns)
Varistor (MOV)
High energy, slower
Crowbar (SCR)
Shorts supply, blows fuse
Key Insight: TVS clamping limits voltage but allows current to flow through the protected circuit. Crowbar completely shorts the input, which requires a fuse to clear the fault.

TVS Selection Parameters

Parameter Symbol Description
Working Voltage VRWM Max continuous voltage (no conduction)
Breakdown VBR Voltage where TVS starts conducting
Clamping Voltage VC Voltage at peak pulse current
Peak Pulse Current IPP Max current for specified pulse width
Peak Pulse Power PPP VC × IPP (e.g., 400W, 600W, 1500W)
Selection Rule: VRWM ≥ max normal operating voltage (including tolerances). VC ≤ absolute max rating of protected IC.

Choose the part before you run it

The selection rule above, applied to this rail: a 12 V supply that may sit 10% high reaches 13.2 V, which rules out a 13 V stand-off. A 15 V stand-off part is the next step up, and Vishay’s SMBJ15A row gives VBR = 16.7 V minimum at 1 mA and VC = 24.4 V at IPPM = 24.6 A. The table models that as a zener with BV = 16.7 V (the solver puts exactly 1 mA through it there) and a dynamic resistance of (24.4 − 16.7)/24.6 = 0.31 Ω, which Circuit Toy accepts as the zener’s rz. Its cathode goes to the rail: turned round, a unidirectional TVS is a forward-biased diode across the supply.

On the 60 V plateau the source pushes (60 − 16.7)/(10 + 0.31) = 4.20 A at the clamp, so V(protected) is about 16.7 + 4.20 × 0.31 = 18.0 V plus the junction’s own few hundred millivolts above its 1 mA knee: 18.21 V in the solver, with 4.18 A in the TVS. That is 76 W for 100 µs, about 7.6 mJ; compare it with the datasheet’s peak-pulse-power curve at that duration rather than with the 600 W headline, which is for a 10/1000 µs pulse.

Bench acceptance: with a current-limited supply, raise the rail slowly and confirm the TVS draws only leakage up to the highest normal voltage; its rated leakage at stand-off is a few microamps, which matters on a battery rail. Then apply a pulse from a generator with a known source impedance and record V(protected) and the TVS current with a current probe. Compare the clamp with the protected IC’s absolute maximum, not its operating range.

What this build cannot tell you: one straight line from VBR to VC is a single fit; a real TVS curves, and its VBR spread runs to 18.5 V. There is no junction capacitance, no thermal derating, and no limit on pulse energy, so the solver will clamp a pulse the real part would not survive.

Reference: Vishay SMBJ5.0A thru SMBJ188A datasheet (document 88392), electrical characteristics table: the SMBJ15A row for this build, and the SMBJ5.0A row (5 V stand-off, 9.2 V clamp) behind the interactive’s defaults above. The datasheet does not endorse this circuit.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.
