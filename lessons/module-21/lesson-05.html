RF Filter Design

RF filters are critical components that select desired frequencies while rejecting unwanted signals, harmonics, and interference. Understanding filter design enables proper frequency channelization, image rejection, and spectral compliance in RF systems.

Purpose:Select/reject specific frequency bands
Types:Low-pass, High-pass, Band-pass, Band-stop (Notch)
Technologies:LC, Crystal, SAW, BAW, Ceramic, Cavity
Key Specs:Insertion loss, bandwidth, selectivity, return loss
Filter Trade-offs: Sharper roll-off requires more poles (complexity), which increases insertion loss and group delay variation. Choose the minimum order that meets requirements.

Selection Guidelines

  • Butterworth: General purpose, no ripple, good for wideband applications
  • Chebyshev: When selectivity matters more than passband flatness
  • Elliptic: Maximum selectivity with minimum order (most efficient)
  • Bessel: When preserving signal waveform is critical (pulse applications)

LC Filter Design

Lumped-element LC filters are fundamental building blocks for RF filtering at frequencies up to a few GHz.

Normalized Prototype Values

Filter design starts with normalized low-pass prototypes (g-values), then scales to desired frequency and impedance.

Scaling:

L = g × Z₀ / (2π × fc)

C = g / (Z₀ × 2π × fc)

where g = normalized element value, Z₀ = characteristic impedance, fc = cutoff frequency

Bandpass Filter Design

Bandpass filters are created by transforming low-pass prototypes. The bandwidth and center frequency define the transformation.

Bandpass Transformation:

f₀ = √(f_L × f_H) - geometric center frequency

BW = f_H - f_L - bandwidth

Q = f₀ / BW - quality factor

Coupled Resonator Filters

For narrow-band filters (Q > 10), coupled resonator structures provide better performance than direct LC transformation.

Edge-coupled:Capacitive coupling between resonators
Hairpin:Compact folded resonators
Interdigital:High stopband rejection
Combline:Good spurious response

RF Filter Technologies

1. LC Filters

  • Frequency range: DC to ~3 GHz
  • Low cost, easily tunable
  • Limited Q (50-200 for SMD inductors)
  • Good for wide-band filters

2. Crystal Filters

  • Very high Q (10,000-100,000)
  • Narrow bandwidths possible
  • Fixed frequencies (10-200 MHz typical)
  • Used for IF filtering

3. SAW (Surface Acoustic Wave)

  • Frequency range: 50 MHz to 3 GHz
  • High selectivity, compact size
  • Moderate insertion loss (1-5 dB)
  • Temperature sensitive

4. BAW (Bulk Acoustic Wave) / FBAR

  • Frequency range: 500 MHz to 10 GHz
  • Higher power handling than SAW
  • Excellent temperature stability
  • Used in modern smartphones

5. Cavity Filters

  • Very high Q (5,000-20,000)
  • Low loss, high power handling
  • Large size (used in base stations)
  • Frequency range: 100 MHz to 40 GHz

Practical Considerations

Component Quality Factor

Real components have finite Q, which causes insertion loss and affects filter response.

Insertion Loss (approx): IL ≈ 4.34 × N × (f₀/BW) / Q_component [dB]

Parasitics and Layout

  • Component self-resonance limits usable frequency
  • PCB traces add inductance (~1 nH/mm)
  • Pad capacitance can detune filter
  • Ground via inductance affects high-frequency performance

Tuning Strategies

  • Use 5% or 2% tolerance components where possible
  • Include tuning pads for critical applications
  • Simulation with parasitic extraction is essential
  • Consider temperature coefficients
