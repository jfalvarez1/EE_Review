Noise Figure, the Cascade and Receiver Sensitivity

A receiver's job is to recover a signal that arrives with less power than a mobile phone radiates in a microsecond. What stops it is not gain — gain is cheap — but noise, and specifically noise added by the first thing the signal touches. This lesson is about where that noise comes from, how it accumulates through a chain, and how the answer turns into a range in metres.

Why only the first stage matters

Imagine passing a whispered message down a line of people in a noisy room. The first person hears the whisper faintly and repeats it loudly. Everyone after that hears a shout, so the room noise barely bothers them.

But whatever the first person mis-heard is now shouted too — amplified along with the message, indistinguishable from it forever after. That is the whole content of the Friis equation: the first stage's noise is added at full weight, and every later stage's contribution is divided by all the gain in front of it.

Where it breaks: the analogy suggests more gain is always better. It is not — shout too loudly and the next person's ears distort, which is exactly the compression and intermodulation of the next lesson. Receiver design is the tension between those two, and neither can be optimised alone.

1. The noise floor is a temperature

Every resistor at a temperature above absolute zero generates noise, and remarkably the available noise power depends only on temperature and bandwidth — not on the resistance. That gives a floor that no receiver design can go below.

\[ P_n = kTB \] \(k = 1.381\times10^{-23}\) J/K. At \(T = 290\) K this is \(4.004\times10^{-21}\) W per hertz.
\[ 10\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{4.004\times10^{-21}}{10^{-3}}\right) = \mathbf{-174\ dBm/Hz} \] The number to memorise. Every noise calculation in radio starts here.
\[ N_{dBm} = -174 + 10\log_{10}B + NF \] The receiver's noise floor: the thermal floor, widened by the bandwidth you chose to look through, raised by the noise your own hardware adds.

Bandwidth is the cheapest sensitivity there is, and you buy it with data rate.

2. Noise figure, noise factor, noise temperature

Three ways of saying the same thing, and every RF datasheet assumes you can move between them without being told.

\[ F = \frac{SNR_{in}}{SNR_{out}} \quad\text{(linear, the noise \emph{factor})}, \qquad NF = 10\log_{10}F \ \text{dB} \] How much worse the signal-to-noise ratio got on the way through. \(F = 1\) (NF = 0 dB) means the block added no noise of its own, which only a lossless passive at absolute zero achieves.
\[ T_e = (F-1)\,T_0, \qquad T_0 = 290\ \mathrm{K} \] Equivalent noise temperature — the temperature a resistor at the input would have to be to produce the same extra noise. Satellite and radio-astronomy work uses this because it stays meaningful for the very low values where NF in dB becomes an awkward way to express small differences.
\[ \text{a passive lossy element: } NF = \text{loss in dB} \] An exact and very useful result. A cable with 1.5 dB of loss has a noise figure of exactly 1.5 dB. A 3 dB splitter has NF = 3 dB. This is why a lossy thing in front of the LNA is so expensive, and why the LNA goes at the antenna.

3. The Friis cascade

\[ F_{total} = F_1 + \frac{F_2-1}{G_1} + \frac{F_3-1}{G_1G_2} + \frac{F_4-1}{G_1G_2G_3} + \cdots \] All quantities linear, not dB. This is the single most-misapplied equation in RF: adding dB values here gives a confidently wrong answer.

Two consequences follow immediately, and they shape every receiver ever built:

  • Put the lowest-noise, highest-gain thing first. Once \(G_1\) is large, everything after it is divided into insignificance.
  • Anything lossy before the LNA costs you its loss, directly. A 1.5 dB filter in front of a 1 dB LNA makes the pair 2.5 dB, full stop — the LNA's gain cannot help with noise that was added upstream of it.
The design tension this creates. Filter-then-LNA costs you noise figure. LNA-then-filter costs you blocking: the LNA now sees every signal in the band, including the 900 MHz base station transmitting a hundred million times harder than the wanted signal, and it will compress or intermodulate. Real receivers put a cheap, low-loss preselector before the LNA and a sharp one after — paying a little noise to keep the LNA linear.

4. Sensitivity, and turning it into metres

\[ S_{dBm} = -174 + 10\log_{10}B + NF + SNR_{req} \] The minimum received power at which the demodulator still works. \(SNR_{req}\) is a property of the modulation and the coding, not of your hardware — it comes from the standard.
\[ FSPL_{dB} = 20\log_{10}d + 20\log_{10}f + 32.45 \quad (d\ \text{in km},\ f\ \text{in MHz}) \] Free-space path loss. Note it grows with frequency as well as distance — not because space absorbs more, but because a fixed-gain antenna has a smaller aperture at a higher frequency.
\[ P_{rx} = P_{tx} + G_{tx} - FSPL + G_{rx}, \qquad \text{Link margin} = P_{rx} - S \] The link budget in one line. Aim for 10–20 dB of margin: free space is an optimistic model and the real world has walls, bodies, rain and fading in it.

The pattern in the right-hand column is the whole of adaptive modulation: roughly 6 dB of extra SNR per extra bit per symbol. That is why a Wi-Fi link drops from 256-QAM to QPSK as you walk away from the router — it is trading rate for the SNR it no longer has. And it is why at the top rates the limit stops being your receiver's noise figure and becomes the transmitter's error vector magnitude.

5. Worked example: a 915 MHz sensor link

An LNA (NF 1.2 dB, gain 16 dB) behind a SAW preselector (1.8 dB loss), into a mixer (NF 9 dB, conversion gain 6 dB), into an IF amplifier (NF 5 dB, gain 30 dB). Bandwidth 200 kHz, GFSK needing 12 dB SNR.

\[ F_1 = 10^{1.8/10} = 1.514,\ G_1 = 10^{-1.8/10} = 0.661 \] The filter's noise factor equals its loss, and its gain is that loss as a fraction below one.
\[ F_2 = 10^{1.2/10} = 1.318,\ G_2 = 39.81; \quad F_3 = 7.943,\ G_3 = 3.981; \quad F_4 = 3.162 \]
\[ F_{tot} = 1.514 + \frac{0.318}{0.661} + \frac{6.943}{0.661\times39.81} + \frac{2.162}{0.661\times39.81\times3.981} \] \(= 1.514 + 0.481 + 0.264 + 0.0206 = 2.279\)
\[ NF_{tot} = 10\log_{10}(2.279) = \mathbf{3.58\ dB} \] The filter alone contributes 1.8 dB of it. Move the LNA in front of the filter and \(F_{tot}\) becomes \(1.318 + 0.514/39.81 + \ldots = 1.35\), i.e. 1.30 dB — a 2.3 dB improvement for zero component cost, purely from order.
\[ S = -174 + 10\log_{10}(2\times10^5) + 3.58 + 12 = -174 + 53.0 + 3.58 + 12 = \mathbf{-105.4\ dBm} \]

At +14 dBm transmit with 2 dBi antennas at both ends, the allowable path loss is \(14 + 2 + 2 + 105.4 = 123.4\) dB. Solving the FSPL equation at 915 MHz gives \(20\log d = 123.4 - 59.2 - 32.45 = 31.7\), so \(d = 38.6\) km in free space — and perhaps 1–3 km in a town once buildings, fading margin and a non-ideal antenna are accounted for.

That 2.3 dB is worth more than it looks. Path loss grows as \(20\log d\) in free space, so 2.3 dB is a 30% range increase; in a real environment where loss grows nearer \(35\log d\), it is about 16%. Either way it came from swapping the order of two parts already on the board — and it is invisible on a schematic that does not record which one the antenna reaches first.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse A +30 dBm transmitter keyed 1 m away, in an adjacent band The LNA receives roughly −10 dBm — around a hundred million times the wanted −105 dBm signal. It is driven far past compression and its gain collapses, taking the wanted signal with it. This is blocking, and it is a specification, not an accident: every standard defines a blocker level the receiver must tolerate. The defence is a preselector before the LNA, sized so the out-of-band blocker is attenuated below the LNA's compression point. An LNA-first chain has the best noise figure and the worst blocking, which is the trade the whole architecture turns on.
Abuse A 3 m coax run added between antenna and receiver during installation RG-174 loses about 1.0 dB/m at 915 MHz, so 3 m is 3 dB. Placed before the LNA it adds 3 dB directly to the cascade NF. Sensitivity drops from −105.4 to about −102.6 dBm, roughly a 28% range loss, and nothing anywhere reports an error. The fix is architectural: put the LNA at the antenna (a mast-head or in-line LNA) so the cable loss lands after the gain, where Friis divides it by 40. This is why every satellite dish has electronics bolted to it rather than indoors.
Abuse Bandwidth widened from 200 kHz to 2 MHz to raise the data rate \(10\log_{10}(10) = 10\) dB more noise admitted. Sensitivity goes from −105.4 to −95.4 dBm. Expected and correct: a 10× data rate costs exactly 10 dB of sensitivity, or about 68% of the free-space range. There is no way round it — it is thermodynamics, not engineering. Deciding where to sit on that curve is what choosing a radio standard is.
Abuse A switching regulator placed under the LNA with a 1.2 MHz fundamental Its 762nd harmonic falls at 914.4 MHz — inside the channel. Even at −120 dBm coupled it sits above the −121 dBm thermal floor of a 200 kHz channel. Sensitivity degrades and no bench measurement with the regulator disabled will ever show it. This is why the self-interference test above exists as a separate line item. Mitigations: move the switching frequency so no harmonic lands in band, spread-spectrum the clock, shield the LNA, or use an LDO for the front end and accept the efficiency cost.
