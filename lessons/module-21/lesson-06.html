Transmission Lines and S-Parameters

Everything before this module treated a wire as a wire: the same voltage at both ends, at the same instant. That assumption is not a simplification, it is a frequency-dependent assumption, and RF is exactly the region where it stops being true. Once a connection is a meaningful fraction of a wavelength long, the voltage at one end is not the voltage at the other, and the connection has an impedance of its own that has nothing to do with the resistance of the copper.

This lesson is the language the rest of RF is written in. Get it and the Smith chart, matching, filters and amplifier design all become one subject rather than five.

Why a wire becomes a thing

A short wire is a rigid rod: push one end and the other end moves at the same moment. A long wire is a rope: flick it and a wave travels down it, and what happens at the far end comes back to you later, as a reflection.

Tie the rope's far end to a wall and the wave comes back inverted. Leave it loose and it comes back the same way up. Attach it to something that absorbs exactly the energy the rope delivers — sand, a damper — and nothing comes back at all. That last case is a matched termination, and it is the entire goal of RF impedance matching.

Where it breaks: a rope carries one wave; a transmission line carries a forward and a reverse wave simultaneously, and what you measure with a probe is their sum. That sum is the standing wave, and it is why a voltage measured somewhere along a mismatched line tells you almost nothing on its own.

1. When does a wire become a transmission line?

The honest answer is “always” — but it only matters when the delay along it is a significant part of a cycle, or of a rise time.

\[ v_p = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,\text{eff}}}}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{v_p}{f} \] On FR4 microstrip \(\varepsilon_{r,\text{eff}} \approx 3.0\) (lower than the bulk 4.3, because some of the field is in the air above the trace), giving \(v_p = 1.73\times10^{8}\) m/s, or 5.8 ps per mm. Stripline is fully embedded, so \(\varepsilon_{r,\text{eff}} = 4.3\) and it is slower at 6.9 ps/mm.
\[ \ell_{crit} \approx \frac{\lambda}{10} \quad\text{(RF)},\qquad \ell_{crit} \approx \frac{t_r}{2 t_{pd}} \quad\text{(digital edges)} \] Two different rules for the same physics. The RF one asks whether the phase shift along the line is significant; the digital one asks whether the reflection gets back before the edge has finished.

This is the single most common reason a radio that works on the dev board does not work on the product.

2. Characteristic impedance is not a resistance

A 50 Ω line does not have 50 Ω of resistance. Put an ohmmeter across it and you will read a short or an open depending on the far end. \(Z_0\) is the ratio of voltage to current in a wave travelling one way along the line, and it is set by geometry.

\[ Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} \] Inductance and capacitance per unit length. Both scale with length, so their ratio — and therefore \(Z_0\) — does not depend on how long the line is. A 1 mm and a 1 m length of the same trace have the same \(Z_0\).
\[ \Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0} \] The reflection coefficient. \(Z_L = Z_0\) gives \(\Gamma = 0\) and nothing comes back; a short gives \(-1\) (inverted), an open gives \(+1\) (upright). Exactly the rope.
\[ Z_{in}(\ell) = Z_0\, \frac{Z_L + jZ_0\tan\beta\ell}{Z_0 + jZ_L\tan\beta\ell}, \qquad \beta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \] The impedance you actually see at the input, which is a function of how far away the load is. This one equation is the whole of distributed matching.

The three cases worth memorising

Line length\(Z_{in}\)Consequence
\(\lambda/2\)\(Z_L\) A half-wave line is invisible. Useful for getting a measurement plane where you want it — but only at one frequency.
\(\lambda/4\)\(Z_0^2/Z_L\) An impedance inverter. A short becomes an open and an open becomes a short. This is the quarter-wave transformer, and it is why an open stub can act as a capacitor and a shorted stub as an inductor.
\(\ll\lambda\), open end\(1/(j\omega C)\) A short open stub is just a capacitor — which is exactly how a lumped circuit behaves, recovered as a limit. The two views agree; they do not compete.
Why 50 Ω? For air-dielectric coax, minimum loss is at about 77 Ω and maximum power handling at about 30 Ω. 50 Ω is roughly the geometric mean, and it is a compromise nobody has had a good enough reason to abandon. Video uses 75 Ω because it is nearer the low-loss optimum and loss matters more than power there. Neither number is fundamental — what matters is that everyone agrees, so parts interoperate.

3. VSWR, return loss and mismatch loss — one number, three costumes

These three are the same information. Engineers use whichever is conventional in the document they are writing, which is why specifications mix them freely and why it pays to be able to convert in your head.

\[ \text{VSWR} = \frac{1+|\Gamma|}{1-|\Gamma|}, \qquad RL_{dB} = -20\log_{10}|\Gamma|, \qquad ML_{dB} = -10\log_{10}\!\left(1-|\Gamma|^2\right) \] VSWR is the ratio of the standing wave's maximum to its minimum, and it is what a slotted line physically measured — a historical artefact that stuck. Return loss is how much power comes back, in dB, stated positive. Mismatch loss is how much of the forward power never reaches the load.

The column that surprises people is mismatch loss. A 2:1 VSWR sounds alarming and reflects 11% of the power, but costs only 0.5 dB — about a 6% range reduction. The reason a bad VSWR still matters is rarely the lost power: it is what the reflection does to the amplifier. A PA presented with a wildly wrong load can see voltage or current far outside what it was designed for, and that is a destruction mechanism, not an efficiency one.

4. S-parameters, and why RF abandoned Z and Y

Low-frequency two-port parameters — Z, Y, h — are all defined by open-circuiting or short-circuiting a port. That is the problem. At 6 GHz you cannot make a true open (the fringing capacitance is a real admittance) or a true short (the via inductance is a real impedance), and an active device presented with either will very often oscillate.

What you can make, and make extremely well, is a 50 Ω termination. S-parameters are defined with every port terminated in \(Z_0\), which is why they are the only two-port description that is actually measurable at RF. That is the whole reason for their existence — not elegance, measurability.

\[ a_n = \frac{V_n + Z_0 I_n}{2\sqrt{Z_0}}, \qquad b_n = \frac{V_n - Z_0 I_n}{2\sqrt{Z_0}} \] Incident and reflected power waves at port \(n\), normalised so that \(|a|^2\) and \(|b|^2\) are powers. Everything else follows from these two.
\[ \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} S_{11} & S_{12} \\ S_{21} & S_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} \] Read the subscripts as out-of, into: \(S_{21}\) is what comes out of port 2 for a wave going into port 1. It is backwards from how most people first read it, and getting it wrong is a rite of passage.
NameMeasured withWhat a designer reads from it
S11Input reflection Port 2 terminated in \(Z_0\) Input match. This is \(\Gamma\) at the input. Convert to VSWR or return loss at will.
S21Forward transmission Drive port 1, terminate port 2 Gain for an amplifier, insertion loss for a filter or cable. The headline number.
S12Reverse transmission Drive port 2, terminate port 1 Isolation. For an amplifier you want it tiny; if it is not, the output pulls the input and the stage can oscillate.
S22Output reflection Port 1 terminated in \(Z_0\) Output match. Matters to whatever comes next.

Properties that let you sanity-check a data file

  • Reciprocity: any network of passive, isotropic materials has \(S_{12} = S_{21}\). If a filter's file does not, either it contains a ferrite (an isolator or circulator, which is deliberately non-reciprocal) or the measurement is wrong.
  • Passivity: \(|S_{11}|^2 + |S_{21}|^2 \le 1\) at every frequency, for every port. A passive part cannot return more power than it was given. A value above 1 means a calibration error or an active device.
  • Losslessness: for a lossless network the sum is exactly 1. The shortfall is the dissipation, which is how you extract a filter's insertion loss from its S-parameters.
  • They do not cascade by multiplication. Two back-to-back S-matrices are not their product, because \(a_2\) of the first is \(b_1\) of the second, not the other way round. Convert to T-parameters (a transfer form), multiply those, convert back. Every RF tool does this for you; know that it is happening.
A dB on an S-parameter is 20 log, not 10 log. S-parameters are ratios of waves — amplitudes — so \(S_{21,dB} = 20\log_{10}|S_{21}|\). The power ratio is \(|S_{21}|^2\), and \(10\log_{10}|S_{21}|^2\) is the same number, which is why it works out. But if you take \(10\log\) of the raw \(|S_{21}|\) you will be exactly a factor of two out in dB, and a 3 dB amplifier will look like 1.5 dB.

5. Worked example: a quarter-wave transformer

A 100 Ω antenna must be driven from a 50 Ω amplifier at 2.4 GHz, on FR4 microstrip.

\[ Z_{0,T} = \sqrt{Z_0 Z_L} = \sqrt{50 \times 100} = 70.7\ \Omega \] From \(Z_{in} = Z_{0,T}^2/Z_L\), setting \(Z_{in} = 50\). The transformer's own impedance is the geometric mean of the two it joins.
\[ \lambda = \frac{3\times10^8}{2.4\times10^9\sqrt{3.0}} = 72.2\ \mathrm{mm}, \qquad \frac{\lambda}{4} = \mathbf{18.0\ mm} \] The electrical length is what matters, so the dielectric constant enters directly. Get \(\varepsilon_{r,eff}\) wrong by 10% and the transformer is 5% too long.
\[ BW = 2 - \frac{4}{\pi}\cos^{-1}\!\left[ \frac{\Gamma_m}{\sqrt{1-\Gamma_m^2}}\cdot \frac{2\sqrt{Z_0 Z_L}}{|Z_L - Z_0|}\right] = 36.7\% \] For \(\Gamma_m = 0.1\), i.e. 20 dB return loss. A single quarter-wave section covers 2.4 GHz ±18%, which comfortably spans the 2.4 GHz ISM band. A larger transformation ratio would narrow this, and that is when you go to two sections.

Without the transformer, \(\Gamma = (100-50)/(100+50) = 0.333\), a 2:1 VSWR, 9.5 dB return loss and 0.51 dB of mismatch loss. The transformer buys back half a dB — which is worth having, but the stronger argument is that the PA now sees the load it was characterised into.

Why a quarter wave inverts

Think of the line as a revolving door with a quarter turn. Whatever you push in emerges rotated ninety degrees. Do it twice — a half wave — and you are back where you started, which is exactly the \(\lambda/2\) rule.

A short at the far end is “zero voltage, maximum current”. Rotate that by a quarter wavelength and it becomes “maximum voltage, zero current” — an open. The transformation \(Z_0^2/Z_L\) is that rotation written as algebra.

Where it breaks: the door turns by a quarter only at one frequency. At twice the frequency the same physical line is a half wave and does nothing at all. Every distributed structure is a frequency-selective device, which is both its limitation and, when you want a filter, the entire point.

Build it in Circuit Toy

A transmission line is a distributed structure, so the way to see it in a lumped solver is to build it out of lumped sections — which is also the most convincing demonstration that the two views are the same physics. Build this in Circuit Toy.

A 50 Ω line as five LC sections, driving a mismatched load

For a 50 Ω line with 5.8 ps/mm of delay, one millimetre has \(L' = Z_0 t_{pd} = 50 \times 5.8\,\mathrm{ps} = 290\ \mathrm{pH}\) and \(C' = t_{pd}/Z_0 = 116\ \mathrm{fF}\). Each section below is 10 mm.

What to run: an AC sweep from 10 MHz to 3 GHz.

What to watch: V(n0) and V(n5). At low frequency the whole thing is a wire and \(V(n0)/V(src) = 100/150 = 0.667\). As the frequency rises the 50 mm of line becomes electrically long and \(V(n0)\) starts to oscillate with frequency — that is \(Z_{in}\) rotating exactly as \(Z_0(Z_L + jZ_0\tan\beta\ell)/(Z_0 + jZ_L\tan\beta\ell)\) predicts. Find the frequency where \(V(n0)\) returns to 0.667: that is where 50 mm is a half wavelength.

Five sections is a coarse model — a lumped ladder only mimics a line up to about \(f = 1/(\pi\sqrt{L'C'})\) per section, above which it behaves as the low-pass filter it actually is. That limitation is not a flaw in the exercise, it is the point: a real transmission line is the limit of infinitely many infinitesimal sections, and you can watch the approximation break down by pushing the sweep higher.
