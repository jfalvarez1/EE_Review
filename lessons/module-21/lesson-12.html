Modern Power Amplifiers: Doherty, Envelope Tracking and DPD

The linearity lesson ended on a number: a class AB amplifier that is 55% efficient at its compression point runs at under 20% when backed off far enough to pass a spectral mask with a 9 dB PAPR signal. It burns four times more power than it delivers, and that is the normal operating condition, not a corner case.

Every technique in this lesson exists to attack that one number, and each does it in a completely different way. Doherty changes the load the device sees. Envelope tracking changes the supply. Digital predistortion changes the signal so the device can be driven harder. They compose, and modern infrastructure uses all three at once.

Three ways to stop wasting fuel

A car engine sized for the steepest hill spends most of its life barely loaded, throttled down, and inefficient. There are three distinct fixes.

Fit a small engine plus a second one that only starts on the hills — that is Doherty. Fit a variable-displacement engine that shrinks to match the load — that is envelope tracking. Or reshape the route so the same engine can be driven closer to full throttle without breaking the speed limit — that is digital predistortion.

Where it breaks: an engine's load changes over minutes. A modern PA's envelope changes in nanoseconds, and that is the whole engineering problem: the second engine must start and stop in a nanosecond, and the supply modulator must slew tens of volts at a hundred megahertz.

1. Why backoff costs so much

\[ \eta_{class\,B}(v) = \frac{\pi}{4}\,v, \qquad v = \frac{V_{out}}{V_{max}} = \sqrt{\frac{P_{out}}{P_{max}}} \] A class B stage's efficiency is proportional to the output voltage, not the power. So it falls as the square root of the power ratio, which is far slower than the power falls — and that is exactly the problem.
\[ 6\ \mathrm{dB\ backoff}: v = 0.501 \Rightarrow \eta = 39.3\%; \qquad 9\ \mathrm{dB}: v = 0.355 \Rightarrow \eta = 27.9\% \] Against a 78.5% peak. Half the efficiency at 6 dB, a third at 9 dB — and modern OFDM signals need 8–12 dB.

Constant-envelope modulation lets the PA sit at compression permanently; the moment the modulation carries amplitude information, the amplifier has to be linear over the whole envelope range, and linear means backed off.

2. Doherty: change the load, not the device

Two amplifiers and a quarter-wave line. The main amplifier is biased class AB and runs all the time. The peaking amplifier is biased class C, so it is off until the drive exceeds a threshold. They combine through a quarter-wave impedance inverter, and the trick is what that inverter does when the peaking amplifier wakes up.

\[ Z_{in} = \frac{Z_0^2}{Z_L} \] The quarter-wave transformer from lesson 6, doing the one job nothing else can: as the peaking amplifier injects current into the combining node it raises the impedance there, and the inverter turns that into a falling impedance at the main amplifier.
\[ \text{Low power: main sees } 2R_{opt}; \qquad \text{Full power: main sees } R_{opt} \] Twice the load means the main amplifier reaches its full voltage swing at half the current — so it saturates, and hits its efficiency peak, at 6 dB below the system's maximum power. That is the entire idea.
\[ \eta_{Doherty}(v) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\pi}{4}(2v) & v \le 0.5 \\[8pt] \dfrac{\pi}{4}\dfrac{2v^{2}}{3v-1} & v > 0.5 \end{cases} \] Two peaks at \(\pi/4\), one at \(v = 0.5\) — the 6 dB backoff point — and one at full power, with a dip to 70.7% between them. Compare with class B's single peak: at 6 dB backoff Doherty gives 78.5% where class B gives 39.3%, exactly double.

What Doherty costs. The quarter-wave inverter is a quarter wave at one frequency, so a classical Doherty is narrowband — typically 5–15% fractional bandwidth, which is a genuine problem for a 5G radio expected to cover several hundred megahertz. It is also non-linear by construction: the class C peaking amplifier turning on is a hard discontinuity in the transfer characteristic. A Doherty amplifier essentially requires digital predistortion to meet any modern spectral mask, which is why the two technologies arrived together.

3. Envelope tracking: change the supply

The waste in a linear amplifier is \((V_{DD} - v_{out})\times i\) dissipated in the device. Envelope tracking removes it at the source: a fast supply modulator moves \(V_{DD}\) up and down with the signal's envelope, so the device always has just enough headroom and never much more.

\[ \eta_{system} = \eta_{PA} \times \eta_{modulator} \] The PA now sits near compression at every power level, so \(\eta_{PA}\) stays near its peak instead of falling with backoff. But everything passes through the modulator, so its efficiency multiplies directly — an 85% modulator caps the whole system at 85% of whatever the PA achieves.
\[ BW_{ET} \approx 3\ldots5 \times BW_{signal} \] The envelope of a modulated signal has several times the bandwidth of the signal itself. A 20 MHz LTE carrier needs a supply modulator good to 60–100 MHz, at tens of volts and amps. That requirement is the whole difficulty, and it is why ET scaled to handsets years before it reached wide-bandwidth infrastructure.

4. Digital predistortion: change the signal

If the amplifier's distortion is known, apply its inverse to the signal before amplifying. The distortion then cancels, and the amplifier can be driven several dB harder for the same spectral mask.

\[ y = f(x) \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{transmit } f^{-1}(x) \text{ so that } y \approx x \] Simple in principle. Hard in practice because \(f\) is not a memoryless function: the PA's response depends on what it was doing microseconds ago, through thermal and bias-network time constants. Those are the memory effects, and a memoryless predistorter cannot correct them.
What DPD needsWhy
An observation receiver with 3–5× the signal bandwidth It must see the distortion products in the adjacent channels — which is the very energy it is trying to remove, and it lives outside the signal band
A memory-polynomial or Volterra model Because the PA has memory. A simple AM-AM/AM-PM lookup handles the memoryless part and leaves the rest.
Continuous adaptation The PA's characteristic moves with temperature, supply, ageing and VSWR. A coefficient set trained once is wrong within minutes.
A PA that is repeatable, not linear The most important consequence. DPD can invert any well-behaved non-linearity, so the analogue design goal shifts from linearity to predictability and efficiency.
This changed what a PA is for. Before DPD, a power amplifier was specified on IP3 and ACPR — on how linear it was. With DPD in the system, those matter far less than efficiency, thermal stability and how well the device's behaviour can be modelled. A PA with strong but smooth and stable compression beats one that is more linear but has hysteresis or long thermal tails, because the first can be inverted and the second cannot. That is a different component specification for the same job, and it is why modern PA datasheets lead with efficiency at backoff.

5. The devices: what changed in industry

TechnologySupplyFrequencyPower densityWhere it is

Why GaN's power density is the whole story: for a given output power you need a certain total gate width, and gate width brings capacitance. Ten times the power per millimetre means a tenth of the periphery, a tenth of the capacitance, and therefore a far easier matching problem and much more bandwidth. GaN did not merely make amplifiers more efficient — it made wideband Doherty amplifiers possible, which is what a multi-band 5G radio needs and what LDMOS could not deliver.

Three more shifts worth knowing. Massive MIMO replaced a few large amplifiers with 64 or 256 small ones, which changed the economics completely: per-chain cost and integration now matter more than per-chain performance. Digital predistortion moved on-chip, into the transceiver, so it is no longer a separate FPGA. And load-modulated balanced amplifiers and other Doherty alternatives are being pursued specifically to escape the λ/4 bandwidth limit — the one thing about the classical Doherty that GaN did not fix.

Abuse A Doherty amplifier operated 300 MHz off its design frequency The impedance inverter is no longer a quarter wave, so the load modulation is wrong: the main amplifier does not see \(2R_{opt}\) at backoff and the peaking amplifier's contribution combines out of phase. The backoff efficiency peak disappears and the amplifier reverts to something worse than plain class AB — worse, because it is now two devices sharing a badly matched load. Nothing is damaged. This is why a Doherty's bandwidth is a headline specification and why so much research goes into escaping it.
Abuse DPD trained at 25 °C, then the amplifier runs at 85 °C The device's gain, compression point and phase response all shift with junction temperature. The stored predistortion coefficients now invert an amplifier that no longer exists, so they add distortion rather than removing it. ACPR can end up worse than with DPD switched off entirely. This is the argument for continuous adaptation rather than factory calibration, and it is why a DPD system needs its observation receiver running all the time, not just at start-up.
Abuse An ET modulator asked for 100 MHz of envelope bandwidth when it is good to 40 The supply cannot follow the envelope's fast excursions, so on peaks the device is left with insufficient headroom and clips. Clipping on the peaks, which is spectral regrowth — and it is not correctable by DPD, because the distortion is caused by the supply rather than by the device's transfer characteristic. Either reduce the signal bandwidth, use a slower "average power tracking" scheme, or specify a faster modulator. Diagnosing it as PA non-linearity and adding DPD effort is the trap.
Abuse A GaN PA presented with a 5:1 VSWR at an unlucky phase while at full power GaN's high power density means a small die dissipating a great deal, so the thermal time constant is short. A mismatch that moves the load line can push the device past its safe operating area in microseconds — far faster than any thermal protection responds. GaN is less forgiving of mismatch than LDMOS precisely because it is smaller for the same power. The defences are a circulator or isolator at the output, a directional coupler with fast foldback, and choosing a device with a stated ruggedness rating at all phase angles. This is a real trade against GaN's advantages and it belongs in the decision.
