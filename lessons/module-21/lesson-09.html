Oscillators and Phase Noise

Every radio needs a local oscillator, and the quality of that oscillator sets limits nothing downstream can recover. A receiver with a perfect front end and a noisy LO will be desensitised by a signal it never even tunes to. A transmitter with a noisy LO fails its spectral mask no matter how linear the PA is. Phase noise is the specification that quietly decides whether a radio works in a crowded band.

Amplitude noise is bounded; phase noise is not. An oscillator's amplitude is stabilised by the very non-linearity that sustains oscillation — grow too large and the gain compresses, pulling you back. Nothing performs that job for phase. A disturbance that shifts the zero crossing is simply remembered, forever, because the oscillator has no reference to compare its phase against. That asymmetry is why phase noise is the specification and amplitude noise usually is not.
A pendulum you cannot stop nudging

A pendulum clock keeps time because the pendulum's amplitude self-corrects: swing too far and it loses more energy, so it settles back. Nudge it sideways and the amplitude recovers within a few swings.

But nudge it along its swing — give it a small push at the wrong moment — and the clock is now permanently a few milliseconds ahead. Nothing brings it back, because the pendulum has no idea what time it is supposed to be. Every future tick inherits that error.

Where it breaks: a real oscillator is nudged continuously by thermal and flicker noise, so the phase performs a random walk rather than taking one step. That is why the phase noise spectrum has a slope rather than a level, and why the slope changes at characteristic frequencies.

1. What phase noise is, as a number

\[ \mathcal{L}(f_m) = \frac{P_{SSB}(f_m,\ 1\,\mathrm{Hz})}{P_{carrier}} \quad\left[\mathrm{dBc/Hz}\right] \] The power in a 1 Hz slice at an offset \(f_m\) from the carrier, relative to the carrier itself. A specification is always a pair: “−120 dBc/Hz” means nothing without the offset it applies at.
\[ \mathcal{L}(f_m) = 10\log_{10}\left\{\frac{2FkT}{P_s} \left[1+\left(\frac{f_0}{2Q_Lf_m}\right)^{\!2}\right] \left[1+\frac{f_c}{f_m}\right]\right\} \] Leeson's equation. Not a derivation from first principles — it is a physically motivated model that fits measured oscillators well. \(F\) is the active device's noise figure, \(P_s\) the power in the resonator, \(Q_L\) the loaded Q, and \(f_c\) the device's flicker corner.

Read the three brackets

TermSetsWhat you change to improve it
\(2FkT/P_s\)The far-out noise floorMore power in the resonator, or a lower-noise device. This is the only term you cannot beat with cleverness — it is thermodynamics.
\((f_0/2Q_Lf_m)^2\)The \(1/f^2\) region, sloping −20 dB/decadeLoaded Q, squared. Doubling \(Q_L\) buys 6 dB everywhere in this region. This is why crystal oscillators are so much better than LC ones and why everything about resonator design is about Q.
\(1+f_c/f_m\)The \(1/f^3\) region close in, sloping −30 dB/decadeDevice flicker corner. BJTs have a much lower \(f_c\) than MOSFETs, which is why discrete low-phase-noise oscillators still use bipolar devices.
\[ \frac{2FkT}{P_s}: \quad F=3\,\mathrm{dB},\ P_s=0\,\mathrm{dBm} \Rightarrow \frac{2(2)(4\times10^{-21})}{10^{-3}} = 1.6\times10^{-17} = \mathbf{-168\ dBc/Hz} \] The floor for a fairly ordinary oscillator. No amount of resonator quality gets you below it; only more power or a quieter device does.
\[ 100\ \mathrm{MHz},\ Q_L=100,\ f_m=10\ \mathrm{kHz}:\quad \left(\frac{10^8}{2(100)(10^4)}\right)^{\!2} = 50^2 = 2500 \ (+34\ \mathrm{dB}) \] So \(\mathcal{L}(10\,\mathrm{kHz}) \approx -168+34 = \mathbf{-134\ dBc/Hz}\), which is about right for a decent LC VCO. At 100 kHz the bracket is \(5^2 = 25\) (+14 dB), giving −154 — the −20 dB/decade slope, visible in the arithmetic.

2. Reciprocal mixing: the reason it matters

Here is the mechanism that turns an abstract dBc/Hz figure into a receiver that does not work.

Your receiver mixes the wanted signal with the LO. But the LO is not a single line — it is a line with skirts of phase noise. A strong interferer at some offset gets mixed by the skirt at that offset, landing broadband noise directly in your IF, exactly on top of the wanted signal.

\[ N_{recip} = P_{blocker} + \mathcal{L}(\Delta f) + 10\log_{10}B \] The blocker's power, attenuated by the LO's phase noise at that offset, integrated across your channel bandwidth.
\[ P_{blk}=-30\,\mathrm{dBm},\ \Delta f = 200\,\mathrm{kHz},\ \mathcal{L} = -120\,\mathrm{dBc/Hz},\ B = 200\,\mathrm{kHz} \]
\[ N_{recip} = -30 - 120 + 53.0 = \mathbf{-97\ dBm} \] Against a thermal floor of \(-174+53+6 = -115\) dBm, that is 18 dB of desensitisation — from a signal in an adjacent channel that the receiver is not even trying to hear.
Nothing downstream can fix this. The noise is created in the mixer, at the right frequency, indistinguishable from signal. No filter, no gain, no processing recovers it. The only defences are a better LO and a sharper preselector — which is why cellular standards specify LO phase noise at particular offsets corresponding to their channel spacing, and why the synthesiser is one of the most expensive blocks in a base station.

3. The topologies, and when each is right

All of them satisfy Barkhausen: loop gain ≥ 1 with 0° (mod 360°) of phase around the loop. What separates them is how they build the resonator and where they tap it.

TopologyResonatorTypical QWhere it is used
ColpittsLC with a capacitive tap50–200The default discrete LC oscillator. The capacitive divider sets the feedback ratio and loads the tank lightly, which preserves Q.
HartleyLC with an inductive tap50–200Rare now — a tapped inductor is harder to make well than two capacitors
ClappColpitts with a series C in the tank100–300Reduces the device capacitance's influence on frequency, so it drifts less
PierceCrystal, with the amplifier as an inverter10k–200kEvery microcontroller crystal oscillator. Cheap, tolerant, and the crystal does all the work.
Cross-coupled LCDifferential LC tank5–20 on-chipThe standard integrated VCO. Differential, so it rejects supply noise, but on-chip inductor Q is poor and phase noise suffers for it.
Ring oscillatorNone — delay stages—Wide tuning range, tiny area, terrible phase noise. Fine for a digital clock, unusable as an RF LO.
Crystal + multiplierCrystal, then ×N—Multiplying by N degrades phase noise by \(20\log N\) — but starting from −170 dBc/Hz leaves room
\[ \text{Multiply by } N:\quad \Delta\mathcal{L} = 20\log_{10}N\ \ \mathrm{dB} \] Phase multiplies with frequency, so phase noise multiplies with it too. A ×100 multiplication from 10 MHz to 1 GHz costs exactly 40 dB. This is the single most important fact about frequency synthesis, and it is why a PLL's in-band phase noise is dominated by its reference multiplied up.

4. Crystals, and the four numbers that matter

ParameterWhat it meansWhat goes wrong
Load capacitance \(C_L\) The capacitance the crystal expects to see. Usually 8, 12, 18 or 20 pF. The commonest crystal fault of all. The circuit's load must match: \(C_L = \frac{C_1C_2}{C_1+C_2} + C_{stray}\), with \(C_{stray}\) typically 3–5 pF of pin and trace. Get it wrong and the oscillator runs at the wrong frequency — a few tens of ppm, which is enough to break a USB or Ethernet link.
ESR The crystal's series resistance at resonance, tens to hundreds of ohms The oscillator's negative resistance must exceed it by a comfortable margin — the rule is 5×. Below about 3× the oscillator may start at room temperature and fail to start at −40 °C, which is the classic intermittent field failure.
Drive level How much power the crystal may dissipate, typically 10–100 µW Overdriving ages the crystal, shifting its frequency permanently over months, and can fracture it. A series resistor from the inverter output limits it, and that resistor is not optional.
Pullability How far the frequency moves per pF of load change What a VCXO or TCXO uses to steer. Also what makes an unmatched \(C_L\) shift the frequency in the first place.
What changed in industry. MEMS oscillators (SiTime and others) have displaced quartz in a lot of consumer and industrial hardware. They are a silicon resonator plus a fractional-N PLL in one package, so they survive shock and vibration far better than quartz, start faster, come in any frequency rather than a catalogue of them, and have no load-capacitance problem to get wrong. What they give up is close-in phase noise: the PLL's contribution puts a floor under them that a good quartz oscillator beats. So MEMS has largely taken the clock market and quartz keeps the RF LO reference market — which is exactly the split you would predict from Leeson.

5. From phase noise to jitter

Data converters and serial links specify jitter, not phase noise. They are the same information in different units.

\[ \sigma_\phi = \sqrt{2\int_{f_1}^{f_2} 10^{\mathcal{L}(f)/10}\,df} \quad [\mathrm{rad}] \] Integrate the single-sideband noise over the band of interest and double it for both sidebands. The limits matter enormously and are set by the application — a serial link's clock recovery tracks slow phase changes, so \(f_1\) is where the CDR's tracking gives out.
\[ t_{jitter} = \frac{\sigma_\phi}{2\pi f_0} \] Convert radians of phase to seconds of time at the carrier frequency.

Worked: a 100 MHz oscillator at −134 dBc/Hz at 10 kHz, falling at 20 dB/decade. For a \(-20\) dB/decade slope the integral from \(f_1\) upward converges to approximately \(\mathcal{L}(f_1)\cdot f_1\):

  • \(\int \approx 10^{-13.4}\times10^4 = 3.98\times10^{-10}\)
  • \(\sigma_\phi = \sqrt{2\times3.98\times10^{-10}} = 2.82\times10^{-5}\) rad
  • \(t_j = 2.82\times10^{-5}/(2\pi\times10^8) = \mathbf{45\ fs}\) RMS

45 fs is a good clock. For context, a 14-bit ADC sampling a 100 MHz input needs about 100 fs to reach its SNR limit, from \(SNR_{jitter} = -20\log(2\pi f_{in} t_j)\) — so this oscillator would just support it. Note the sensitivity: the requirement scales with the input frequency, so the same converter sampling a 1 GHz input needs 10 fs, which is close to the state of the art.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse An 18 pF crystal used with 22 pF load capacitors and 4 pF of stray The circuit presents \(22\cdot22/44 + 4 = 15\) pF where the crystal wants 18. Under-loading pulls the frequency high, typically by 20–40 ppm. Nothing is damaged and the oscillator runs happily. But 40 ppm breaks USB (needs ±500 ppm, so fine), gets marginal for Ethernet (±100 ppm), and fails outright for anything needing ±10 ppm — and it will be blamed on the crystal vendor. Compute \(C_L\) including stray and choose the capacitors from it, rather than copying a reference design with a different layout.
Abuse The board is taken to −40 °C with only 3× negative-resistance margin Crystal ESR rises at low temperature and the inverter's transconductance falls. Margin drops below 1 and oscillation never starts. The product is dead on a cold morning and works perfectly on the bench. This is one of the most expensive field failures in embedded hardware because it is intermittent, environmental, and invisible in every room-temperature test. The 5× margin rule exists precisely for this; verify it by adding a series resistor and testing cold.
Abuse A ring-oscillator-based PLL used as the LO of a receiver in a crowded band A ring oscillator has no resonator, so \(Q_L \approx 1\) and Leeson's second bracket is enormous. Phase noise is perhaps 40 dB worse than an LC VCO at the same offset. Reciprocal mixing desensitises the receiver by tens of dB whenever any strong signal is nearby, while the sensitivity measured in a shielded room is perfect. This is why an RF LO uses an LC or crystal-referenced source and a ring oscillator is confined to digital clocking, where the only requirement is that edges arrive.
Abuse A switching regulator's 500 kHz ripple appearing on the VCO's supply Supply voltage modulates the varactor bias and the device capacitance, so the oscillator is frequency-modulated at 500 kHz. This appears as discrete spurs at ±500 kHz, not as a raised noise floor. A spur is worse than broadband noise of the same total power, because it is coherent: it mixes one specific interferer straight into the channel. Defences are an LDO for the VCO supply, a differential (cross-coupled) topology that rejects common-mode supply movement, and placing the regulator far away. Look for spurs at the switching frequency and its harmonics as a routine check.
