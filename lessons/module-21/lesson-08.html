Linearity: Compression, IP3 and Intermodulation

The previous lesson said the first stage of a receiver decides its noise. Linearity works the opposite way round: the last stage decides it, because everything before has amplified the signal on its way there. Receiver design is the fight between those two facts, and almost every architectural decision in RF is a position taken in that fight.

Nothing is linear; things are linear enough. Every amplifier's transfer characteristic curves eventually. That curvature produces new frequencies that were not in the input, and the ones that matter are the ones that land inside your channel, where no filter can ever remove them.
Two voices in a room with an echo

Two people speaking at once in a normal room are just two voices. Speak louder and the room starts to rattle — a window buzzes, and the buzz is not either voice; it is a product of both, at a frequency neither of them contains.

Turn the volume up by 1 dB and the voices get 1 dB louder, but the rattle gets 3 dB louder, because it depends on the product of the amplitudes. That 3:1 slope is the entire definition of third-order intermodulation, and it is why a system that is perfectly clean at one signal level can be unusable 10 dB higher.

Where it breaks: the rattle is audibly different from the voices, so your ear separates them. Third-order products from two nearby carriers land immediately beside them — sometimes exactly on top of the signal you are trying to receive. There is nothing to separate them with.

1. Where the products come from

\[ v_{out} = a_1 v_{in} + a_2 v_{in}^2 + a_3 v_{in}^3 + \cdots \] A Taylor expansion of any smooth non-linear transfer characteristic. \(a_1\) is the gain; everything after it is distortion.
\[ v_{in} = A\cos\omega_1 t + A\cos\omega_2 t \] Two tones — the standard test, because it is the simplest input that produces the products that actually matter.
\[ a_3 v_{in}^3 \Rightarrow \frac{3a_3A^3}{4}\cos(2\omega_1-\omega_2)t + \frac{3a_3A^3}{4}\cos(2\omega_2-\omega_1)t + \cdots \] The third-order products. Note \(A^3\): amplitude cubed, so power rises 3 dB for every 1 dB of input. And note where they land — \(2\omega_1-\omega_2\) is just below \(\omega_1\), and \(2\omega_2-\omega_1\) just above \(\omega_2\). They sit in the band.
OrderProductsWhere they landCan a filter remove them?
2nd\(2f_1\), \(2f_2\), \(f_1\pm f_2\)Far from the carriers — near DC or near \(2f\)Usually yes. Matters most in direct-conversion receivers where \(f_1-f_2\) lands at DC, in the channel.

Why differential circuits are used. A perfectly balanced differential stage cancels all even-order products, because the two halves produce them in phase and the difference removes them. That is the real reason mixers and modern LNAs are differential: not noise, but second-order linearity. It does nothing for third order, which is why IP3 still has to be designed for.

2. P1dB and IP3

\[ P_{1dB}: \text{ the output power at which gain has fallen 1 dB} \] A real, measurable point. Beyond it the amplifier is visibly compressing.
\[ OIP3 = P_{out} + \frac{\Delta_{IMD3}}{2}, \qquad IIP3 = OIP3 - G \] \(\Delta_{IMD3}\) is how far the third-order product sits below the fundamental, in dB, measured per tone. IP3 is a fictitious point — the amplifier compresses long before reaching it — but it is a useful extrapolation because the 1:1 and 3:1 slopes are reliable well below it.
\[ \Delta_{IMD3} = 2\,(IIP3 - P_{in}) \] The working form. Back off 10 dB from IIP3 and the products are 20 dB down; back off 20 dB and they are 40 dB down. Every dB of backoff buys 2 dB of spurious-free headroom.
\[ OIP3 \approx P_{1dB} + 10\ \mathrm{dB} \] A rule of thumb, not a law — real parts range from about 9 to 15 dB. Use it to sanity-check a datasheet, never to substitute for one.

Worked: an LNA at two tone levels

LNA with \(IIP3 = -10\) dBm and 15 dB of gain, fed two tones at −30 dBm each:

  • \(\Delta_{IMD3} = 2(-10 - (-30)) = 40\) dBc
  • Fundamental out: \(-30 + 15 = -15\) dBm
  • Products out: \(-15 - 40 = -55\) dBm

Now raise the tones by 10 dB, to −20 dBm each:

  • \(\Delta_{IMD3} = 2(-10 - (-20)) = 20\) dBc
  • Fundamental out: −5 dBm (up 10 dB)
  • Products out: −25 dBm — up 30 dB

That is the 3:1 slope in numbers, and it is why linearity problems appear so suddenly. A receiver that is completely clean with a moderate interferer becomes unusable when someone walks closer with a phone.

3. The cascade, and why it is Friis backwards

\[ \frac{1}{IIP3_{tot}} = \frac{1}{IIP3_1} + \frac{G_1}{IIP3_2} + \frac{G_1G_2}{IIP3_3} + \cdots \] All linear, in watts and power ratios. Note the gain appears in the numerator — the opposite of Friis, where it was in the denominator.
Noise (Friis)Linearity
Which stage dominatesthe FIRSTthe LAST
Effect of more front-end gainBetter — suppresses later noiseWorse — drives later stages harder
Effect of a lossy filter firstWorse — its loss is its noise figureBetter — it attenuates the interferer
What you want in frontLow noise, high gainSelectivity, and not too much gain

Every entry is opposite. This is the central tension of receiver design and it has no general solution — only a choice made for a particular band and a particular interference environment.

\[ SFDR = \frac{2}{3}\left(IIP3 - N_{floor}\right) \] Spurious-free dynamic range: the span between the smallest signal you can detect and the largest you can accept before its intermodulation products rise above the noise floor. The factor of 2/3 comes from the 3:1 slope — that is the whole reason the number is smaller than you would hope.

Worked: the cascade that gets worse

LNA \(IIP3 = -10\) dBm with 15 dB gain, into a mixer with \(IIP3 = +5\) dBm:

  • \(IIP3_1 = 10^{-1} = 0.1\) mW; \(IIP3_2 = 10^{0.5} = 3.16\) mW; \(G_1 = 31.6\)
  • \(1/IIP3_{tot} = 1/0.1 + 31.6/3.16 = 10 + 10 = 20\)
  • \(IIP3_{tot} = 0.05\) mW \(= \mathbf{-13\ dBm}\)

The mixer is 15 dB more linear than the LNA and yet contributes exactly as much to the total — because the LNA's 15 dB of gain exactly cancels its advantage. The cascade is 3 dB worse than the LNA alone. Reduce the LNA gain to 9 dB and the cascade IIP3 improves to −11.2 dBm; at the cost, of course, of noise figure. That trade is the job.

4. What this looks like in the field

SymptomMechanismWhere to look
Desensitisation (blocking): sensitivity collapses when a strong signal appears elsewhere in the band The interferer compresses a stage, so its gain drops and takes the wanted signal down with it Preselector attenuation, and the compression point of the first stage that sees the interferer unfiltered
Phantom signals at frequencies where nothing is transmitting Two real transmitters producing \(2f_1-f_2\) that lands on your channel IP3. Identify the two culprits by frequency arithmetic — the answer is usually a pair of nearby broadcast or cellular carriers.
Reciprocal mixing: a strong adjacent channel raises your noise floor The LO's phase noise mixes the interferer into your IF Not linearity at all — this is the synthesiser. See the phase noise lesson.
Transmitter fails its spectral mask at the band edges PA non-linearity spreading the modulated signal into adjacent channels — spectral regrowth, measured as ACPR PA backoff, or linearisation (Doherty, envelope tracking, DPD)
EVM degrades at high output but the spectrum looks fine AM-AM and AM-PM compression distorting the constellation without generating much out-of-band energy The same PA, measured differently. A clean spectrum does not prove a clean constellation.
PAPR is why modern transmitters run so far backed off. An LTE or Wi-Fi OFDM signal has a peak-to-average ratio of 8–12 dB. To keep the peaks below P1dB, the average must sit 8–12 dB below it — and efficiency falls roughly with the backoff. A PA that is 50% efficient at P1dB is under 10% efficient at 8 dB of backoff. That single fact is the entire commercial motivation for Doherty amplifiers, envelope tracking and digital predistortion.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse Two strong carriers appear whose \(2f_1-f_2\) lands exactly on the wanted channel — for example 935 and 940 MHz producing 930 MHz The receiver generates the interference internally. No filter anywhere in the chain can remove it, because it is created after the filtering and lands on the signal. The channel is unusable and nothing in the receiver is faulty. The only defences are more IP3 (a more linear front end, or less front-end gain) and a preselector sharp enough to attenuate the two culprits before they combine. This is why cellular base stations spend so much on front-end linearity: the interferers are their own neighbours' carriers and they are not going away.
Abuse Front-end gain increased from 15 to 30 dB to improve sensitivity Noise figure improves marginally — the later stages were already suppressed. Cascade IIP3 degrades by nearly 15 dB, because \(G_1\) in the numerator of the cascade equation has gone up 30×. Sensitivity improves by a fraction of a dB and the receiver becomes unusable near any transmitter. This is the single most common receiver design mistake, and it is made in pursuit of a specification (sensitivity) that is easy to measure, at the cost of one (blocking) that is not. The right amount of LNA gain is the least that makes the downstream noise negligible.
Abuse An OFDM signal with 10 dB PAPR transmitted at the PA's rated P1dB average power The peaks are 10 dB into compression. The constellation's outer points are squashed inward (AM-AM) and rotated (AM-PM), and the spectrum regrows into the adjacent channels. EVM fails, the spectral mask fails, and the PA runs hot — three symptoms of one cause. The average power must sit at least PAPR below P1dB, which is what “backoff” means. Note that the datasheet's P1dB is a single-tone number and tells you almost nothing about behaviour with a 10 dB PAPR signal.
Abuse A direct-conversion receiver placed near a strong AM-modulated interferer Second-order distortion produces \(f_1 - f_2\), which for a single modulated carrier is the demodulated envelope — landing at DC and low frequency, exactly where a zero-IF receiver's channel lives. This is why IIP2 is a specification for direct-conversion receivers and essentially irrelevant for superheterodyne ones. The defence is differential balance throughout the front end, and often an IIP2 calibration loop in the chip. A superhet's IF is far from DC, so the same product falls harmlessly out of band.
