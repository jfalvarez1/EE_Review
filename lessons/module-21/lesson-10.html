Receiver and Transmitter Architectures

The previous three lessons gave you the three currencies a radio trades in: noise, which the first stage sets; linearity, which the last stage sets; and phase noise, which no stage can recover from. An architecture is a decision about how to spend them, and there are only a handful of distinct answers. This lesson is the map.

There is no best architecture. Each one takes a hard problem and converts it into a different hard problem. Superheterodyne trades an image problem for a filtering problem; direct conversion trades the image away and gets DC offset instead. Knowing which problem you would rather have, for your band and your integration budget, is the whole skill.
Why translate at all

Building a sharp, tunable filter at 2.4 GHz is like building a sieve that can change its hole size while spinning at speed. Building the same sharp filter at a fixed 10.7 MHz is easy — you can buy one for pennies, and it never has to change.

So instead of moving the sieve, the superheterodyne moves the material: shift whichever channel you want down to the one fixed frequency where the good filter already lives. That is the whole idea, and it is why it dominated radio for eighty years.

Where it breaks: the translation is symmetric. Two different input frequencies land on the same output, and the receiver cannot tell them apart afterwards. That second one is the image, and every consequence of the superheterodyne architecture flows from having to deal with it.

1. The image, and why it exists

\[ f_{IF} = |f_{RF} - f_{LO}| \] The absolute value is the whole problem. Both \(f_{LO}+f_{IF}\) and \(f_{LO}-f_{IF}\) produce the same \(f_{IF}\).
\[ f_{image} = f_{RF} \pm 2f_{IF} \] The unwanted input sits \(2f_{IF}\) away from the wanted one, on the other side of the LO. Whatever is there — another service, a spurious emission, thermal noise — lands directly on top of your channel.

Example. Receiving 100.1 MHz FM with a 10.7 MHz IF and high-side injection: \(f_{LO} = 110.8\) MHz, and the image is at \(110.8 + 10.7 = 121.5\) MHz. Anything transmitting at 121.5 MHz — which is the international aviation distress frequency — arrives in your FM channel unless the front end rejects it.

That table is the entire argument that produced double conversion, and it is why every serious communications receiver from the 1930s to the 1990s had two IFs. It is also why modern integrated radios do not: two IFs means two filters that cannot be integrated, and the whole thrust of the last thirty years has been to get the filters onto the chip.

3. Direct conversion's three problems, and their fixes

DC offset

The LO runs at exactly the received frequency, so any leakage from the LO port to the RF port — through the substrate, the package, or radiation from the antenna and back — mixes with itself and produces a DC term. Because it is at DC and the wanted signal is also at DC, it cannot be filtered without removing signal.

  • Static offset (fixed leakage) is calibrated out at manufacture.
  • Dynamic offset is the hard one: the LO radiates from the antenna, reflects off a moving object, and returns with a changing phase — so the offset changes as the environment changes. A servo loop with a high-pass corner well below the channel handles it, at the cost of not being able to use modulation schemes with energy at DC.
  • This is why direct-conversion systems avoid DC-heavy modulation, and why OFDM standards leave the centre subcarrier unused — that null exists specifically so a zero-IF receiver can high-pass its own offset away.

Flicker (1/f) noise

The baseband signal sits from DC upward, exactly where MOS devices are noisiest. Mitigations: use larger devices, use passive (switching) mixers which have far lower flicker than active ones, and choose a modulation whose energy is not at DC. This is a genuine disadvantage with no complete cure, and it is why zero-IF is a poor choice for very narrowband signals.

Second-order distortion

A strong AM-modulated interferer produces \(f_1-f_2\) at low frequency — effectively its own envelope, demodulated — landing in the channel. Hence IIP2 is a headline specification for zero-IF receivers and essentially irrelevant for superheterodyne ones. Fixes: differential balance throughout, and an on-chip IIP2 calibration loop, which most modern transceivers have.

\[ IRR \approx -10\log_{10}\left[\frac{\epsilon^2 + \theta^2}{4}\right] \] Image rejection ratio of a low-IF or image-reject receiver, for a gain error \(\epsilon\) (as a fraction) and a phase error \(\theta\) (in radians). For 1% and 1° (0.01745 rad): \(-10\log[(10^{-4}+3.05\times10^{-4})/4] = 41\) dB.

41 dB is what careful analogue matching achieves, and it is not enough for a demanding standard. That is why modern low-IF receivers calibrate I/Q imbalance digitally and reach 60–70 dB — a purely computational fix to an analogue matching problem, which is the recurring theme of modern radio design.

Direct RF DAC Synthesise the RF digitally and convert it directly Power, and DAC linearity. Increasingly used in base stations and instruments.
What changed in industry. Three things have reshaped radio architecture since 2010. Direct RF sampling moved from instruments into base stations as GSPS converters became affordable, removing the mixer and the IF chain entirely. Massive MIMO and beamforming made the count of transceiver chains the dominant cost, which favours whatever integrates best rather than whatever performs best per chain. And digital predistortion became standard, which changed what the analogue PA has to be: it no longer needs to be linear, it needs to be repeatable and characterisable, so its non-linearity can be inverted digitally. That is a different specification for the same component, and it is why modern PAs are designed for efficiency and memory-effect behaviour rather than for raw IP3.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse A strong transmitter sits exactly on the image frequency of a superhet with only 30 dB of preselector rejection The interferer arrives in the IF attenuated by 30 dB but otherwise intact, superimposed on the wanted channel. If it is 50 dB stronger than the wanted signal it wins by 20 dB. The channel is unusable and no processing recovers it, because the two signals are at identical IF frequencies and are genuinely indistinguishable after the mixer. The only fixes are upstream: a sharper preselector, a different IF so the image lands somewhere quiet, or an image-reject mixer. This is why frequency planning is a real engineering task and not an arbitrary choice.
Abuse A zero-IF receiver held against a metal surface, or a hand moved near the antenna LO leaking from the antenna reflects off the moving object and returns with a time-varying phase. Self-mixing produces a DC offset that changes as the object moves — an offset the factory calibration cannot know about. The baseband wanders, and if it exceeds the ADC's range the receiver goes deaf until it recovers. This is the defining weakness of zero-IF and the reason a DC servo loop is mandatory rather than optional. It also means the high-pass corner is a real design parameter: too low and it cannot track the wander, too high and it removes signal.
Abuse A direct up-conversion transmitter run at full PA output with the VCO at the output frequency The PA's output couples back to the VCO through the substrate, the supply and the package. Being far stronger than the VCO's own oscillation, it pulls the VCO toward its own frequency — and since the PA output is the VCO output, the loop is positive. The synthesiser loses lock, or locks to a shifted frequency, whenever the PA transmits at full power. The symptom is bizarre: the radio works at low power and fails at high power, and the spectrum shows the carrier moving. The structural fix is to never run the VCO at the transmit frequency — use 2× and divide, or 2/3× and mix — which is why almost every integrated transceiver does exactly that.
Abuse A polar transmitter with 5 ns of delay mismatch between its amplitude and phase paths The two components recombine misaligned, so the reconstructed signal is not the intended one. The error appears as wideband spectral regrowth — energy spread into adjacent channels — even though both paths individually are clean. The spectral mask fails and no amount of PA linearity helps, because the PA is faithfully amplifying a signal that is already wrong. Polar architectures need delay matching to a fraction of the modulation bandwidth's reciprocal, which is a calibration rather than a design margin. This is the cost of polar's efficiency, and the reason it is used where efficiency is worth that trouble and avoided where it is not.
