Antennas, and the Board They Sit On

An antenna is the one component in a radio that cannot be bought to specification and dropped in. Its behaviour depends on the board it is soldered to, the plastic around it, the battery behind it and the hand holding it — so a part that measures perfectly on the vendor's evaluation board can be 10 dB worse on yours, with nothing wrong with the part.

This lesson is about why that happens, what is physically fixed and what is yours to choose, and the single hard limit that says a small antenna cannot be both efficient and broadband.

A loudspeaker in a box

A bare loudspeaker driver on a bench sounds thin. Mount it in a cabinet and it sounds full — and the cabinet did that, not the driver. The driver alone cannot couple to the air at low frequencies because it is small compared to the wavelength; the box gives it the size it needs.

A chip antenna is the driver. Your ground plane is the cabinet. The chip is a few millimetres across and could not possibly radiate a 122 mm wavelength on its own — it is a matching structure that couples energy into the board, and the board is what radiates.

Where it breaks: a loudspeaker cabinet is passive and predictable. A ground plane is also the return path for every other circuit on the board, so its shape is decided by considerations that have nothing to do with the antenna — and it is still, whether you meant it or not, the radiating element.

1. Radiation resistance, and what efficiency actually is

An antenna presents a resistance to the transmitter. Part of that resistance represents energy that leaves as radiation, and part is ordinary loss in copper, solder, dielectric and any nearby lossy material. Only the first part does anything useful.

\[ \eta = \frac{R_{rad}}{R_{rad} + R_{loss}} \] Radiation efficiency. This is where the dB go, and no matching network can recover them — a perfect match into a lossy antenna simply delivers all the power into the loss.
\[ R_{rad} \approx 40\pi^2\left(\frac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^{2} \quad\text{(short monopole over a ground plane)} \] Note the square. Halving the length quarters the radiation resistance, while the loss resistance stays roughly where it was. That single fact is why small antennas are inefficient.
\[ G = \eta D \] Gain is directivity times efficiency. A datasheet quoting "2 dBi gain" is quoting the product — and a directivity figure with the efficiency left out is the oldest trick in the antenna business.

They achieve that because they do not radiate alone — the ground plane does, and the effective radiating structure is centimetres across rather than millimetres. Which is why the datasheet specifies a ground plane size and a keepout, and why ignoring those specifications takes you back to the last row.

2. The Chu limit: small, efficient, broadband — pick two

There is a hard bound relating an antenna's size to the product of its bandwidth and efficiency. It comes from the fields themselves, not from any particular design, so no clever geometry escapes it.

\[ ka = \frac{2\pi a}{\lambda} \] \(a\) is the radius of the smallest sphere enclosing the antenna — including everything that carries current, which for a chip antenna means the whole ground plane. \(ka\) is the antenna's size measured in wavelengths, and it is the only variable that matters here.
\[ Q_{min} = \frac{1}{(ka)^3} + \frac{1}{ka}, \qquad BW_{frac} \approx \frac{1}{Q} \] The Chu–McLean bound. Below \(ka = 1\) the cubic term takes over and Q explodes, which is why antennas get suddenly, brutally narrowband once they are much smaller than \(\lambda/2\pi\).

Why this settles the chip-antenna question

At 2.45 GHz, \(\lambda = 122\) mm and \(k = 51.3\) rad/m.

The keepout is not a suggestion. Every chip and trace antenna specifies a region with no copper on any layer — no ground pour, no traces, no plane. Copper in the keepout is in the antenna's near field, where it capacitively loads the element, detunes it downward and absorbs energy. This is the single most common way a working reference design becomes a bad product antenna, and it usually happens because a ground pour was set to fill the whole board and nobody drew the exclusion.

4. Everything that detunes it

An antenna's resonance depends on the total electrical environment. The list below is not exotic — it is the normal contents of a product.

WhatEffectWhat to do about it
Plastic enclosure \(\varepsilon_r \approx 2.5\ldots3\) close to the element slows the wave and pulls resonance down, typically 2–5% Tune with the enclosure fitted. A match measured on a bare board is a match to the wrong thing.
A hand Mostly water: high \(\varepsilon_r\) and genuinely lossy. Detunes and absorbs. 3–10 dB is normal. Cannot be designed away. Place the antenna where a hand is least likely — which is why phone antennas moved to the bottom edge.
The battery A large flat conductor. It becomes part of the ground plane, or a reflector, or a shield, depending where it is. Fix its position early and treat it as board copper for antenna purposes.
A metal enclosure or shield can Ends the discussion. An antenna inside a conductive box radiates nothing. External antenna, or a deliberate slot/aperture designed as part of the antenna.
Ground plane size For anything monopole-like the plane is half the antenna. Changing the board outline changes the antenna. Freeze the outline before tuning, and re-tune if it changes.
Nearby traces and components Loading in the near field. A crystal or a shield can 3 mm away is inside it. Respect the keepout, on every layer.
The only correct way to tune an antenna. Fit a pi network (three placeholders: series, shunt, shunt) between the radio and the antenna feed. Build the complete product — enclosure, battery, display, everything. Measure S11 at the feed with a VNA, on that assembled unit. Then populate whichever of the three positions the measurement asks for. Two of the three will end up as a 0 Ω link or unpopulated, and which two is not knowable in advance. Leaving the pi network off the board to save three placements is how a project loses a month.

5. Worked example: a 2.45 GHz sensor node

A 40 × 25 mm board, chip antenna, +4 dBm transmitter, receiver sensitivity −95 dBm, both ends identical, in free space.

\[ a = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{40^2+25^2} = 23.6\ \mathrm{mm}, \qquad ka = \frac{2\pi(0.0236)}{0.1224} = 1.21 \] Above 1, so the Chu bound is not the limiting factor — bandwidth is available and the design question is efficiency and detuning, not physics.
\[ Q_{min} = \frac{1}{1.21^3} + \frac{1}{1.21} = 0.56 + 0.83 = 1.39 \Rightarrow BW \approx 72\% \] Enormously more than the 3.4% the 83 MHz ISM band needs. The bound is nowhere near binding.
\[ FSPL_{dB} = 20\log_{10}d_{km} + 20\log_{10}f_{MHz} + 32.45 \]
\[ \text{Link budget: } 4 + (-2) - FSPL + (-2) \ge -95 \Rightarrow FSPL \le 95\ \mathrm{dB} \] Taking a realistic −2 dBi for a chip antenna on a board this size, at both ends.
\[ 20\log_{10}d_{km} \le 95 - 67.78 - 32.45 = -5.23 \Rightarrow d \le 0.547\ \mathrm{km} \] About 550 m in free space. Indoors, with walls and fading, expect 20–50 m — and the difference between those two numbers is entirely environment, not hardware.

Now change one thing: ignore the keepout and let the ground pour run under the antenna. Efficiency falls from perhaps 55% to perhaps 10%, which is 7.4 dB — at both ends, so 14.8 dB of link budget. The allowable path loss drops to 80 dB and the free-space range to 100 m. One pour setting, a fifth of the range, and every component on the board exactly as specified.

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse The ground pour set to fill the whole board, covering the antenna keepout Copper in the near field capacitively loads the element and absorbs energy. Resonance shifts down by several percent and efficiency collapses — 55% to 10% is a realistic pair of numbers. Nothing is damaged, S11 may still look acceptable, and the range is a fifth of what it should be. That combination is what makes it so hard to find: the measurement people trust is the one that does not reveal it. Plot the copper layers under the antenna and look.
Abuse The product's board outline shortened by 8 mm late in the mechanical design For a monopole-like antenna the ground plane is half the radiating structure. Changing it changes the antenna's resonance and its pattern — this is not a second-order effect. The tuning done on the old outline no longer applies. Re-measure and re-populate the pi network; a mechanical change to the board outline is an antenna change, and should be treated as one in the change process.
Abuse The antenna transmitted into with no antenna fitted (a broken connector, an unpopulated chip) \(|\Gamma| = 1\). All the power returns to the PA, and depending on the feed length it arrives as a voltage maximum or a current maximum. A low-power radio SoC generally survives; a discrete PA may not. This is the ruggedness case from the transmission-line lesson, and it is a real production scenario — a missing 0402 in the pi network produces exactly it.
Abuse A metal decorative trim ring added by industrial design 4 mm from the antenna A conductor in the near field. It couples strongly, becomes part of the radiating structure, and shifts and distorts both the resonance and the pattern. The antenna must be re-tuned, and may need to move. The general rule is that any conductive object within about \(\lambda/10\) — 12 mm at 2.45 GHz — is part of the antenna whether anyone intended it or not. Antenna position must be settled with the mechanical design, not after it.
