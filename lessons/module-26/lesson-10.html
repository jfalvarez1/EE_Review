Distribution Engineering and Reliability

Distribution is where the grid meets people, and where almost all outages happen. It is radial, it is exposed, and its design is dominated by two questions: how do you hold voltage at the end of a long feeder, and how few customers can you drop when something breaks.

Topology

  • Radial — one source, branching outward. Cheapest, and the default nearly everywhere. Any upstream fault kills everything downstream.
  • Loop (open-loop) — a feeder that can be fed from either end with a normally-open tie. After a fault you sectionalise and back-feed the healthy part from the other source.
  • Network — multiple primaries feeding a common secondary grid through network protectors. Extremely reliable, used in dense urban cores, and expensive.

Voltage regulation and the 120 V base

Every distribution voltage is regulated as though it were 120 V, because that is what the potential transformers deliver and what ANSI C84.1 specifies at the customer:

ANSI C84.1Service voltageUtilization voltage
Range A (normal)114–126 V110–125 V
Range B (infrequent, limited duration)110–127 V106–127 V

Controls available:

  • Substation LTC — tap-changing on the power transformer, with a voltage set point, a bandwidth of ±1–2 V on the 120 V base and a time delay of 30–120 s so it ignores transients instead of hunting.
  • Step voltage regulators out on the feeder, typically ±10% in 32 steps of 0.625% each.
  • Capacitor banks, fixed or switched, supplying VArs locally and raising voltage upstream of themselves.
  • Line drop compensation — the regulator is told the line's R and X so it regulates a point out on the feeder rather than at its own terminals.

Fuse saving versus fuse blowing

Fuse savingFuse blowing
Recloser settingFast trip first, before the fuse meltsDelayed, so the fuse melts first
Transient fault on a lateralCleared with no fuse operation, no truck rollFuse blows; crew must replace it
Cost to the rest of the feederEveryone upstream sees a momentary blinkNo blink for anyone else

The industry has drifted toward selective fuse saving, because commercial and industrial customers increasingly care more about voltage sags disrupting drives and process equipment than about a lateral's restoration time. A “blink” that does not turn the lights off can still drop a data centre's chiller or trip a plant's VFDs.

Reliability indices (IEEE 1366)

SAIFI = total customer interruptions / total customers
SAIDI = total customer-minutes interrupted / total customers
CAIDI = SAIDI / SAIFI

Typical US utility performance, excluding major event days: SAIDI roughly 100–150 minutes/year and SAIFI roughly 1–1.3 interruptions/year. Major event days are reported separately under IEEE 1366's 2.5-beta method, because a single ice storm would otherwise dominate the annual number and destroy any year-over-year comparison.

What actually moves these numbers: vegetation management first, animal guards second, and FLISR (fault location, isolation and service restoration) third. FLISR uses SCADA-connected switches to isolate a faulted section and back-feed the healthy part automatically in seconds — it does not prevent the interruption, but it converts a long outage into a short one for most affected customers, which is a SAIDI improvement rather than a SAIFI one.

Why does going from 4.16 kV to 12.47 kV reduce losses by roughly 9× for the same load?

Current scales as 1/V, and I²R loss as 1/V². (12.47/4.16)² = 9.0. The same conductor carries the same power with one ninth the loss and one ninth the voltage drop — which is why 4.16 kV systems have been converted almost everywhere.

Why does FLISR improve SAIDI more than SAIFI?

SAIFI counts interruptions; SAIDI counts minutes. FLISR cannot stop the fault from interrupting customers in the first instants, so almost everyone still records an interruption (SAIFI barely moves). What it does is restore the un-faulted sections within a minute or two instead of an hour, which removes most of the customer-minutes.
