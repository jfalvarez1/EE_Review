Power Supply with Power Factor Correction

Power Factor Correction (PFC) is mandatory for power supplies above 75W to meet international regulations like IEC 61000-3-2. This lesson covers a complete PFC power supply design: boost PFC stage, LLC resonant converter, and control loops. Understanding the interaction between stages is critical for a robust, efficient design.

Key Insight: Modern PFC supplies use a two-stage approach: boost PFC creates a stable 380-400V DC bus with near-unity power factor, followed by an isolated DC-DC converter (LLC, flyback) for the final output voltage.

Build it in Circuit Toy

The interactives above are models with the physics written into them. To put this circuit in front of a real solver, build it in Circuit Toy from the table below. Node names are just labels — anything sharing a name is one wire.

Boost PFC Stage - Average Model for Control Design

PartWhatValueConnect
XPFCBlock (boost PFC stage)Sub-Circuitpins AC_RECT, BUS, IMON, CTRL (the return goes to the shunt, not straight to ground)
RSHUNTResistor50 mΩbetween IMON and ground
XCTRLBlock (PFC controller)Sub-Circuitpins AC_RECT, BUS, IMON, CTRL, ground
VFCTRLVoltage source1.5 V DC — the LLC frequency command, open loop for this runfrom FCTRL to ground
XLLCBlock (LLC resonant converter)Sub-Circuitpins BUS, OUT, ground, FCTRL

What to run: a transient run of 100 ms in steps of 1 µs.

What to watch: V(BUS), V(OUT), V(IMON), V(CTRL).

The control path is the point of this table and it was the part that used to be missing. XCTRL watches the rectified line and the bus, measures the stage current as a voltage across RSHUNT, and drives CTRL; without that loop the PFC stage is just a boost converter and does nothing for the power factor. The LLC's frequency command is a fixed source here, which means this run closes the PFC loop and leaves the LLC loop open — worth knowing before you read anything into V(OUT)'s regulation.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.
