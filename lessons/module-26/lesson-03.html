Power Transformers, Impedance and Fault Duty

A power transformer's percent impedance is the single number that decides how much fault current reaches everything downstream of it. Pick it too low and you must buy bigger breakers everywhere; pick it too high and you cannot hold voltage under load. The whole design sits in that trade.

Ohmic impedance
Z = (%Z/100) · kV²/MVA
Through-fault current
If = Irated / (%Z/100)
The rule of thumb: a transformer with 10% impedance passes about 10× its rated current into a bolted secondary fault. 5% impedance passes 20×. This is why %Z appears on every switchgear coordination study.

Vector groups and the 30° shift

A delta-wye transformer shifts phase by 30° and blocks zero-sequence current from crossing between windings. Both facts are load-bearing:

  • Dyn11 — delta HV, wye-grounded LV, HV leading LV by 30°. The near-universal distribution substation transformer.
  • The delta traps zero sequence. A ground fault on the LV side draws its zero-sequence current from the LV neutral, not from the HV system. That makes the transformer a zero-sequence source for the LV network and isolates HV ground relaying from LV ground faults.
  • Generator step-ups are delta on the machine side. The delta keeps triplen harmonics circulating in the winding instead of reaching the generator, and the wye-grounded HV side provides the ground-fault current path the HV relays need.
  • You cannot parallel different vector groups. A 30° mismatch between two banks on the same bus is a dead short through the phase difference.

Worked example: fault duty at a distribution bus

A 138 kV source with 2,000 MVA available fault duty feeds a 20 MVA, 138/12.47 kV transformer with Z = 8%, X/R = 20. Find the three-phase fault current on the 12.47 kV bus.

  1. Source impedance on a 100 MVA base: Zsrc = 100/2000 = 0.05 pu.
  2. Transformer on 100 MVA base: 0.08 × (100/20) = 0.40 pu.
  3. Total: 0.05 + 0.40 = 0.45 pu.
  4. Base current at 12.47 kV: 100×106/(√3 × 12,470) = 4,630 A.
  5. Fault current: (1/0.45) × 4,630 = 10.3 kA.

Note the source contributed only 0.05 of 0.45 pu — the transformer dominates. That is generally true at distribution voltages, which is why the infinite-bus approximation (ignoring the source entirely, giving 11.6 kA here) is a reasonable worst case.

From the field: the X/R ratio matters as much as the magnitude. A high X/R means a large DC offset in the first cycles, so the breaker's asymmetrical duty exceeds the symmetrical RMS number you just computed. Breaker ratings are quoted symmetrically; the standard multiplying factors take X/R into account.

Loading and loss of life

Transformer insulation ages thermally. The working rule from IEEE C57.91:

Ageing acceleration ≈ 2(ΔT / 7) for ΔT degrees above rated hottest-spot

So a 20% overload that raises hottest-spot temperature by 14 °C consumes 22 = 4 hours of insulation life per hour of operation. Short emergency overloads are routine and accepted; sustained ones quietly buy a replacement transformer years early.

Check yourself

Why is a lower %Z not simply better?

Lower %Z gives better voltage regulation but proportionally higher through-fault current. Halving %Z doubles the fault duty every downstream breaker, recloser, fuse and cable brace must withstand. The choice is a system decision, not a transformer one.

A 50 MVA, 138 kV, 10% transformer. What is Z in ohms on the HV side?

Z = 0.10 × 138²/50 = 0.10 × 380.9 = 38 Ω.

Why does a transformer differential relay need second-harmonic restraint?

Energising a transformer draws magnetising inrush — several times rated current, flowing into the transformer with nothing coming out. To a differential element that is indistinguishable from an internal fault. Inrush is rich in second harmonic (typically >15–20% of fundamental) while genuine fault current is not, so relays block the trip when that ratio is exceeded.

Two 20 MVA transformers, one 7% and one 9%, are paralleled. How does load split?

Inversely with impedance: the 7% unit takes 9/(7+9) = 56% and the 9% unit 44%. The lower-impedance unit reaches its rating first, so the pair delivers less than 40 MVA before one is overloaded. Paralleling mismatched impedances always costs capacity.
