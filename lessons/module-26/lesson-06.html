Generators, Inertia and the Swing Equation

Grid frequency is a measure of stored kinetic energy. Every synchronous machine on the system is a flywheel electromagnetically locked to every other one; when generation and load fall out of balance, the whole assembly speeds up or slows down together. How fast it moves is set by how much rotating mass is online.

Inertia constant
H = stored kinetic energy (MJ) / Srated (MVA)
Rate of change of frequency
df/dt = f0 · ΔPpu / 2H

H has units of seconds and a direct physical reading: the number of seconds the machine could supply its rated output from rotational energy alone. Typical values: large steam turbine-generators 2–9 s, combined-cycle gas turbines 4–6 s, and grid-following wind or solar inverters effectively 0 s.

The swing equation

(2H/ωs) · d²δ/dt² = Pm − Pe − D(dδ/dt)

Mechanical input minus electrical output accelerates the rotor. During a fault Pe collapses while Pm is unchanged, so the rotor accelerates and δ grows. When the fault clears, Pe returns and the rotor decelerates — but only if there is enough decelerating area left under the power-angle curve. That is the equal-area criterion, and it is what sets critical clearing time: the longest a fault may persist before the machine cannot be pulled back into step.

Why 4–5 cycle clearing is a design target: critical clearing times on a stressed transmission system are typically 6–10 cycles. Relay decision (about 1 cycle) plus breaker interruption (2–3 cycles) has to fit comfortably inside that.

Worked example: ERCOT's design contingency

ERCOT plans against the simultaneous loss of its two largest units, 2,750 MW. Suppose 45,000 MVA of synchronous plant is online with an average H of 2.89 s (which corresponds to the 130 GW·s minimum inertia ERCOT recorded in 2017).

  1. ΔPpu = 2,750/45,000 = 0.0611 pu
  2. df/dt = f0ΔP/(2H) = 60 × 0.0611/(2 × 2.89) = 0.634 Hz/s

At that rate frequency falls from 60.0 Hz to the first under-frequency load-shedding stage at 59.3 Hz in about 1.1 seconds if nothing responds. Governors need a second or more; batteries providing Fast Frequency Response must act within 15 cycles (0.25 s). That timing gap is the entire argument for FFR as a distinct product.

Why this is getting harder: ERCOT tracks system inertia as an operating parameter with a documented critical floor of 100 GW·s, and control-room alarm bands at 120 / 110 / 100 GW·s. Wind and solar serve load without adding rotating mass, so the same MW contingency produces a larger per-unit imbalance on a smaller inertia base — frequency falls faster and further.

Reactive capability and the generator's limits

  • Real power is set by the prime mover — steam valve, fuel flow, gate position. It manifests as torque angle.
  • Reactive power is set by field excitation. Over-excited exports VArs (lagging); under-excited absorbs them.
  • The capability curve bounds this: armature heating limits the MVA magnitude, field heating limits over-excitation, and end-region core heating plus steady-state stability limit under-excitation.
  • Loss of field (device 40) is a real hazard: the machine slips poles and draws large reactive current from the system, dragging down voltage across the area.

Check yourself

H = 5 s and a 0.2 pu generation loss. What is the initial RoCoF?

df/dt = 60 × 0.2/(2 × 5) = 1.2 Hz/s. At that rate you reach 59.3 Hz in under 0.6 s.

Two systems lose the same 1,000 MW. One has H = 6 s over 40 GVA, the other H = 3 s over 40 GVA. Compare.

ΔP = 0.025 pu in both. df/dt = 60(0.025)/(2×6) = 0.125 Hz/s versus 60(0.025)/(2×3) = 0.25 Hz/s. Halving inertia doubles the rate of decline and roughly halves the time available for reserves to arrive.

Why does an inverter-based resource contribute no inertia?

A grid-following inverter has no rotating mass coupled to grid frequency; it measures frequency with a phase-locked loop and injects current at that frequency. There is no physical mechanism by which stored energy is released in response to a frequency change. Grid-forming control can synthesise an inertial response from a battery, which is why ERCOT is moving toward grid-forming requirements for new storage.

Why is fault clearing time a stability parameter, not just an equipment one?

During a fault the machine accelerates unopposed. The longer the fault lasts, the more kinetic energy is added and the larger the angle swing after clearing. Beyond the critical clearing time the decelerating area available under the post-fault power-angle curve is smaller than the accelerating area already accumulated, and the machine pulls out of step regardless of what happens afterwards.
