Field Safety and Practice

Everything in this module so far has been analysis. This lesson is about the part of power engineering where a mistake is not a wrong answer but an injury. The rules below exist because people were killed learning them.

Scope note: this is study material, not a work procedure. Qualified electrical work is governed by your employer's written safety programme, OSHA 29 CFR 1910.269, NFPA 70E, and the NESC. Nothing here authorises anyone to approach energised equipment.

Arc flash

The hazard is thermal, not shock: an arc fault releases energy that burns skin and ignites clothing at distance. Incident energy is computed per IEEE 1584 and drives the PPE selection in NFPA 70E:

The engineering lever is clearing time. Incident energy is roughly proportional to arc duration. A relay that clears in 6 cycles instead of 30 cuts the energy by five — often moving a panel down two PPE categories. This is where protection engineering and worker safety are the same task: a miscoordinated setting is a safety defect, not just a reliability one.

Working de-energised: the sequence that keeps people alive

  1. Switching order. A written, reviewed, sequenced set of operations, executed by an authorised switching operator. Not improvised.
  2. Open and isolate. Breaker first to interrupt current; only then the disconnect switch, which provides the visible break. A disconnect cannot interrupt load current — opening one under load draws a sustained arc, which is why interlocks exist.
  3. Test before touch. Verify absence of voltage with a rated tester, and verify the tester itself before and after.
  4. Apply grounds. Bracket the work location — grounds on both sides — so the worker is inside an equipotential zone regardless of which direction any accidental re-energisation or induced voltage comes from. Grounds must be rated to carry the available fault current for the full clearing time.
  5. Tag and lock. The clearance is held by the crew, and released only in reverse order.
Induced voltage is the one people forget. A de-energised circuit running parallel to an energised one picks up real, dangerous voltage by capacitive and inductive coupling. It reads dead on a proximity tester at the wrong point and is emphatically not. This is why grounding is not optional even on a confirmed-dead line.

Working energised

  • Rubber glove work — typical at distribution voltages, commonly to around 15 kV class, governed by ASTM glove classes 0 through 4.
  • Hot stick — insulated tools maintain the approach distance instead of insulating the worker; used at higher distribution and sub-transmission voltages.
  • Bare-hand / live-line — at transmission voltages the worker is bonded to the conductor and works at line potential, from an insulated bucket or a helicopter. The critical clearance then becomes the distance to grounded objects, not to the conductor.

What actually goes wrong

  • Misidentified circuit. The single most common serious incident: grounding or cutting into the wrong line. Tagging and independent verification exist for this reason alone.
  • Switching sequence errors. Closing into a fault, or opening a disconnect under load. Arc flash follows immediately.
  • Backfeed. A customer's improperly interlocked generator energising a “dead” line during restoration. Rooftop solar and portable generators have made this more common, not less.
  • Vehicle and boom contact. Cranes, dump beds and bucket trucks contacting overhead conductors — frequently involving contractors rather than line crews.
  • Public contact with downed conductors after storms, which is why the first priority in storm restoration is making sites safe rather than restoring load.

These are two different hazards and they do not scale together. Approach distance protects against flashover and grows with voltage. The arc-flash boundary protects against thermal burn and grows with available fault energy and clearing time — a 480 V switchboard with slow upstream protection can have a larger arc-flash boundary than a 138 kV structure. Never reason about one from the other.

Check yourself

Why does the minimum approach distance jump from 5 ft 3 in at 230 kV to 8 ft 6 in at 345 kV?

Because the governing hazard changes. At lower voltages the air gap only has to withstand power-frequency voltage. At EHV, switching a line produces transient overvoltages of several per unit, and the gap must withstand those. That is also why OSHA's current tables let you use a smaller distance if you have engineered and limited the transient overvoltage factor.

Two identical 480 V switchgear lineups. One clears in 6 cycles at 3.5 cal/cm²; the other is miscoordinated and clears in 30. Estimate its incident energy.

Energy scales roughly with time: 30/6 = 5×, so about 17.5 cal/cm² — from PPE Category 1 to well past Category 2, requiring Category 3 (25 cal/cm²) gear. A relay setting error changed what a worker must wear to open that door.

Why ground on both sides of the work location rather than just the source side?

Bracket grounding creates an equipotential zone around the worker. If energisation comes from the far side — backfeed from a customer generator, a mis-operated switch, or induced voltage from a parallel circuit — a single source-side ground leaves the worker between the source and the ground point, which is exactly where the potential difference appears.

Why is a disconnect switch operated only after the breaker has opened?

A breaker is designed to interrupt current, with arc-quenching media and a transient-recovery-voltage rating. A disconnect is a mechanical isolator with none of that; opening it under load draws an arc that will not self-extinguish and can escalate into a phase-to-phase fault at the switch. The breaker interrupts; the disconnect only establishes the visible isolation afterwards.
