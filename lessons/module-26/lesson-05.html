Power Flow, the Power-Angle Equation and Voltage Control

Two buses joined by a reactance is the whole of transmission in one equation. Real power flows because of the angle between the two voltages; reactive power flows because of the difference in their magnitudes. Every operating decision on a transmission system comes back to that separation.

Real power
P = (V1V2/X) sinδ
Reactive power
Q = (V1(V1 − V2cosδ))/X
The decoupling that makes operations possible: P is controlled by angle (generator torque), Q by magnitude (excitation, capacitors, taps). At small δ the two are almost independent, which is exactly what fast-decoupled power flow exploits.

The power-angle curve

P peaks at δ = 90°, where Pmax = V1V2/X. This is the steady-state stability limit. Past 90°, increasing the angle reduces transferred power, so any further mechanical input accelerates the machine and it loses synchronism.

  • Systems are planned to operate well below 90° — typically under 30–45° across a corridor — because a fault must be survivable, not merely the steady state.
  • Lowering X (series capacitors, a parallel circuit, a higher voltage class) raises Pmax proportionally.
  • Raising either terminal voltage raises Pmax proportionally too, which is one more reason voltage support matters for more than equipment ratings.

Solving a real network: Newton-Raphson

A real system has thousands of buses, so the two-bus formula becomes a set of non-linear simultaneous equations solved iteratively. Bus types:

Bus typeKnownSolved forPhysically
Slack / swing|V|, δP, QReference machine; absorbs the mismatch and system losses
PV (generator)P, |V|Q, δGenerator on voltage control, within its VAr limits
PQ (load)P, Q|V|, δLoad bus

Newton-Raphson linearises about the current guess using the Jacobian and converges quadratically — typically 3–5 iterations regardless of system size. Fast Decoupled Power Flow exploits the P–δ / Q–V decoupling above to hold the Jacobian constant, trading a few more iterations for far cheaper ones.

Where it breaks: the decoupling assumes X >> R. On distribution feeders R and X are comparable, and fast-decoupled methods converge slowly or not at all. That is why distribution uses different solvers (backward-forward sweep) than transmission.

Steady-state voltage is held inside the planning envelope: 0.95–1.05 pu pre-contingency and 0.92–1.05 pu post-contingency under NERC TPL-001. A post-contingency deviation greater than about 8% above 100 kV triggers mandatory engineering review.

Check yourself

V1 = V2 = 1.0 pu, X = 0.2 pu, δ = 20°. Find P and Pmax.

P = (1×1/0.2)sin20° = 5 × 0.342 = 1.71 pu. Pmax = 5 pu at δ = 90°. The system is at 34% of its stability limit.

A 345 kV line has X = 40 Ω between two buses both at 345 kV, δ = 25°. Real power?

P = V²sinδ/X = (345,000)² × 0.4226 / 40 = 1,258 MW. Pmax = 345²/40 = 2,976 MW.

Why is operating at δ = 80° unacceptable even though it is below 90°?

Stability is judged against disturbances, not the steady state. A fault momentarily removes the electrical load on the machine; the rotor accelerates and δ swings well past its pre-fault value. Starting at 80° leaves no decelerating area after the fault clears, so the machine goes unstable on a contingency it would survive from 30°. This is the equal-area criterion.

Why does adding a shunt capacitor at a load bus raise voltage there?

It supplies the reactive power the load was drawing through the line. Less Q flowing through X means less I×X drop, so the receiving-end magnitude rises. It does not change δ appreciably, which is the P–Q decoupling in action.
