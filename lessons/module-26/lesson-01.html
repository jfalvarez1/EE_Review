Three-Phase Power and the Per-Unit System

Every number in power systems is quoted in one of two languages: actual volts, amps and MVA, or per unit — a fraction of a chosen base. Per unit exists because a transmission network has half a dozen voltage levels tied together by transformers, and working in volts means dragging turns ratios through every equation. Choose bases correctly and transformers disappear from the arithmetic entirely.

Three-phase apparent power
S = √3 · VLL · IL
Base impedance
Zbase = Vbase² / Sbase
The convention that matters: Vbase is always line-to-line kV and Sbase is always three-phase MVA. Mix in a per-phase quantity by accident and every answer is off by √3 or by 3.

The three-phase relationships

S = √3 VLL IL   (VA)
P = √3 VLL IL cosθ   (W)
Q = √3 VLL IL sinθ   (VAr)
VLN = VLL / √3
S = P + jQ,   |S| = √(P² + Q²),   pf = P/|S| = cosθ

For a balanced system these hold regardless of whether the load is wye or delta — that is the whole point of writing them in line quantities. Balanced also means you can analyse a single phase and multiply by three, which is what a one-line diagram assumes throughout.

A misconception worth killing early

“The source is 345 kV” describes the line-to-line RMS voltage. The peak line-to-neutral voltage a single conductor actually reaches is

Vpk,LN = √2 · VLL/√3 = 0.8165 · VLL

so a 345 kV line peaks at 282 kV per conductor to ground, not 345 kV and not 488 kV. Insulation, clearance and BIL are all sized against that number.

Per unit in four steps

  1. Pick one Sbase for the whole system (100 MVA is the near-universal choice).
  2. Pick Vbase in one zone; every other zone's Vbase follows the transformer turns ratios.
  3. Derive Zbase = Vbase²/Sbase and Ibase = Sbase/(√3 Vbase) in each zone.
  4. Convert every impedance onto the system base.
Zbase = Vbase² / Sbase   (kV²/MVA gives ohms)
Ibase = Sbase / (√3 Vbase)
Zpu,new = Zpu,old · (Snew/Sold) · (Vold/Vnew)²

Because a transformer's per-unit impedance is the same viewed from either winding when the voltage bases follow its turns ratio, the transformer stops being a ratio and becomes a plain series impedance. That is why per unit is not a convenience — it is the reason network solvers are tractable at all.

A generator, its step-up transformer, and a line

Three per-unit zones. Choosing Vbase = 13.8 kV at the generator forces 138 kV across the transformer, so the transformer's own 10% impedance can be used directly once rebased onto the system MVA.

Worked example

A 100 MVA, 13.8 kV generator with X″d = 0.15 pu feeds a 100 MVA, 13.8/138 kV step-up transformer with X = 10% on its own base, then a 138 kV line of j20 Ω. Put everything on a 100 MVA, 138 kV system base and find the three-phase fault current at the far end of the line.

  1. Bases in the 138 kV zone: Zbase = 138²/100 = 190.4 Ω; Ibase = 100×106/(√3×138×103) = 418.4 A.
  2. Generator: already on 100 MVA base → X = j0.15 pu.
  3. Transformer: 10% on 100 MVA / 13.8 kV base; same Sbase and the voltage bases match the turns ratio, so it stays j0.10 pu.
  4. Line: 20/190.4 = j0.105 pu.
  5. Total: j0.15 + j0.10 + j0.105 = j0.355 pu.
  6. Fault current: I = 1.0/0.355 = 2.817 pu → 2.817 × 418.4 = 1,179 A, i.e. a fault duty of 2.817 × 100 = 282 MVA.
Note what did not happen: no turns ratio appeared anywhere after step 1. That is per unit earning its keep.

Field notes

  • 100 MVA is the default base. Every utility short-circuit model you will be handed is on 100 MVA. If a number looks 10× wrong, someone used the equipment base instead of the system base.
  • Transformer %Z is on the transformer's own nameplate base. A 10%, 20 MVA unit and a 10%, 100 MVA unit are wildly different impedances in ohms. Rebase before adding.
  • Fault duty in MVA is base-independent once you are in per unit, which is why breaker interrupting ratings are often discussed in MVA first and amps second.
  • Watch the √3. The single most common error in this subject is dividing by √3 twice, or not at all.

Check yourself

A 50 MVA, 138/12.47 kV transformer has Z = 8%. What is that in ohms on the 12.47 kV side?

Zohm = (%Z/100) × kV²/MVA = 0.08 × 12.47²/50 = 0.08 × 3.110 = 0.249 Ω.

The same transformer, referred to the 138 kV side?

0.08 × 138²/50 = 0.08 × 380.9 = 30.5 Ω. Per unit it is 0.08 on both sides — that is the point.

A 20 MVA transformer with 8% impedance supplies a bolted secondary fault. Roughly what multiple of rated current flows?

1/0.08 = 12.5× rated, ignoring source impedance. Rated secondary current at 12.47 kV is 20×106/(√3×12,470) = 926 A, so about 11.6 kA. The real source impedance upstream reduces this, so treat it as the worst case.

A 4 MW load at 0.80 pf lagging. How many kVAr of capacitors correct it to 0.95?

tan(cos-10.80) = 0.750, tan(cos-10.95) = 0.329.
Qc = P(tanθ1 − tanθ2) = 4000(0.750 − 0.329) = 1,684 kVAr. In practice you would install 1,800 kVAr as three 600 kVAr cans.
