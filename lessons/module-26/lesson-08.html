Distance, Differential and Breaker-Failure Protection

Overcurrent protection has a fatal weakness on transmission: the fault current a relay sees depends on how much generation happens to be online behind it, which changes hour to hour. Transmission protection therefore measures something that does not move — impedance, or the difference between what goes in and what comes out.

Distance protection (21)

The relay computes V/I at its terminal. Because line impedance is proportional to length, that ratio maps directly to distance to the fault — and it is independent of source strength, which is the entire point.

ZoneReachDelayPurpose
Zone 180–90% of the lineInstantaneousPrimary, high-speed
Zone 2120–150%~0.3 sRest of the line + backup for the next
Zone 3Past the longest adjacent line~1–2 sRemote backup
Why Zone 1 deliberately under-reaches: CT and PT errors, line impedance uncertainty and fault resistance all blur the measurement. If Zone 1 reached 100% it would sometimes see past the remote bus and trip instantaneously for a fault that is not on its line. Under-reaching by 10–20% guarantees it never does. The last stretch of line is covered by Zone 2 with a delay, or instantaneously by a pilot scheme.

Pilot schemes remove that delay by letting the two ends talk:

  • POTT (permissive overreaching transfer trip) — both ends must see the fault forward and receive permission from the other end. Secure: if the channel fails, no trip, and the relays fall back to Zone 2 timing.
  • DCB (directional comparison blocking) — trip immediately for a forward fault unless blocked from the far end. Dependable: channel loss still trips. Historically preferred on power-line carrier, because an internal fault often disrupts the carrier on the very line that is faulted.

Differential protection (87)

Sum the currents entering a zone. In a healthy zone they sum to zero; anything else is an internal fault. It is the most selective protection there is — it has no reach setting because its boundaries are the CTs themselves.

Idiff = |I1 + I2|    Irest = (|I1| + |I2|)/2    trip if Idiff > slope · Irest

The percentage restraint slope (typically 25–40%) exists because the differential is never exactly zero: CT ratios do not match perfectly, tap changers move the turns ratio, and CTs saturate at high through-current. All of those errors grow with through-current, so the trip threshold grows with it too.

Transformer differential (87T) adds second-harmonic restraint: magnetising inrush on energisation looks exactly like an internal fault to a differential element, but it is rich in second harmonic (typically >15–20% of fundamental) while fault current is not. Fifth-harmonic restraint similarly blocks on overexcitation.

Breaker failure (50BF)

Breakers do fail to open — a stuck mechanism, a failed trip coil, loss of DC. Since nothing else in the scheme would notice, a breaker-failure timer is started whenever a trip is issued. If current is still flowing after roughly 8–10 cycles, the scheme trips every adjacent breaker that can still feed the fault.

t50BF = tbreaker + tmargin   ≈ 3 cycles + 5 cycles
Why this matters on the ground: a stuck breaker means the fault burns until remote backup clears it — possibly a second or more. That is enough to cause equipment destruction, a much larger outage, and dangerous arc-flash energy at the affected switchgear. Breaker failure is not an exotic contingency; it is planned for on every transmission breaker.

Reclosing (79)

  • Transmission: single-shot, high-speed — typically 15–30 cycles dead time, enough for the arc path to de-ionise. Most transmission faults are transient lightning flashovers. Where the line is fed from both ends, a synchronism-check element (device 25) confirms the two systems are still in step before closing.
  • Distribution: multi-shot with increasing intervals, giving a marginal transient fault more chances to clear itself before the feeder locks out.

The R-X plane — how the relay actually sees the world

A distance relay does not measure distance. It measures the complex ratio V/I at its terminal and plots it on the R-X plane. A fault puts that point somewhere along the line impedance vector; load puts it far out along the resistive axis. The relay's zones are literally shapes drawn on this plane, and the entire art of setting one is making the shapes enclose faults without enclosing load.

Try this: set fault location to 0 and push load to 160%. The operating point walks out along the resistive axis and into the Zone 3 circle — with no fault anywhere on the system. That is load encroachment, and it is the mechanism behind several historic cascading blackouts: a heavily loaded line sags in voltage, the apparent impedance falls into a backup zone, and the relay trips a healthy circuit, shifting even more load onto its neighbours. NERC PRC-023 exists to govern exactly this, and modern relays add load blinders that carve a wedge out of the characteristic.

Zone reach along the protected line

The same settings drawn geographically. Zone 1 stops short of the remote bus on purpose; Zone 2 deliberately overreaches into the next line and waits; Zone 3 reaches further still and waits longer.

Why can't a differential relay simply trip on any non-zero difference?

Because the difference is never exactly zero. CT ratios mismatch, tap changers alter the turns ratio, and at high through-fault current one CT saturates before the other, producing a large spurious difference precisely when the relay must not trip. The percentage-restraint slope raises the threshold in proportion to through-current, so the relay stays sensitive for genuine internal faults and secure for external ones.

A POTT scheme loses its communication channel during an internal fault. What happens?

No permissive signal arrives, so the high-speed trip is blocked. The fault is still cleared, but by Zone 2 timing — roughly 0.3 s instead of one or two cycles. POTT fails secure (no false trip) but not dependable (delayed real trip). DCB makes the opposite trade.

Why is Zone 3 sometimes considered a liability?

Zone 3 reaches far, and under heavy load with depressed voltage the apparent impedance V/I can fall inside the Zone 3 characteristic even with no fault present. Several historic cascading blackouts involved Zone 3 elements tripping healthy lines on load encroachment. Modern practice adds load-encroachment blinders or omits Zone 3 entirely, and NERC PRC-023 exists specifically to govern this.
