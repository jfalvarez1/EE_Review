Line Impedance, Structures and Insulation

Once the conductor and the span are chosen, the geometry of the structure decides the line's electrical parameters. R, X and B are not looked up — they fall out of where you put the phases and how far apart they are.

Series reactance
XL = 0.2794 log10(GMD/GMR) Ω/mi
Shunt susceptance
B = 5.79×10-6 / log10(GMD/r) S/mi

GMD is the geometric mean distance between phases, GMR the geometric mean radius of the conductor (or bundle), and r the physical outside radius. Both formulas are per phase, per mile, at 60 Hz.

GMD = cbrt(Dab · Dbc · Dca)

Phase configuration

The three common arrangements. Horizontal needs the widest right-of-way and the shortest structure; vertical (delta) is the opposite; double-circuit stacks two three-phase sets on one structure.

ConfigurationGMDTrade-off
Horizontal (flat)1.26 × phase spacingWidest ROW, lowest structure, easiest to build and maintain
Delta (triangular)= phase spacing if equilateralNaturally balanced, no transposition needed, compact
Vertical1.26 × spacingNarrowest ROW, tallest structure
Double circuit verticalComplex; use low-reactance phasingTwo circuits on one ROW, but a single structure failure takes both
Transposition. On a flat or vertical configuration the three phases do not see identical geometry, so their impedances differ and the line introduces unbalance. Rotating the phase positions in thirds along the route — transposition — averages it out. It is expensive, and on modern short lines utilities frequently accept the small unbalance instead. On long EHV lines it is still done.

A standard suspension disc is 5¾ in × 10 in and contributes roughly 15 kV of dry flashover each in a string. Add discs for contaminated environments (coastal salt, industrial, agricultural dust) and for altitude, where air density is lower.

  • Shield wires (overhead ground wires) run above the phases to intercept lightning, typically at a 30° shielding angle or less. They also carry a share of ground-fault current back to the source, which is why they matter for zero-sequence impedance.
  • Footing resistance decides whether a lightning strike to the shield wire causes a back-flashover onto the phase. Below about 10–25 Ω is the usual target; counterpoise wire is buried where soil resistivity is poor.
  • Structure types: lattice steel for long spans and heavy loading, tubular steel poles for narrow ROW and faster construction, wood for distribution and low-voltage transmission, concrete where corrosion is severe.

Check yourself

Flat configuration, 28 ft between adjacent phases. What is the GMD?

Dab = Dbc = 28 ft, Dca = 56 ft.
GMD = ∛(28 × 28 × 56) = ∛43,904 = 35.3 ft, which is the 1.26 × spacing rule.

Drake, GMR 0.0375 ft, GMD 35.3 ft. Find XL per mile.

XL = 0.2794 log10(35.3/0.0375) = 0.2794 log10(941) = 0.2794 × 2.974 = 0.831 Ω/mi.

Why does bundling reduce reactance?

X depends on log(GMD/GMR), and bundling raises the bundle's effective GMR substantially — two Drake sub-conductors 18 in apart give GMRb = √(0.0375 × 1.5) = 0.237 ft, over six times the single-conductor value. That drops the log term and typically cuts reactance by 25–30%, which raises SIL by the same proportion.

Why does a lightning strike to the shield wire sometimes flash over to a phase?

The strike current flows down the structure into the footing resistance, raising the whole structure's potential by I×R. With 30 kA into a 30 Ω footing that is 900 kV on the tower, which can exceed the insulator string's withstand from the tower side and flash backwards onto the phase conductor. Lowering footing resistance is the fix, which is why counterpoise is buried in rocky soil.
