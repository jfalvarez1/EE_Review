Case Study: Automotive ECU Power System

Design a robust power supply for an automotive ECU that must survive load dumps, cold cranking, and reverse polarity. This scenario covers the harsh automotive electrical environment and protection strategies.

Key Challenge: The module has to work from 6 V during cranking and still be alive after a load dump, which reaches 65–87 V unsuppressed on a 12 V system. A TVS, or a central suppressor at the alternator, brings that down to about 40 V at the input pin, so the regulator sees roughly a 6:1 range — and that is what rules out most linear regulators. Keep the two numbers straight: 87 V is what the standard says, and 40 V is a design choice you have to make happen.

The Scenario

Requirement: Design power system for body control module.

  • Input: 12V nominal, ISO 7637-2 transient protection
  • Outputs: 5V @ 500mA (MCU), 3.3V @ 200mA (CAN/LIN), 8V @ 50mA (sensors)
  • Operating temp: -40°C to +85°C
  • Must survive cranking down to 6V
  • Load dump: 65–87 V unsuppressed for 40–400 ms (ISO 16750-2, 12 V system), clamped to no more than 40 V at the module input

Why each front-end part is wired the way it is

The reverse-polarity FET conducts at power-up through its body diode, which lifts its source to the battery; Rgate holds the gate at ground, so VGS is about −12 V and the channel shorts the diode out. Dgs holds the gate within 12 V of the source, because during a 40 V event an unclamped gate would see the whole rail, beyond the ±20 V a typical gate oxide is rated for. Reverse the battery and the body diode is reverse-biased while VGS is zero: nothing flows. A gate resistor to the FET’s own source instead of to ground, as an earlier version of this table had it, holds VGS at zero in both cases and leaves the whole load on the body diode.

The TVS reads the diagram’s “33 V” as a stand-off: Vishay’s SM8S33A breaks down at 36.7 V minimum and clamps at 53.3 V at 124 A, modelled as BV = 36.7 V and rz = (53.3 − 36.7)/124 = 0.13 Ω. Its cathode goes to the rail; one unidirectional part is enough because the FET already blocks reverse battery. In a Test B suppressed load dump the bus sits near 35 V, below that breakdown, so the TVS stays off and the regulator must take 35 V directly. Its job is the faster pulses: ISO 7637-2 pulse 2a at level IV adds 50 V to the 13.5 V supply behind 2 Ω, and with about 1.3 A of load the clamp holds roughly 38.3 V with 11 A in the TVS for 50 µs. That meets the 40 V target; an unsuppressed 87 V dump at low Ri does not, which is why this design assumes central suppression.

The series element is an inductor, not a resistor. An earlier version of this page put 10 Ω in the supply path. At 6 V cranking the 5 V, 1 A buck draws about 1 A at full load, so 10 Ω would drop the 6 V the module must run from; even at 13.5 V it would waste about 2 W. Series resistance belongs on signal lines. Here 10 µH with Cin forms an LC filter for conducted emissions and fast transients with almost no DC drop. Any input LC filter ahead of a switching regulator needs damping, because the regulator’s input looks like a negative resistance; the electrolytic’s ESR usually provides it, so check its value rather than assume it.

Bench acceptance: verify reverse battery first, from a current-limited supply at −14 V: input current should be leakage only. At 6 V and full load, measure the drop across the FET and confirm the regulator stays in regulation. Then apply pulse 2a and a suppressed load dump from a generator with stated US and Ri, recording V(prot), V(filtered) and the FET’s VGS together.

What this build cannot tell you: Circuit Toy’s MOSFET has no body diode, so the power-up path the FET depends on is absent from the solve; the TVS is a straight-line fit with no energy limit; and the regulator blocks stand in for parts whose input-voltage ratings you must take from their own datasheets.

Reference: Vishay SM8S10A thru SM8S43A datasheet (document 88387), SM8S33A row. It does not endorse this build.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.
