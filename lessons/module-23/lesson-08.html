Class AB Output Stage

The numerical case is an illustrative troubleshooting exercise. Use the actual unit's service manual for expected voltages and bias targets.

Complementary emitter-follower output stage. Both drive ports connect to the preceding amplifier stage, which must establish the DC bias-current path through Q3; they are not interchangeable signal inputs. Q3 and R1/R2 form a VBE multiplier. Emitter resistors provide local feedback, but do not alone guarantee thermal stability. ADI: class B/AB bias and thermal behavior.

Neglecting Q3 base current, Vbias ≈ VBE3(1 + R1/R2). At idle, it must cover both output VBE drops plus Iq(RE1 + RE2). Measure across one emitter resistor to obtain that device's idle current; use the amplifier manufacturer's specified adjustment procedure.

Test the bias-network hypothesis

Interpretation: Low emitter-resistor voltage and too little bias-spreader voltage point toward the bias network; they do not by themselves prove Q3 has degraded.

Check the bias current path, R1/R2 and any adjustment potentiometer, solder joints, and Q3 junctions against the actual schematic. Compare temperature behavior with the service procedure before assigning a failure cause.

Calculated example: 15 mV across a 0.33 Ω emitter resistor corresponds to 45.5 mA. It is a measurement conversion, not a universal bias target.

How the table maps onto the diagram

Q3, R1 and R2 are the VBE multiplier between the upper and lower drive nodes, d1 and d2. The diagram leaves the preceding stage out; here it is two ideal parts. Ibias is the driver stage’s current-source load, which pushes the bias current down through Q3. Vdrive is the voltage-amplifier stage that sinks it: it carries the signal, and moves the whole bias network up and down with it. Its −0.755 V offset puts the output at idle near 0 V; in an amplifier, global feedback sets that level. An earlier version of this table drove both output bases from emitter followers and shorted Q3’s base to its emitter, so no bias current could flow and every transistor sat at cut-off.

What a correct build reads. Both transistors use the generic models: IS = 10 fA and β = 100. Q3 carries about 8.9 mA, so its VBE is 0.711 V. With R1 = R2 the formula above gives 2 × 0.711 = 1.42 V; the solver gives 1.511 V, because Q3’s 89 µA of base current also flows in R1, adding 89 mV. At β = 100 that is not negligible. The two output junctions take 0.745 V each, leaving 10.8 mV across each 0.33 Ω emitter resistor: about 33 mA of idle current. That is in the 10–20 mV window the measurement table above calls normal.

The bias is exponentially sensitive. Raise R1 by 10%, to 1.1 kΩ, and the idle current rises 2.4 times, to about 78 mA. Lower R1 to 470 Ω and the spread falls to 1.09 V, about 0.4 V short of the two output junctions. At idle Q4 is cut off and Q5 barely conducts, and between Vdrive = −0.7 V and −0.4 V the output moves 37 mV where it should move 280 mV. That flat step through zero is the crossover distortion this case study starts from. It is why a bias trimmer is adjusted slowly with the amplifier at operating temperature, and why Q3 is mounted on the output heatsink.

Bench acceptance: follow the service manual. Typically you warm the amplifier at idle with no signal, measure millivolts across one emitter resistor, and trim in small steps, letting the reading settle after each one. Check both channels, and recheck after the heatsink has reached its steady temperature.

What this build cannot tell you: the generic models have no temperature, so the solver cannot show thermal drift or runaway; the absolute idle current depends on IS, so another model gives another number; and the ideal driver stage cannot run short of current except through Ibias. At a 4 V peak Q4 already needs about 5 mA of its 10 mA; drive harder and the positive half clips first.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.
