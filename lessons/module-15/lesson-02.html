Cell Balancing Circuits

Multi-cell battery packs require balancing to prevent overcharge of individual cells and maximize usable capacity. Learn passive and active balancing topologies and design trade-offs.

Why Balance? Cells have slight capacity differences. During charge, weaker cells reach 4.2V first. Without balancing, charging must stop early, leaving stronger cells undercharged and reducing pack capacity.

Balancing Circuit Schematics

Passive: bleed the high cell

A resistor across the cell, switched in when that cell runs ahead of the others. The energy becomes heat, which is why passive balancing currents are small — typically 50–200 mA — and why it can only balance down, never up.

Q1 is one of the balance FETs; a real pack has one per cell. Rbal sets the current, \(I = (V_{cell} - V_{DS})/R_{bal}\), and dissipates all of it: at 4.2 V through 33 Ω that is 127 mA and 0.53 W per cell, which is the thermal limit that actually decides the resistor value.

Active: move charge from the high cell to the low one

A flying capacitor is switched across the high cell, then across the low one, transferring charge rather than burning it. Balancing currents of an amp or more become practical, and the pack can balance in either direction.

S1 and S2 never close together — that would short the two cells through Cfly. Phase 1 closes S1 and Cfly charges toward the higher cell; phase 2 closes S2 and it discharges into the lower one. Charge moved per cycle is \(Q = C_{fly}\Delta V\), so the balancing current is \(I = f\,C_{fly}\Delta V\) — it falls to zero as the cells converge, which is the behaviour you want.

Integrated: the AFE does all of it

A monitor IC with the balance FETs on-die. It measures every cell, decides which to bleed, and switches its own internal FETs — so the board carries one IC, one resistor per cell, and the sense wiring.

Note the external Rbal: even an IC with internal balance FETs puts the resistor outside, because the resistor is where the heat goes and the die is the last place you want it. ICs with fully internal balancing exist and are limited to tens of milliamps for exactly that reason.
