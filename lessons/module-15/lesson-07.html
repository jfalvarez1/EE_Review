Pack Architecture: Series, Parallel and the C-Rate

A cell is a fixed thing. It has one voltage, one capacity and one internal resistance, and no amount of design changes any of them. What you choose is how many, in what arrangement — and that single choice sets your pack voltage, your runtime, your voltage sag, your heat, and whether the pack is a nuisance or a fire.

This lesson is about the arithmetic of that choice, and about the one number datasheets bury and beginners ignore: the cell's internal resistance, which is what actually separates a “high-discharge” cell from an ordinary one.

Series and parallel, physically

Think of each cell as a pump with a narrow pipe attached. The pump sets the pressure it can raise; the pipe's narrowness is the cell's internal resistance.

In series you stack pumps end to end. The pressures add — you get a higher voltage — but so do the narrow pipes, so the resistance adds too. In parallel you put pumps side by side. The pressure is unchanged, but you now have several pipes carrying the flow, so the resistance divides.

Where it breaks: pumps do not care what their neighbours are doing. Cells in parallel are welded to one another and will forcibly equalise — a fully charged cell paralleled with an empty one dumps current into it with nothing but the two internal resistances limiting it. That is why cells are matched before they are paralleled, and it has no analogue in the plumbing picture.

1. The notation, and what it buys you

Packs are written <S>S<P>P: S cells in series, and each of those series positions made of P cells in parallel. A 13S4P pack is 52 cells: thirteen groups of four.

\[ V_{pack} = S \cdot V_{cell}, \qquad Q_{pack} = P \cdot Q_{cell} \] Series multiplies voltage, parallel multiplies charge. Neither one alone changes the other.
\[ E_{pack} = S \cdot P \cdot V_{cell} Q_{cell} \] Energy depends only on the total cell count. 4S2P and 2S4P from the same cell store exactly the same watt-hours. The arrangement decides how that energy is delivered, not how much there is.
\[ R_{pack} = R_{cell}\frac{S}{P} \] The number that matters most and gets checked least. Series stacks resistance; parallel divides it.

Since energy is fixed by cell count, the only reasons to prefer one arrangement over another are the ones below.

Push toward more seriesPush toward more parallel
Lower current for the same power, so thinner wire, smaller connectors and less \(I^2R\) everywhere — including in the cells themselves. Lower pack resistance, so less sag under load and a flatter voltage at the terminals.
The rail you need may already be up there. A 48 V bus from a 13S pack needs no boost converter at all. Each cell shares the current, so ordinary energy cells can serve a high-current load.
Motor and inverter efficiency generally improve at higher voltage for the same power. Cells in parallel self-balance, so only \(S\) voltages need monitoring, not \(S \times P\).
But: every series cell needs its own sense wire and balance channel, and above ~60 V you cross into regulatory territory that changes the whole product. But: paralleling needs matched cells, and a shorted cell in a parallel group is fed by its siblings with no fuse in the way unless you put one there.

2. The arrangements you will actually meet

“Nominal” is the voltage the pack spends most of its discharge near, and it is what the pack is named after. It is never the voltage your electronics must survive — that is the full column — nor the voltage they must still work at, which is the empty column. Designing to the nominal number is the single most common beginner mistake in battery-powered hardware.

3. The C-rate

C-rate is current expressed as a multiple of the cell's own capacity. It exists because a 200 mAh coin cell and a 200 Ah prismatic cell behave identically at the same C-rate and completely differently at the same current, so it is the only way to state a discharge specification that travels across cell sizes.

\[ \text{C-rate} = \frac{I}{Q} \quad\text{with } Q \text{ in ampere-hours} \] A 3000 mAh cell at 3 A is at 1C. The same 3 A from a 1500 mAh cell is 2C, and is twice as hard on the cell despite being the same current.
\[ t_{run} \approx \frac{1}{\text{C-rate}} \ \text{hours} \] 1C for one hour, 0.5C for two, 2C for half. Approximate because capacity itself falls as the rate rises.

Read the burst rating sceptically. A pouch cell marked “50C” is claiming 150 A from 3 Ah. If it were true for more than a few seconds, at even 2 mΩ the cell would be dissipating \(150^2 \times 0.002 = 45\ \mathrm{W}\) inside a package the size of a paperback. It is a marketing number for a ten-second pulse. Size the pack on the continuous rating and treat burst as headroom, not as capability.

4. High-discharge versus high-energy: it is all internal resistance

Two 18650 cells look identical. One holds 3500 mAh and is rated 10 A; the other holds 2600 mAh and is rated 30 A. The difference is not magic and it is not a marketing tier — it is electrode thickness. Thick electrodes hold more lithium and store more energy; thin electrodes give ions a shorter path and so present a lower resistance. You cannot have both, and the resistance is what you are buying.

\[ V_{term} = V_{OC}(SOC) - I R_{cell} \] Sag. It is not a loss of charge — the voltage comes back the instant the load goes away — but while the load is on, this is what your circuit actually sees.
\[ P_{cell} = I^2 R_{cell} \] Heat, inside a sealed can with no heatsink. This is the number that decides whether the pack needs cooling or a fire extinguisher.
\[ \Delta T \approx P_{cell} \cdot \theta_{cell},\qquad \theta_{18650} \approx 25\ \mathrm{^\circ C/W} \ \text{in still air} \] A bare 18650 has about 42 cm² of surface. With natural convection at roughly 10 W/m²K that is 25 °C per watt. Two watts is warm. Five is a problem.

The 125 °C in the first row is the whole lesson. That cell is being run at its own datasheet rating, and in free air it would cook itself. Nobody runs one cell at 10 A; they run four in parallel at 40 A, which is the same 10 A each — so the heat per cell is unchanged, and the pack still needs to get 20 W out. The parallel arrangement bought current capability, not thermal relief.

Why thick electrodes are slow

A high-energy cell is a deep warehouse with one loading door: enormous storage, but everything has to travel the length of the building to get out. A power cell is a shallow warehouse with the same door: far less stock, but nothing is more than a few steps from the exit.

Lithium ions must physically diffuse through the electrode to the separator. Double the electrode thickness and you roughly double the stored charge, but you also roughly double the distance every ion travels — which shows up at the terminals as internal resistance.

Where it breaks: resistance is not only diffusion. Tab welds, the can, the separator and the electrolyte all contribute, and a badly built power cell can be worse than a well-built energy cell. Measure DCIR; do not infer it from the marketing category.

5. Worked example: a 48 V e-bike pack

The requirement: 500 W continuous, 1500 W on a hill, 40 km of range at about 12 Wh/km, and a controller that expects a nominal 48 V bus and cuts out below 40 V.

\[ E_{req} = 40\,\mathrm{km} \times 12\,\mathrm{Wh/km} = 480\ \mathrm{Wh} \] Before any derating. Real packs are sized 15–25% above this because the last 10% of capacity is not usable and the pack fades with age.
\[ S = \frac{48}{3.7} = 12.97 \Rightarrow \mathbf{13S} \] Nominal 48.1 V, full 54.6 V, empty 39 V. Check the empty figure against the controller's 40 V cutoff: the pack reaches the controller's limit slightly before its own, so the controller is what ends the ride. Acceptable, and arguably desirable — it keeps the cells off their floor.
\[ P = \left\lceil \frac{480\ \mathrm{Wh}}{13 \times 3.7\,\mathrm{V} \times 3.0\,\mathrm{Ah}} \right\rceil = \left\lceil \frac{480}{144.3} \right\rceil = \mathbf{4P} \] 13S4P of 3000 mAh cells: 52 cells, 12 Ah, 577 Wh. The 20% over the requirement is the derating, not a mistake.
\[ R_{pack} = 25\,\mathrm{m\Omega} \times \frac{13}{4} = 81.3\ \mathrm{m\Omega} \]

A constant-power load does not draw a fixed current, so \(I = P/V_{OC}\) is the wrong sum. The terminal voltage and the current settle together:

\[ \frac{V(V_{OC}-V)}{R_{pack}} = P \;\Rightarrow\; V = \frac{V_{OC} + \sqrt{V_{OC}^2 - 4PR_{pack}}}{2} \] Take the larger root — the smaller one is the high-current solution the supply cannot hold. When the discriminant goes negative there is no operating point: the pack cannot deliver that power at that state of charge, at any current.

Solving for the cutoff exactly: the terminals reach 40 V when \(V_{OC} = 43.05\) V, which is 3.31 V per cell — roughly 7% state of charge. So the pack loses its hill capability with about 7% of its energy still in it, while cruise power stays available to nearly 0%. Whether that matters is a product decision, not an electrical one; if it does, 13S5P (65 cells, 65 mΩ) moves the cutoff down to about 3% and 13 mΩ power cells would remove it entirely at the cost of a third of the capacity.

6. Lead-acid, and the LiFePO4 that replaces it

Lead-acid is not legacy — it still starts nearly every internal combustion engine on earth, and it is still the default for UPS and standby. It has two properties nothing else matches: it tolerates a float charge indefinitely, and it delivers enormous cranking current from a cheap cell. It also has properties that make it awful everywhere else.

StagePer cell12 V batteryWhat is happening

Cutoff1.75 V10.5 VBelow this, sulfation is permanent

Two lead-acid facts that catch people out:

  • Capacity is quoted at the 20-hour rate (0.05C). A “100 Ah” battery gives 100 Ah at 5 A. Draw 100 A from it and you will get about 45 Ah, not 100. This is Peukert's effect, and it is far stronger in lead-acid (\(k \approx 1.25\)) than in lithium (\(k \approx 1.05\)).
  • Charge voltage needs temperature compensation of about −4 mV/°C per cell, so −24 mV/°C on a 12 V battery. A charger set for 25 °C will chronically overcharge a battery in a hot engine bay and chronically undercharge one outdoors in winter.
\[ t = H\left(\frac{Q}{I \cdot H}\right)^{k} \] Peukert. \(H\) is the rating period (20 h), \(Q\) the rated capacity, \(k\) the Peukert exponent. At \(k=1\) it reduces to the ideal \(t = Q/I\); every real battery has \(k>1\).

The 4S LiFePO4 drop-in

Four LiFePO4 cells give 12.8 V nominal and charge to 14.6 V — almost exactly a lead-acid battery's window. That coincidence is the entire reason LiFePO4 took over the replacement market. It is not a perfect substitution:

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse A 4S Li-ion pack charged by a 5S charger (21 V into a 16.8 V pack) Every cell is driven past 4.2 V toward 5.25 V. Above about 4.3 V the electrolyte begins to oxidise and lithium plates on the anode; the cell gasses, swells, and can go into thermal runaway. The BMS overvoltage comparator must open the charge FET before any cell exceeds 4.25 V. This is not a nice-to-have: a charger is the one thing in the system with the authority to push energy in, and mismatched chargers are how most consumer lithium fires start.
Abuse Terminals shorted with a spanner on the 13S4P example 81 mΩ of pack against milliohms of spanner: \(48/0.085 \approx \mathbf{560\ A}\). Each cell delivers 140 A and dissipates \(140^2 \times 0.025 = \mathbf{490\ W}\) internally. The nickel strip vaporises, the spanner glows, and any cell that stays connected goes into runaway within seconds. A fuse in the main path rated well below the strip's fusing current, and a BMS short-circuit detector with a sub-millisecond response. Neither alone is sufficient — a fuse is too slow to protect a semiconductor, and the BMS FET is destroyed by the fault it is trying to interrupt if the fuse is not there to back it up.
Abuse A shorted cell inside one parallel group The other three cells in that group now discharge into it through the busbar, with only \(25/3 = 8.3\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\) plus the strip limiting them. That is hundreds of amps inside the pack, where no main fuse can see it, because the pack terminals show nothing unusual. This is the argument for per-cell fusible links — a deliberately thin section of nickel to each cell that opens at a few tens of amps. It costs a little resistance and removes the one failure mode that a pack-level fuse is blind to.
Abuse LiFePO4 drop-in charged at −10 °C by a lead-acid charger Lithium cannot intercalate fast enough into cold graphite, so it plates as metal on the anode surface. The plating is permanent, it consumes capacity, and the dendrites it forms can eventually pierce the separator. The BMS must refuse charge below 0 °C — the battery cannot rely on the charger, because the charger it is replacing had no such concept. A drop-in that omits this will pass every bench test and fail its first winter.
Abuse A fully charged cell paralleled with a fully discharged one during assembly 4.2 V against 3.0 V through \(2 \times 25\ \mathrm{m\Omega}\): \(1.2/0.05 = \mathbf{24\ A}\) at the instant of contact, with no load and no switch in the circuit. Cells must be brought to the same voltage — typically all to storage charge, around 3.7 V — before they are welded in parallel. There is no protection circuit that can help here; the fault happens on the assembly bench, upstream of the BMS.
