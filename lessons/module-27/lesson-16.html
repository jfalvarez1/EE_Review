Sag, Tension and Clearance

A conductor hung between two structures is a catenary. How far it dips decides how tall the structures have to be, how far apart they can stand, and whether the line is legal — and it changes with temperature and ice all year. This is where most of a transmission line's cost is actually determined.

Parabolic sag
S = w L² / (8 T)
Governing code
NESC (ANSI C2)

with w the conductor weight per unit length, L the span, and T the horizontal tension. The exact form is a catenary, S = (T/w)(cosh(wL/2T) − 1), but the parabola is within a fraction of a percent for normal sag-to-span ratios and is what you will use by hand.

The three things that fight each other

  • More tension → less sag → shorter structures. Attractive until you notice tension is limited: NESC caps it at roughly 60% of rated strength under maximum ice and wind, 25% at final unloaded condition, and about 33–35% initial unloaded, specifically to control aeolian vibration fatigue.
  • Longer spans → fewer structures → less money — but sag goes as L², so doubling the span quadruples the sag and the structures get much taller. There is a cost minimum in the middle, and finding it is the design job.
  • Hot conductor → more sag. The worst case is not storm loading, it is a still, hot day at maximum current: aluminium expands about 23×10-6/°C, so a 100 °C rise over a 1,000 ft span adds roughly 2.3 ft of length — and far more than that in sag.
Thermal elongation: ΔL = L · α · ΔT,  αACSR ≈ 19×10-6/°C
Ruling span: Lr = sqrt( ΣL³ / ΣL )
Conductor length ≈ L (1 + 8S² / 3L²)

Above 22 kV, add roughly 0.4 inches per kV over 22 kV to the base figure. These are clearances at the maximum operating temperature or under the ice loading of the applicable NESC district, whichever sags more — not at the temperature the crew happened to string it.

Clearance is checked at the worst case, not the built case. A line strung on a cool spring morning looks generous. The question the code asks is where that conductor sits on a 40 °C August afternoon at full emergency load, years later, after the conductor has crept. Sag-tension software exists because getting this wrong is how vehicles contact conductors.

Field notes

  • Ruling span is how a real line is strung. Conductor runs between dead-ends across many suspension structures of different span lengths; tension equalises across them. The ruling span is the single equivalent span used to compute the stringing chart the crew works from.
  • Creep is permanent and real. Aluminium elongates slowly under sustained tension, adding sag over the line's first years. Designs use a 10-year creep condition, and crews string slightly high to compensate.
  • Aeolian vibration is why tension is capped. Steady light wind sheds vortices that resonate the span; over years it fatigues strands at the clamps. Dampers help; low final tension helps more.
  • Uplift kills structures. A structure in a valley between two higher ones can see the conductor pull up at cold temperatures. It must be checked separately — suspension hardware and some structures cannot take it.

Check yourself

Drake weighs 1.094 lb/ft. Span 800 ft, horizontal tension 6,000 lb. Find the sag.

S = wL²/(8T) = 1.094 × 800² / (8 × 6000) = 1.094 × 640,000 / 48,000 = 14.6 ft.

Same conductor and tension, span doubled to 1,600 ft. Sag?

Sag goes as L², so 4×: 58.3 ft. To keep the same sag you would need four times the tension, which almost certainly exceeds the NESC limit — which is exactly why long spans need much taller structures rather than more tension.

Spans of 700, 900 and 1,100 ft between dead-ends. What is the ruling span?

Lr = √(ΣL³/ΣL) = √((343 + 729 + 1331)×106 / 2700) = √(2.403×109/2700) = √890,000 = 943 ft. Note it is pulled toward the longest span, not the arithmetic mean of 900.

Why is maximum operating temperature the governing clearance case rather than ice loading?

Ice adds weight and therefore sag, but it happens at low temperature where the conductor has contracted, and the code allows loaded-condition clearances to be checked separately. Maximum operating temperature combines full thermal elongation with long-term creep and no compensating tension increase — on most modern lines, especially with high-temperature conductors, that is the deepest the conductor ever hangs.
