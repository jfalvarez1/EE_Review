Transmission Lines, SIL and Loadability

A transmission line is a distributed RLC network, but you almost never solve it that way. You pick a lumped model matched to the line's length, and you judge how much power it can carry against one of three limits — thermal, voltage, or stability — depending on how long it is.

Surge impedance
Zc = √(L/C)
Surge impedance loading
SIL = VLL² / Zc

Which model for which length

LengthModelABCD parameters
Short, < 80 km (50 mi) Series Z only; shunt admittance ignored A = D = 1, B = Z, C = 0
Medium, 80–250 km Nominal π: half the shunt Y at each end A = D = 1 + ZY/2, B = Z, C = Y(1 + ZY/4)
Long, > 250 km Distributed parameters, hyperbolic A = D = coshγl, B = Zcsinhγl, C = sinhγl/Zc

with γ = √(zy) the propagation constant and Zc = √(z/y) the characteristic impedance, both per unit length. The sending end relates to the receiving end by

Vs = A Vr + B Ir    Is = C Vr + D Ir

Surge impedance loading — the line's natural operating point

At SIL the reactive power absorbed by the series inductance exactly equals the reactive power generated by the shunt capacitance. The line is reactively self-sufficient and its voltage profile is flat end to end.

Loaded below SIL → net capacitive: the line generates VArs, voltage rises
Loaded at SIL → flat voltage profile, no net VAr exchange
Loaded above SIL → net inductive: the line absorbs VArs, voltage sags

The rise on a lightly loaded or open-ended line is the Ferranti effect. For a lossless nominal-π line,

Vr/Vs = 1 / (1 − XLB/2)

which is why long EHV lines carry shunt reactors that get switched in at light load, and why energising a long open line is a deliberate, studied operation rather than a routine one.

Loadability: the St. Clair curve

How much a line can actually carry depends on length, expressed as a multiple of SIL:

LengthLoadabilityBinding limit
< 50 mi~3× SILThermal (conductor temperature)
100 mi~2× SILVoltage drop
200 mi~1.3× SILVoltage drop
300 mi~1.0× SILSteady-state stability

This is the single most useful curve in transmission planning: it tells you immediately whether a corridor problem is solved by bigger conductor (thermal), by reactive support (voltage), or by series compensation and a new circuit (stability).

Field notes

  • Voltage drop on transmission is reactive, not resistive. The textbook I×R intuition from low-voltage wiring is wrong here: X is typically 3–10× R, so the drop is dominated by I×X and is controlled with VArs, not with bigger conductor.
  • A line's rating is a temperature, not a current. Ampacity depends on ambient temperature, wind speed and solar heating. Dynamic line rating exploits exactly this — a cold windy night can buy 20–30% extra capacity.
  • Series capacitors raise transfer but bring sub-synchronous resonance risk. A series-compensated line can interact with a nearby turbine-generator's torsional modes. That is why ERCOT screens every new interconnection for SSR.
  • Charging current matters at switching. Energising a long 345 kV line is a capacitive switching duty; breakers are rated for it separately.

Check yourself

A 345 kV line has Zc = 365 Ω. What is its SIL?

SIL = V²/Zc = 345²/365 = 119,025/365 = 326 MW.

Why does one 765 kV circuit replace three or four 345 kV circuits?

SIL scales with V². (765/345)² = 4.9, so one 765 kV circuit carries roughly five times the natural loading of a 345 kV circuit — on one right of way, with proportionally lower I²R loss for the same delivered power. That geometry is exactly why AEP built 765 kV to move mine-mouth coal generation to distant load centres.

A 12.47 kV feeder delivers 3 MW at 0.9 pf over 5 miles of 795 kcmil ACSR (0.259 + j0.734 Ω/mi). Estimate the percent voltage drop.

I = 3×106/(√3 × 12,470 × 0.9) = 154.5 A.
Z = 5(0.259 + j0.734) = 1.295 + j3.67 Ω.
θ = cos-10.9 = 25.8°, so VD ≈ I(R cosθ + X sinθ) = 154.5(1.166 + 1.600) = 427 V per phase.
VLN = 12,470/√3 = 7,199 V, so 5.9% — beyond the usual 3–5% design guideline, so this feeder needs a regulator, a capacitor bank, or larger conductor.

A 300-mile 345 kV line is loaded to 700 MW. Is that plausible?

SIL ≈ 326 MW, so 700 MW is 2.1× SIL. At 300 miles the St. Clair curve gives roughly 1.0× SIL before stability binds. Not plausible without series compensation or a parallel circuit.
