Overcurrent Protection and Coordination

Protection has one job: isolate a fault, using the smallest possible piece of the system, as fast as possible, and never operate for anything that is not a fault. Those goals fight each other, and coordination is where the fight is settled.

The inverse-time curve

Higher fault current must trip faster. The IEEE C37.112 standard curves have the form

t = TD · [ A / ((I/Ipu)p − 1) + B ]

TD is the time dial, which scales the whole curve vertically, and Ipu is the pickup, which slides it horizontally. Those are the only two settings you get, and every coordination study is spent choosing them.

Coordination: the two rules

  • Pickup above load, below fault. Set pickup at roughly 125–150% of maximum load so normal swings and cold-load pickup do not trip it, while staying below the minimum fault current the device is responsible for seeing.
  • Coordination time interval (CTI). Each upstream device must be slower than the device below it by 0.25–0.40 s at every current they both see. The margin covers breaker interrupting time, relay overtravel, CT error and curve tolerance.
Coordination is checked across the whole current range, not at one point. Two curves that are 0.3 s apart at 2,000 A can cross at 8,000 A, and the crossing is exactly where the fault will be.

Field notes

  • Cold-load pickup is real. Restoring a feeder that has been out for hours draws 2–3× normal load for several seconds while thermostats, motors and compressors all restart together. A pickup set tightly against normal load will trip on restoration and you will not get the feeder back.
  • Ground pickup can be much more sensitive. Load is balanced, so residual current is near zero in healthy operation. 51N pickup is commonly set at 10–20% of the phase setting, which is what lets it find high-impedance ground faults.
  • Directional elements are needed the moment there are two sources. On a looped or networked system, current for a fault behind the relay looks identical in magnitude to a fault in front of it. Device 67 adds the polarising reference that distinguishes them.
  • Faster clearing is a safety feature, not just a reliability one. Arc-flash incident energy is roughly proportional to clearing time. A miscoordinated relay that clears in 0.5 s instead of 0.1 s multiplies the energy a worker at that panel would be exposed to by five.

How a trip actually happens — the DC control circuit

Everything above decides whether to trip. This is the circuit that does it. It is worth knowing in detail, because more outages are caused by problems in this loop than by relays making wrong decisions.

The trip path: station battery, relay output contact, breaker auxiliary contact 52a, trip coil, back to battery negative. The 52a contact is closed only while the breaker is closed.

Battery (125 VDC) → relay trip contact → 52a → trip coil → battery negative
  • Why the 52a contact is there. It opens as soon as the breaker travels open, cutting current to the trip coil. Trip coils are rated for a brief pulse, not continuous duty — without 52a the coil would burn out on every operation.
  • Why the relay contact never interrupts the current. The 52a contact is what breaks the inductive trip-coil current. Relay output contacts are not rated to interrupt it, which is exactly why the scheme is wired in this order.
  • Trip coil monitoring. A small current is continuously passed through the coil to prove the circuit is intact. An open trip coil is completely silent otherwise — you find out at the worst possible moment.
  • Why it is DC. The station battery keeps working when the AC system is faulted or dark. Losing station DC means losing the ability to trip anything, which is why battery and charger maintenance is treated as protection work rather than facilities work.
  • Lockout relay (86). For serious faults the trip goes through a hand-reset lockout that trips everything in the zone and holds it open until a human investigates and resets it.
Field reality: the most common cause of a breaker failing to trip is not the relay. It is the DC circuit — a blown fuse, a loose terminal, a failed trip coil, or a discharged station battery. That is the entire reason breaker-failure protection (50BF, covered in the next lesson) exists as a separate scheme.

Check yourself

A lateral fuse clears in 0.25 s at a given fault current. With a 0.3 s CTI, what is the minimum upstream relay time?

0.25 + 0.30 = 0.55 s. The margin covers fuse melt-time scatter, breaker interrupting time and relay tolerance, so the fuse reliably clears alone and only the lateral goes dark.

Very inverse curve, pickup 200 A, TD = 2, fault 2,000 A. Find the trip time.

M = 2000/200 = 10. t = 2 × [19.61/(10² − 1) + 0.491] = 2 × [19.61/99 + 0.491] = 2 × [0.198 + 0.491] = 1.38 s.

Why choose extremely inverse for coordinating with fuses?

A fuse's melting characteristic is very steep — time falls sharply with current. An extremely inverse relay curve (p = 2 with a small constant term) has a similar slope, so the two curves stay roughly parallel across the whole current range instead of converging and crossing at high current.

Why is pickup set on the CT secondary rather than in primary amps?

The relay only ever sees secondary current. A 600:5 CT scales by 120, so a 600 A primary pickup is a 5 A secondary setting. Getting the CT ratio wrong in the setting sheet is one of the most common and most consequential commissioning errors.
