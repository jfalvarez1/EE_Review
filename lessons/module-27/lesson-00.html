AC Power: RMS, Phasors and the Power Triangle

Everything else in this module is written in a vocabulary that a power engineer uses without thinking and that nobody else has any reason to know. Megawatts and megavars. Apparent power. Power factor, leading and lagging. Per-unit. This lesson builds all of it from one idea — that in AC, voltage and current need not peak at the same moment — and defines every term properly on the way past.

The one sentence the rest of the module rests on. If current lags voltage, then for part of every cycle the load is returning energy to the source rather than consuming it. That returned energy does no work, but it still flows through every conductor, transformer and breaker between here and the generator — and they all have to be sized for it. That is reactive power, and it is why the grid is more complicated than Ohm's law.

1. RMS: why 120 V is not 120 V

A sinusoid spends most of its time away from its peak, so quoting the peak overstates what it can do. The useful number is the DC voltage that would heat a resistor at the same rate — the root mean square, and the name is the recipe read backwards.

\[ P_{avg} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \frac{v(t)^2}{R}\,dt = \frac{1}{R}\cdot\overline{v^2} \] Power in a resistor goes as the square of voltage, so the average power depends on the average of the square, not the average of the voltage — which for a sinusoid is zero.
\[ V_{rms} \equiv \sqrt{\overline{v^2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P_{avg} = \frac{V_{rms}^2}{R} \] Defined precisely so that the DC formula keeps working. That is the whole point of RMS; it is not a property of sinusoids.
\[ \overline{v^2} = \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T V_m^2\cos^2(\omega t)\,dt = V_m^2\cdot\frac{1}{2} \] Because \(\cos^2\) averages to one half — the single fact behind every \(\sqrt{2}\) in power engineering.
\[ V_{rms} = \frac{V_m}{\sqrt{2}} = 0.707\,V_m \qquad\text{(sinusoid only)} \]
What that means on a bench and on a pole

A 120 V outlet peaks at \(120\sqrt2 = \mathbf{170\ V}\), and swings 340 V peak-to-peak. A 240 V circuit peaks at 340 V. A 12.47 kV distribution feeder is 17.6 kV line-to-neutral at the crest, and its insulation is specified for the crest, not the RMS.

The \(\sqrt2\) is only for sinusoids. A square wave has \(V_{rms} = V_m\); a triangle has \(V_m/\sqrt3\). Feed a distorted waveform to a cheap "average-responding" meter calibrated for sinusoids and it will read low — which is why the phrase true RMS is printed on the good ones and matters wherever there are switching loads.

2. Phasors: replacing calculus with algebra

At a single frequency in steady state, every voltage and current in a linear circuit is a sinusoid of that same frequency. Only two things distinguish them: amplitude and phase. So carry those two and drop the rest.

\[ v(t) = V_m\cos(\omega t + \theta) \;\longleftrightarrow\; \mathbf{V} = V_{rms}\angle\theta \]

Differentiation becomes multiplication by \(j\omega\), so the calculus of inductors and capacitors collapses into arithmetic on complex numbers:

\(\omega = 2\pi f = 377\) rad/s at 60 Hz. It appears in nearly every calculation in this module and is worth knowing on sight. In Europe and most of the world it is 314 rad/s at 50 Hz.

Phasors are only valid at one frequency, in steady state, in a linear circuit. A transformer inrush, a fault, a converter's switching harmonics: none of those are one sinusoid, and the phasor picture does not describe them. That is the boundary this whole module operates inside, and the fault lessons step outside it on purpose.

3. The three powers, derived from one multiplication

Instantaneous power is just \(p(t) = v(t)\,i(t)\). Take a voltage and a current with an angle \(\varphi\) between them and multiply:

\[ p(t) = V_m\cos(\omega t)\cdot I_m\cos(\omega t - \varphi) \]
\[ = \frac{V_m I_m}{2}\Bigl[\cos\varphi + \cos(2\omega t - \varphi)\Bigr] \] Using \(\cos A\cos B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B) + \cos(A+B)]\). Note the \(2\omega\): instantaneous power pulsates at twice the line frequency, which is why a 60 Hz transformer hums at 120 Hz.
\[ p(t) = VI\cos\varphi + VI\cos(2\omega t - \varphi) \] With \(V, I\) now RMS, since \(V_mI_m/2 = V_{rms}I_{rms}\). The first term is constant; the second averages to zero.
\[ p(t) = \underbrace{P\bigl(1 + \cos 2\omega t\bigr)}_{\text{never negative}} + \underbrace{Q\sin 2\omega t}_{\text{averages to zero}} \] Expanding the second cosine and regrouping. This form is worth more than the one above, because it separates the two behaviours completely.
Read that last line carefully — it defines everything

The first term, \(P(1+\cos2\omega t)\), swings between 0 and \(2P\) and is never negative. Energy only ever flows toward the load. Its average is \(P\): the real power, in watts, the part that turns into torque, heat and light.

The second term, \(Q\sin2\omega t\), is symmetric about zero. For a quarter cycle energy flows into the load, for the next quarter it flows back out. Nothing is consumed. Its amplitude is \(Q\): the reactive power, in VAR, borrowed and returned 120 times a second by the magnetic field of every motor and transformer on the system.

The two combine as a right triangle, because they are 90° apart by construction:

\[ \mathbf{S} = P + jQ, \qquad |S| = \sqrt{P^2 + Q^2} = VI, \qquad \mathrm{pf} = \frac{P}{|S|} = \cos\varphi \]

Set the phase to ±90°. The load now draws 20 A from a 240 V source. What is the real power?

  1. 4800 W, because \(P = VI\)
    \(VI\) is the apparent power \(S\), in volt-amperes. It equals the real power only when the two are in phase. This confusion is exactly why the units are deliberately different: watts, VAR and VA are all dimensionally identical and are kept apart on purpose.
  2. About 3400 W, since some of it is lost
    Nothing is being lost in the load — a pure reactance dissipates nothing at all. The energy is not consumed and not wasted in the load; it is borrowed and given back.
  3. Zero
    Right. \(P = VI\cos\varphi\) and \(\cos(\pm90°) = 0\). The waveform is symmetric about the axis: every joule delivered in one quarter cycle comes back in the next. And yet 20 A is genuinely flowing — through the conductors, the transformer and the breaker, heating all of them at \(I^2R\). A load that consumes no power can still overload a circuit.
  4. Zero, and no current actually flows either
    The current is entirely real and a clamp meter reads it. Only the net energy transfer is zero.

This is the whole argument for power factor correction, and for why the utility meters kVAR separately and bills industrial customers for it.

5. Why reactive power costs money although it does no work

\(Q\) does no work, and it is still expensive, because the current that carries it is indistinguishable from any other current to the copper it flows through.

\[ I = \frac{S}{\sqrt3\,V_{LL}} = \frac{P}{\sqrt3\,V_{LL}\cdot \mathrm{pf}} \] For the same delivered real power, the current is inversely proportional to power factor.
\[ P_{loss} = 3I^2R \;\propto\; \frac{1}{\mathrm{pf}^2} \]
\[ \mathrm{pf}: 1.0 \to 0.7 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{losses} \times \frac{1}{0.7^2} = \times 2.04 \] A load at 0.7 power factor makes the line lose twice as much as the same kilowatts at unity, and needs a conductor and transformer 43% larger to carry it.

Correcting it

An inductive load absorbs VAR; a capacitor supplies them. Put a capacitor bank next to the load and the VAR are exchanged locally instead of being dragged from the generator.

\[ Q_C = P\bigl(\tan\varphi_1 - \tan\varphi_2\bigr) \]
\[ P = 100\,\mathrm{kW}, \quad \mathrm{pf}_1 = 0.70 \;\Rightarrow\; \varphi_1 = 45.6°,\ Q_1 = 102\,\mathrm{kVAR} \]
\[ \text{target } \mathrm{pf}_2 = 0.95 \;\Rightarrow\; \varphi_2 = 18.2°,\ Q_2 = 32.9\,\mathrm{kVAR} \]
\[ Q_C = 102 - 32.9 = 69.2\,\mathrm{kVAR} \]
\[ S: 143 \to 105\,\mathrm{kVA}, \qquad I: 172 \to 127\,\mathrm{A} \text{ at }480\,\mathrm{V} \] 26% less current for exactly the same useful work, and \((105/143)^2 = 0.54\) of the line loss — 46% of the heating gone.

The capacitor bank sits at the load, not at the substation, and that placement is the entire point: every metre of conductor upstream of it is relieved of the reactive current, and everything downstream still sees it.

6. Three phase, and where the \(\sqrt3\) comes from

Three sinusoids 120° apart. Two consequences follow, and they are the reason the entire grid is built this way.

\[ p_a + p_b + p_c = 3VI\cos\varphi \quad\text{— a constant, with no } 2\omega \text{ ripple} \] The three pulsating terms are 120° apart and sum to zero. A balanced three-phase load receives perfectly smooth power, which is why three-phase motors do not vibrate at 120 Hz and single-phase ones do.
\[ V_{LL} = \sqrt3\,V_{LN} \] Two phasors of equal length 120° apart subtract to \(\sqrt3\) times either. 120 V line-to-neutral gives 208 V line-to-line, not 240.
\[ S_{3\phi} = 3\,V_{LN}I_L = \sqrt3\,V_{LL}I_L \] The form everyone uses, because \(V_{LL}\) is what a system is named by. The \(\sqrt3\) is not a fudge: it is \(3/\sqrt3\).
\[ P_{3\phi} = \sqrt3\,V_{LL}I_L\cos\varphi \]
And the third consequence, which is about copper. Three balanced currents 120° apart sum to zero, so a balanced three-phase load needs no neutral return at all. Delivering the same power single-phase would need two conductors carrying full current; three-phase needs three carrying 57.7% of it. That is roughly half the copper for the same power, and it is why transmission is three-phase everywhere on earth.

Problem Set — AC power

1Level 1

A motor draws 45 A at 480 V line-to-line, three phase, at 0.86 power factor lagging. Find \(S\), \(P\) and \(Q\).

Solution

\[ S = \sqrt3\,V_{LL}I = 1.732 \times 480 \times 45 = 37.4\,\mathrm{kVA} \]

\[ P = S\cos\varphi = 37.4 \times 0.86 = 32.2\,\mathrm{kW} \]

\[ Q = S\sin\varphi = 37.4 \times \sin(30.7°) = 37.4 \times 0.510 = 19.1\,\mathrm{kVAR} \]

Check: \(\sqrt{32.2^2 + 19.1^2} = 37.4\) ✓. Note the motor is rated in horsepower or kW but the transformer feeding it must be sized for the 37.4 kVA.

2Level 1

A 480 V system feeds a purely resistive 50 kW heater. What is its power factor, and what is \(Q\)?

Solution

A resistor's current is in phase with its voltage, so \(\varphi = 0\), \(\mathrm{pf} = \cos 0 = \mathbf{1.0}\) and \(Q = VI\sin 0 = \mathbf{0}\).

\(S = P = 50\,\mathrm{kVA}\). Resistive heating is the one load that needs no correction — which is why utilities like it and why a resistive load bank is the reference case every other load is compared against.

3Level 2

A 1000 kVA transformer supplies a plant drawing 900 kW at 0.80 pf lagging. Is the transformer overloaded? What is the largest \(P\) it could supply if the plant corrected to 0.98?

Solution

\[ S = \frac{900}{0.80} = 1125\,\mathrm{kVA} \]

Yes — 12.5% over its rating, even though the plant is drawing only 900 kW from a "1000 kVA" transformer. This is the most common way a transformer ends up overloaded: the kilowatts look fine.

At 0.98 power factor:

\[ P_{max} = 1000 \times 0.98 = \mathbf{980\,kW} \]

So correcting the power factor releases 80 kW of capacity from a transformer that is already installed. Capacitors are almost always cheaper than a larger transformer, and that comparison is the everyday business case for correction.

4Level 2

Why are generators and transformers rated in MVA rather than MW?

Solution

Because their two failure limits are set by current and by voltage, and neither cares about the phase angle between them.

Current heats the windings at \(I^2R\), and the current is set by \(S\), not \(P\). Voltage stresses the insulation. A machine delivering 100 MVA at zero power factor is doing no useful work at all and is still fully loaded on both counts.

Rating in MW would let a customer at 0.5 pf draw twice the current the machine was built for while appearing to be within its rating.

5Level 3

A plant corrects to unity power factor with a fixed capacitor bank sized for full production. On a night shift the load drops to 20% but the bank stays connected. What happens, and why do utilities dislike it?

Solution

The inductive \(Q\) of the load falls with production, but the capacitor's \(Q_C = V^2\omega C\) does not — it depends only on voltage. So the bank now over-corrects and the plant becomes a net exporter of VAR: the power factor goes leading.

Two consequences. Leading current through the line's series inductance makes the voltage rise toward the load rather than droop — the Ferranti effect — and can push the plant's terminal voltage above equipment ratings at exactly the time when system voltage is already high because everyone else's load is off. And the excess VAR flow causes the same \(I^2R\) losses as a lagging one; the sign of \(Q\) does not matter to the copper.

The fix is a switched bank in stages, controlled by a VAR or power-factor relay, so correction tracks the load. This is why a modern capacitor bank on a distribution feeder has contactors and a controller rather than being bolted on permanently.

6Level 4

A variable-speed drive draws a current whose fundamental is almost exactly in phase with the voltage, yet its measured power factor is 0.65. Explain, and say why adding capacitors would make things worse.

Solution

Power factor is \(P/S\), and \(S = V I_{rms}\) uses the total RMS current including every harmonic. A six-pulse rectifier front end draws current in narrow pulses near the voltage peaks, rich in the 5th, 7th, 11th and 13th harmonics.

Only the fundamental component carries real power. The harmonics add to \(I_{rms}\), and so to \(S\), while contributing nothing to \(P\):

\[ \mathrm{pf}_{total} = \underbrace{\cos\varphi_1}_{\approx 1} \times \underbrace{\frac{I_1}{I_{rms}}}_{\text{distortion factor}} \]

So the displacement factor is near unity and the distortion factor is 0.65. Nothing is out of phase; the current is simply the wrong shape.

Why capacitors make it worse: they correct displacement, and there is no displacement to correct — so they add leading VAR that was not wanted. Worse, the bank's capacitance forms a resonant circuit with the supply transformer's leakage inductance, typically landing somewhere near the 5th to 13th harmonic. A harmonic current at that frequency is then amplified, and capacitor banks fail in exactly this way.

The correct fixes are a line reactor or a detuned (reactor-connected) bank whose resonance is deliberately placed below the lowest harmonic present, a 12-pulse or 18-pulse rectifier, or an active front end.

Lab — measure a power factor with two meters and some arithmetic

Time 45 min Skill distinguishing W from VA on real loads Parts a plug-in energy meter that reads W, VA and pf; an incandescent lamp, a small induction motor or fan, a laptop charger, an LED lamp

Every term in this lesson is visible on a $25 plug-in meter, and the point of the exercise is to see that the same 60 W means different things to the wiring depending on what is drawing it.

  1. Measure the incandescent lamp. Record W, VA and pf. It should read very close to unity: a filament is a resistor.
  2. Measure the motor or fan, running unloaded. Record the same three. Expect 0.5–0.8 lagging.
  3. Now load the fan — restrict the airflow with a piece of card — and watch the power factor improve as the real power rises. The magnetising VAR is roughly constant while \(P\) grows, so the angle closes.
  4. Measure the laptop charger and the LED lamp. Both are switching supplies. Compare their VA to their W.
  5. For each load compute \(Q = \sqrt{S^2 - P^2}\) and check it against the meter's own reading if it has one.
  6. Finally, add the watts of everything and add the VA of everything. They will not scale the same way, and that is the lesson.

What you should find. The lamp near 1.00. The fan lagging and improving under load. The switching supplies somewhere around 0.5–0.65 — and here is the interesting part: that is not a phase angle. A cheap switching supply draws current in narrow spikes at the voltage peaks, so its displacement factor is close to unity while its distortion factor is poor, exactly as in problem 6. A meter that reports "pf" is reporting \(P/S\) and cannot tell you which of the two it is.

Where it goes wrong, and why that is the interesting part. Add up the watts of five small loads and you get a number you could have guessed. Add up the VA and you get more than you expected, because VA do not add arithmetically unless the loads share a phase angle — strictly you should add \(P\) and \(Q\) separately and recombine. Doing it the wrong way and comparing is the fastest way to feel why the distinction exists.

An LED lamp with a good driver may read close to unity, and a bad one near 0.5. If you have two, measure both: they draw the same watts and load the building's wiring quite differently, which is precisely the argument behind the harmonic limits in IEC 61000-3-2.
