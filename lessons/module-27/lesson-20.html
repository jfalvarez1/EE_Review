Interview Prep — Power Systems

Utility interviews are different from IC interviews in one specific way: they care much more about whether your numbers are plausible than whether they are exact. Nobody expects you to load-flow a network on a whiteboard. They expect you to say "that is about a hundred megavars, so that bank is too big" and be right.

Part 1 — Derive it

1 Derive Warm-up

Why \(\sqrt3\)? Derive it, and give me two other consequences of three-phase that follow from the same picture.

How to answer
\[ V_{ab} = V_a - V_b, \quad |V_a| = |V_b| = V_{LN},\ \angle 120° \text{ apart} \]
\[ |V_{ab}| = 2V_{LN}\sin(60°) = \sqrt3\,V_{LN} \] Two equal phasors 120° apart subtract to \(\sqrt3\) times either. That is the whole derivation.
\[ S_{3\phi} = 3V_{LN}I_L = \sqrt3\,V_{LL}I_L \] The \(\sqrt3\) in the power formula is \(3/\sqrt3\), not a separate fact.

Two consequences:

  • Constant power. The three \(2\omega\) pulsating terms are 120° apart and sum to zero, so a balanced three-phase load receives perfectly smooth power. That is why three-phase motors do not vibrate at 120 Hz and single-phase ones do.
  • No neutral current. Three balanced currents 120° apart sum to zero, so a balanced load needs no return conductor at all — roughly half the copper for the same power, which is why transmission is three-phase everywhere.
What they are testing: a warm-up, but the two consequences are what distinguish a real answer. Anyone can quote \(\sqrt3\); saying why the grid is three-phase rather than two- or six- is the actual content.
2 Derive Standard

A 20 MVA transformer with 8% impedance on a 138 kV / 13.8 kV system. What is the fault current on the low side? Do it in your head.

How to answer
\[ I_{rated} = \frac{20\,\mathrm{MVA}}{\sqrt3 \times 13.8\,\mathrm{kV}} = \frac{20\times10^6}{23900} = 837\,\mathrm{A} \]
\[ I_{SC} = \frac{I_{rated}}{Z_{pu}} = \frac{837}{0.08} = 10.5\,\mathrm{kA} \] That is the entire calculation. Percent impedance IS the reciprocal of the per-unit fault current — it is defined as the voltage needed to drive rated current into a short.

"And that assumes an infinite source behind the transformer, so it is the worst case and a conservative number for equipment ratings. Add the upstream system impedance in per-unit and the real figure comes down. Note also that low impedance is not automatically good: 4% instead of 8% would double the fault duty to 21 kA and might exceed the switchgear rating, which is why utilities sometimes specify a higher impedance than the manufacturer would naturally build."

What they are testing: whether percent impedance means something to you or is a number on a nameplate. The observation that lower impedance can be a problem is the mark of someone who has specified equipment rather than only analysed it.
3 Derive Standard

Why per-unit? What does it actually buy you?

How to answer
\[ Z_{base} = \frac{kV^2}{\mathrm{MVA}}, \qquad Z_{pu} = \frac{Z_{actual}}{Z_{base}} \]

Three things, and the first is the big one:

  • Transformers disappear. Choose bases that scale with the turns ratio and a transformer's per-unit impedance is the same seen from either side. A network with six voltage levels becomes one flat circuit with no ratios to carry through the algebra — which is where mistakes come from.
  • Numbers become comparable. Nearly every power transformer is 5–10% impedance regardless of size or voltage, so a per-unit value that looks wrong is wrong. That sanity check is worth a great deal.
  • The \(\sqrt3\) vanishes. In per-unit, single-phase and three-phase formulas are identical.

"The one thing to be careful about is base changing. Manufacturer impedances are given on the equipment's own MVA base, so before you can add them you convert everything to a common system base with \(Z_{new} = Z_{old}\cdot\frac{MVA_{new}}{MVA_{old}}\cdot\left(\frac{kV_{old}}{kV_{new}}\right)^2\). Forgetting that is the most common error in a hand fault study."

What they are testing: whether you can articulate the benefit rather than just perform the arithmetic. "Transformers disappear" is the answer they want to hear, and the base-change formula is the follow-up.
4 Derive Harder

Explain why real power flows with the angle and reactive power flows with the magnitude. Derive it.

How to answer
\[ P = \frac{V_1V_2}{X}\sin\delta, \qquad Q = \frac{V_1(V_1 - V_2\cos\delta)}{X} \] The standard two-bus result, assuming the line is mostly reactive — which for transmission it is, X/R being 5 to 20.
\[ \delta \text{ small}: \quad P \approx \frac{V_1V_2}{X}\delta, \qquad Q \approx \frac{V_1(V_1-V_2)}{X} \] P depends on the angle DIFFERENCE and barely on magnitudes; Q depends on the magnitude DIFFERENCE and barely on angle. They decouple.

"Which is the basis of the entire operating practice. You control real power with generator torque, which moves angles, and you control voltage with excitation and capacitor banks, which moves magnitudes — and the two do not fight each other. It is also why fast-decoupled load flow works and why voltage collapse is a local phenomenon: reactive power does not travel well, because moving it requires a voltage gradient that the system cannot afford. So you fix a voltage problem locally, near the load, not by importing vars from the next substation."

What they are testing: whether you understand the P-δ / Q-V decoupling as the organising idea of power system operation. "Vars do not travel" is a phrase worth having, because it explains capacitor bank placement, voltage collapse, and why the answer to a low-voltage problem is almost never at the generator.
5 Derive Harder

Why 765 kV? Justify it with numbers rather than "less loss".

How to answer
\[ I = \frac{P}{\sqrt3 V} \;\Longrightarrow\; P_{loss} = 3I^2R \propto \frac{1}{V^2} \]
\[ \frac{765}{345} = 2.22 \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{loss} \times \frac{1}{2.22^2} = 0.203 \] One fifth the loss for the same power over the same distance.
\[ \mathrm{SIL} \approx \frac{V^2}{Z_c} = \frac{765^2}{400} \approx 1460\,\mathrm{MW} \quad\text{vs}\quad \frac{345^2}{400} \approx 300\,\mathrm{MW} \] Surge impedance loading scales as \(V^2\) too, so ONE 765 kV circuit does the work of roughly five 345 kV circuits.

"So the argument is right-of-way as much as losses. Five 345 kV corridors need vastly more land, more structures and more easements than one 765 kV corridor, and land is the thing you cannot buy more of. AEP built the largest 765 kV network in the country for that reason. The costs are that everything scales: insulation, structure size, and corona — which is why every 765 kV phase is a bundle of six conductors, to raise the effective radius and get the surface gradient back below the corona threshold."

What they are testing: whether you can make an engineering argument with numbers instead of a slogan. Bringing in SIL and right-of-way rather than only \(I^2R\) is what makes it an answer rather than a recital, and mentioning bundling shows you know what the voltage costs.

Part 2 — Judgement

6 Estimate Standard

A plant draws 500 kW at 0.75 power factor lagging on 480 V. Size a capacitor bank to correct to 0.95. Then tell me what you would worry about.

How to answer
\[ \varphi_1 = \arccos 0.75 = 41.4°, \quad Q_1 = 500\tan(41.4°) = 441\,\mathrm{kVAR} \]
\[ \varphi_2 = \arccos 0.95 = 18.2°, \quad Q_2 = 500\tan(18.2°) = 164\,\mathrm{kVAR} \]
\[ Q_C = 441 - 164 = 277\,\mathrm{kVAR} \;\rightarrow\; \text{a 300 kVAR bank} \]
\[ I: \frac{500/0.75}{\sqrt3(0.48)} = 802\,\mathrm{A} \;\longrightarrow\; \frac{500/0.95}{\sqrt3(0.48)} = 633\,\mathrm{A} \] 21% less current, and \((633/802)^2 = 0.62\), so 38% less line loss.

Two worries, and the second is the one they want:

  • Overcorrection at light load. The load's vars fall with production; the capacitor's do not, since \(Q_C = V^2\omega C\) depends only on voltage. On a night shift the plant goes leading, and leading current through the line reactance makes the voltage rise. Use a switched bank in stages with a var controller.
  • Harmonic resonance. The bank's capacitance resonates with the supply transformer's leakage inductance. The resonant order is roughly \(h = \sqrt{\mathrm{MVA}_{sc}/\mathrm{MVAR}_{cap}}\) — and if that lands near the 5th or 7th, which it very often does, the bank amplifies harmonic current instead of correcting anything, and capacitor fuses start blowing.

"So I would check the resonant order before ordering, and if it lands near a characteristic harmonic I would use a detuned bank — a reactor in series putting the resonance at, say, the 4.2nd order, below the 5th, where nothing lives."

What they are testing: the arithmetic is bookwork. Harmonic resonance is the part that separates people who have commissioned a bank from people who have sized one on paper, and blown capacitor fuses on a new installation is a story every power engineer has heard.
7 Trap Harder

A long transmission line is energised at one end with the far end open. The receiving-end voltage is higher than the sending end. Explain.

How to answer

The Ferranti effect. It is capacitive charging current, not a measurement error.

A transmission line has shunt capacitance to ground distributed along it. With the far end open, that capacitance still draws charging current through the line's series inductance. A capacitive current through an inductive impedance produces a voltage rise rather than a drop, so the voltage climbs along the line and is highest at the open end.

\[ \frac{V_R}{V_S} \approx \frac{1}{\cos(\beta \ell)} \approx \frac{1}{1 - \frac{\omega^2 LC\ell^2}{2}} \] Greater than one, and it grows as the square of length. A 200-mile 765 kV line can rise 10% or more.

"It is worst on long, high-voltage, lightly-loaded lines — so it is a real operating constraint on exactly the 765 kV circuits that are most valuable. The countermeasure is shunt reactors, permanently connected or switched, to absorb the charging vars. It also drives energisation procedure: you close the remote end first where you can, and light-load overnight conditions are when operators watch for it."

What they are testing: whether a line is a distributed circuit to you or just an impedance. "Voltage rises at the open end" sounds impossible unless you have the capacitive charging current picture, and shunt reactors are the answer they want.
8 Reason Harder

What actually limits how much power a transmission line can carry? Name the limits in the order they bind as the line gets longer.

How to answer

Three limits, and which one binds depends almost entirely on length — this is the St. Clair curve.

  • Short lines (under ~50 miles): thermal. The conductor's temperature, which is where sag and annealing come from. This is the only limit that is about the wire itself.
  • Medium (50–200 miles): voltage drop. You run out of acceptable receiving-end voltage before the conductor gets hot. Typically the limit is around 5%.
  • Long (over ~200 miles): stability. The angle \(\delta\) across the line approaches the point where synchronism is lost. Practically you keep \(\delta\) under about 30–40° so there is margin to ride through a fault.

"Which is why line ratings are not one number. A 300-mile line might be rated at half its thermal capability, and the fix for that is not a bigger conductor — it is series compensation to reduce the effective X, which raises both the stability limit and the voltage performance at once. Adding aluminium to a stability-limited line buys nothing."

What they are testing: whether you know that "ampacity" is not the answer for long lines. The observation that a bigger conductor does not help a stability-limited circuit is the point of the question.
9 Rapid

Why is ERCOT electrically separate, and what does that mean operationally?

Answer

It is an intentional island — synchronously separate from the Eastern and Western Interconnections — which keeps it outside FERC's interstate jurisdiction. Operationally it means limited import capability: the DC ties to the east and to Mexico are a few hundred megawatts against a peak demand around 80 GW, so ERCOT cannot lean on neighbours in a shortage the way a member of a large interconnection can. It also means less system inertia, so frequency moves faster after a generation loss, which is why ERCOT's fast frequency response and responsive reserve products are structured the way they are.

10 Rapid

What is a TDU, and what does it not do?

Answer

A transmission and distribution utility in the deregulated Texas market — it owns and operates the wires, restores outages and reads meters, and is a regulated monopoly in its territory. What it does not do is sell electricity: the retail electric provider does that, and the customer chooses the REP but never the TDU. The practical consequence is that a TDU's revenue comes from delivery rates set by the PUC, not from energy sales, so its incentives are about reliability and capital investment rather than volume.
