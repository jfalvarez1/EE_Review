Conductor Selection and Ampacity

Picking a conductor is the first real design decision on a line, and it is not a current rating you look up — it is a temperature you agree not to exceed. Everything else (sag, clearance, structure height, loss, cost) follows from that one choice.

Ampacity is a weather statement, not a property of the wire. The same ACSR Drake conductor is rated near 900 A on a still 40 °C afternoon and comfortably above 1,200 A on a windy 10 °C night. Any ampacity number you are handed is meaningless without the assumed ambient, wind speed, solar heating and maximum conductor temperature.

Bundling

Above about 230 kV a single conductor per phase is no longer viable — the surface gradient causes corona, which means audible noise, radio interference and real power loss. The fix is to split the phase into sub-conductors on spacers, which raises the effective radius and drops the gradient.

Bundle geometry by voltage class. The bundle also lowers inductive reactance and raises SIL, so it improves transfer as well as corona.

GMRbundle (2 sub) = sqrt(GMR · d)
GMRbundle (3 sub) = cbrt(GMR · d²)
GMRbundle (4 sub) = 1.09 · (GMR · d³)1/4

where d is the sub-conductor spacing, typically 18 inches.

Field notes

  • Sag governs more designs than ampacity does. A conductor that can thermally carry 1,200 A may sag into the clearance limit at 800 A. Check the sag-tension study before celebrating the ampacity.
  • Emergency ratings buy you time, not capacity. A typical utility has a normal rating, a 4-hour emergency rating and a 15-minute rating. They exist because the conductor has thermal mass, not because the limits are soft.
  • Annealing is permanent. Take standard ACSR much past 100 °C and the aluminium loses strength irreversibly. That is the whole point of ACSS, which is annealed from the factory and therefore has nothing left to lose.
  • Reconductoring beats rebuilding. Replacing Drake with ACSS or ACCC on existing structures can double capacity without new right-of-way, permits or towers — usually the cheapest MW you will ever add.

Check yourself

A 345 kV line must carry 800 MW. What current per phase, and is one Drake conductor enough?

I = P / (√3 V) = 800×106 / (1.732 × 345,000) = 1,339 A at unity power factor.
One Drake is ~900 A, so no. Two-conductor bundle gives about 1,800 A and works comfortably — which is also what 345 kV needs for corona anyway.

Why does ampacity rise so much on a cold windy night?

Ampacity comes from a heat balance: I²R plus solar gain must equal convective plus radiative cooling. Wind dominates convection, and a larger ambient-to-conductor temperature difference increases every cooling term. Both move the same way at night, which is why dynamic line rating can find 20–30% extra capacity that a static summer rating leaves on the table.

Losses on 40 miles of Drake carrying 700 A, three phases.

R = 0.129 Ω/mi × 40 = 5.16 Ω per phase.
P = 3 I²R = 3 × 700² × 5.16 = 7.6 MW. At $40/MWh running continuously that is roughly $2.6M a year in losses, which is how bigger conductor pays for itself.

Why bundle at 345 kV when a single large conductor could carry the current?

Corona. Surface voltage gradient depends on conductor radius, and above roughly 230 kV a single conductor of any practical size exceeds the onset gradient — producing audible noise, radio interference and continuous loss. Bundling raises the effective radius far more cheaply than a single enormous conductor, and lowers reactance while raising SIL as a bonus.
