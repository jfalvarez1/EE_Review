AEP — 765 kV and the Texas TDU Model

American Electric Power operates the largest transmission system in the United States and sits in three different RTOs at once. It is a useful case study precisely because its seven operating companies are regulated in three incompatible ways, which forces you to separate the physics from the market design.

Why 765 kV

AEP was the first US utility to use 345 kV (1953) and went on to build more 765 kV transmission than any other American system. The engineering argument is the SIL relationship from Lesson 2:

SIL = VLL² / Zc  →  (765/345)² = 4.9

One 765 kV circuit carries roughly what three to four 345 kV circuits carry, on a single right of way, with losses for the same delivered power falling as 1/V². AEP's geography made that decisive: very large blocks of mine-mouth coal generation in West Virginia and Ohio needed to reach load centres hundreds of miles away in Ohio, Indiana and Michigan.

At 765 kV the design constraint shifts. Insulation is governed by switching surges rather than lightning, corona and audible noise drive conductor bundling, and every line needs shunt reactors because charging is around 5 MVAr per mile.

The Texas TDU model

Texas Senate Bill 7 (1999) split the vertically integrated utility into three roles. AEP Texas is a Transmission and Distribution Utility — a wires-only, PUCT-regulated monopoly for its footprint:

  • Generators compete and sell into the ERCOT wholesale market.
  • Retail Electric Providers buy wholesale, market to consumers, and issue the consolidated bill.
  • TDUs (AEP Texas, Oncor, CenterPoint, TNMP) own the poles, wires, transformers and meters, restore outages, and are paid through a PUCT-approved delivery tariff that every REP passes through. They own no generation and make no retail sales.
  • ERCOT operates the grid and the market but owns no wires.
The consequence for a customer: switching retail providers changes only the energy charge. The AEP Texas delivery charge is identical no matter which REP you choose, because the wires company is not part of the competition at all.

Separately, Electric Transmission Texas is AEP's 50/50 joint venture with Berkshire Hathaway Energy that builds and owns transmission inside ERCOT — legally and functionally distinct from AEP Texas the distribution TDU. AEP Texas's approved ROE is 9.76% (October 2024) on a $10.7B rate base, with a 2025–2029 capital plan of $9.3B, 54% of it transmission.

The economics behind AEP's bet. Losses scale as I²R, and for a fixed delivered power I scales as 1/V — so loss scales as 1/V². Moving from 345 kV to 765 kV cuts loss for the same transfer by (345/765)² ≈ 0.20, a five-fold reduction, while needing roughly a quarter of the circuits and a fraction of the right-of-way per MW-mile. That trade is why AEP built more 765 kV than anyone else in the country, and why ERCOT is now studying 765 kV for its own West Texas export problem.

Check yourself

Why does AEP Ohio show zero owned generation while Appalachian Power owns 7,497 MW?

Ohio restructured: generation was legally separated from the regulated wires business, so AEP Ohio is a T&D utility that buys power under PPA for the default-service customers who have not chosen a competitive supplier. Virginia and West Virginia did not restructure, so Appalachian Power remains vertically integrated and recovers generation costs through cost-of-service rates. Same parent company, different regulatory worlds.

A generator wants to interconnect in AEP Texas's footprint. Does it file with FERC?

No. AEP Texas is inside ERCOT, which is not FERC-jurisdictional for internal transmission. The request goes through ERCOT's Planning Guide Section 5 process under PUCT oversight. The same company's PJM and SPP footprints do follow FERC's Order 2023 cluster process — which is why AEP runs parallel interconnection practices.

Why does a 765 kV line need shunt reactors that a 138 kV line does not?

Charging MVAr scales with V² and with line length: roughly 5 MVAr/mile at 765 kV versus 0.13 at 138 kV. A 200-mile 765 kV line generates about 1,000 MVAr at no load, which would drive the receiving end well above rated voltage (Ferranti effect). Shunt reactors absorb it, and are switched out as load rises.

AEP's transmission is 25% of PJM's line-miles but serves only 14% of PJM's peak load. What does that imply?

AEP's territory is geographically large and comparatively sparse — long lines serving dispersed load, historically built to move mine-mouth generation rather than to serve dense urban centres. It also means AEP's transmission revenue requirement per customer is high relative to a compact urban utility, which is why 55% of its 2025 projected operating earnings come from transmission.
