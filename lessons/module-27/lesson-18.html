Specifying a Transmission Line End to End

The previous three lessons each covered one decision. This one runs the whole sequence on a single project, in the order a real design actually happens, and ends with the spec sheet you would hand to a contractor.

The brief: move 900 MW across 85 miles between two existing 345 kV substations, on new right-of-way, in a NESC Heavy loading district. N-1: the line must carry its share with one parallel circuit out.

Step 1 — voltage class

Already fixed at 345 kV by the terminals. If it were open, the SIL comparison from Lesson 2 would decide it: at 85 miles the loadability is roughly 2.1× SIL, so

345 kV: SIL ≈ 326 MW → 2.1 × 326 ≈ 685 MW per circuit
900 MW / 685 MW → 2 circuits, or 1 circuit with compensation

Going to 500 kV would carry it on one circuit, but the terminals would need new transformers and switchyards — almost always more expensive than a second 345 kV circuit over this distance.

Step 2 — current, and therefore conductor

I = P / (sqrt(3) · V · pf) = 900e6 / (1.732 × 345e3 × 0.98) = 1,537 A
Per circuit with two circuits: 769 A. Under N-1 (one circuit out): the full 1,537 A

N-1 is what sizes the conductor, not normal operation. A twin bundle of ACSR Drake gives about 1,800 A at 40 °C, so it carries the contingency with roughly 15% margin. Corona at 345 kV requires a bundle anyway, so the twin bundle is decided twice over.

Step 3 — geometry, and therefore impedance

Horizontal configuration on lattice steel, 28 ft phase spacing:

GMD = cbrt(28 × 28 × 56) = 35.3 ft
GMRbundle = sqrt(0.0375 × 1.5) = 0.237 ft
X = 0.2794 log10(35.3 / 0.237) = 0.607 Ω/mi
R = 0.129 / 2 = 0.0645 Ω/mi (X/R = 9.4)
Over 85 miles: Z = 5.5 + j51.6 Ω

Step 4 — span and structures

Ruling span 1,000 ft, Drake at 1.094 lb/ft bare, final unloaded tension 20% RTS = 6,300 lb:

S = wL² / 8T = 1.094 × 1000² / (8 × 6300) = 21.7 ft at 15 °C
At 100 °C maximum operating temperature, sag grows to roughly 31 ft
Required clearance at 345 kV over roads ≈ 18.5 + 0.4(345−22)/12 ≈ 29.3 ft
Attachment height ≥ 31 + 29.3 = 60.3 ft, so specify 70 ft to the lowest conductor

85 miles at 1,000 ft ruling span is about 449 structures, which at typical lattice cost dominates the project budget — and is why the span length is worth optimising rather than defaulting.

Step 5 — losses and the economic check

The finished line: two 345 kV circuits, twin-bundle Drake, terminated at breakers in each substation, with shunt compensation available at the receiving end.

What this sequence leaves out

  • Routing and permitting usually take longer than engineering and construction combined. Environmental review, landowner negotiation and agency approval commonly run 3–7 years on a new EHV corridor.
  • Foundation design depends entirely on geotechnical investigation along the route; a rock line and a swamp line have very different structure costs for identical electrical design.
  • Electric and magnetic field profiles at the ROW edge are a public-consultation issue whether or not they are a regulatory limit where you are building.
  • Constructability: access roads, pulling sites, outage windows on crossed circuits. A design that cannot be built at a reasonable cost is not a design.

Check yourself

Why does N-1 rather than normal loading size the conductor?

Planning criteria require the system to survive the loss of any single element. With two parallel circuits, losing one puts the entire 900 MW on the survivor. If you sized for the normal 450 MW per circuit, the first contingency would overload the remaining line and cascade. The conductor is sized for the worst credible flow, not the average one.

Doubling the ruling span from 1,000 to 2,000 ft — good idea?

It halves the structure count, which is tempting. But sag goes as L², so sag quadruples from about 22 ft to 87 ft, and attachment height must rise by roughly 65 ft to keep the same clearance. Taller structures cost far more per unit and need bigger foundations. There is a cost minimum, usually in the 800–1,400 ft range for EHV, and it is found by iterating this trade rather than by picking an extreme.

The line has 5.5 Ω of resistance and carries 1,537 A under contingency. What is the loss?

P = 3 I²R = 3 × 1537² × 5.5 = 39.0 MW, about 4.3% of the 900 MW delivered. Under normal two-circuit operation each circuit carries 769 A, so total loss is 2 × 3 × 769² × 5.5 = 19.5 MW — the contingency case is four times worse per circuit because loss goes as I².

Why is the loss factor smaller than the load factor?

Loss varies with the square of current while energy varies linearly, so a line that averages 60% of peak current does not average 60% of peak loss — it averages closer to 0.6² adjusted for the shape of the load curve. The common approximation is LsF ≈ 0.3·LF + 0.7·LF², which for a 0.6 load factor gives about 0.43.
