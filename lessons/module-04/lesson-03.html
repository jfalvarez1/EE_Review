Interview Prep — Semiconductors and Diodes

Diode questions are usually the opening move. They are quick, they have clean answers, and they let an interviewer find out in ninety seconds whether you think about devices physically or as symbols with a 0.7 V drop attached.

The single most valuable thing to know here is that 0.7 V is not a property of a diode. It is roughly where the exponential crosses the current you happen to be using. Say that out loud and most of the follow-ups become easy.
\[ I = I_S\left(e^{V/nV_T}-1\right), \qquad V_T = \frac{kT}{q} = 25.9\,\mathrm{mV} \text{ at } 300\,\mathrm{K} \]

Questions

1 Derive Warm-up

Why is a diode drop "0.7 V"? What is it at 1 µA? At 10 A?

How to answer
\[ V = nV_T\ln\frac{I}{I_S} \] Logarithmic, so the drop moves slowly with current — which is why one number seems to work.
\[ \Delta V = nV_T\ln(10) = 60\,\mathrm{mV\ per\ decade} \quad (n=1) \] The number to memorise. It falls out of the exponential and it answers most diode questions.
\[ 1\,\mathrm{mA} \to 0.7\,\mathrm{V}; \quad 1\,\mu\mathrm{A} \to 0.7 - 3(0.06) = 0.52\,\mathrm{V} \]
\[ 10\,\mathrm{A} \to 0.7 + 4(0.06) = 0.94\,\mathrm{V}, \text{ plus } I\!\cdot\!R_S \] At high current the bulk series resistance dominates and the real drop is well above the exponential's prediction — often 1.2 V or more for a power rectifier.

"So 0.7 V is a convention for silicon at about a milliamp. A Schottky is 0.3 V because its barrier height is lower, not because it obeys a different equation, and an LED is 2 to 3 V because its bandgap is larger. Same exponential, different \(I_S\)."

What they are testing: whether "0.7 V" is a fact you memorised or a point on a curve you understand. 60 mV/decade is the whole answer and it takes one sentence.
2 Derive Standard

A diode's forward drop falls about 2 mV per °C. But the equation has \(V_T = kT/q\), which rises with temperature. Resolve that.

How to answer

Two temperature dependences pull opposite ways, and \(I_S\) wins decisively.

\[ V_T = \frac{kT}{q}: \quad +0.086\,\mathrm{mV/°C} \] Small and positive.
\[ I_S \propto T^3 e^{-E_g/kT} \] Enormously temperature dependent — roughly doubling every 10 °C. Since \(V = nV_T\ln(I/I_S)\), a rising \(I_S\) drives the drop DOWN hard.
\[ \frac{dV}{dT} = \frac{V - E_g/q}{T} \approx \frac{0.7 - 1.12}{300} = -1.4\,\mathrm{mV/°C} \] And with the \(T^3\) term included it comes out near −2 mV/°C, which is the number everyone quotes.

"And notice the result extrapolates to the bandgap voltage at absolute zero — which is exactly the fact a bandgap reference exploits. You add a \(V_{BE}\) with its −2 mV/°C to a scaled \(\Delta V_{BE}\) with its +0.086 mV/°C, multiply the second by about 23, and the sum is flat. Every voltage reference in every chip you have ever used is that cancellation."

What they are testing: whether you can hold two competing effects in your head and say which dominates. Getting to bandgap references from here without being prompted is the strong answer.
3 Trap Standard

A capacitor-input rectifier delivers 1 A DC. What is the diode's average current, and what should you rate it for?

How to answer

Average is 1 A (or 0.5 A each in a bridge). Rate it for far more, because the current comes in narrow spikes.

\[ \text{Conduction angle} \approx 10\text{–}20° \text{ of } 360° \] The diode only conducts while the transformer voltage exceeds the capacitor voltage, which is a small slice near each peak.
\[ \frac{I_{peak}}{I_{avg}} \approx 5\text{–}15 \;\Longrightarrow\; I_{peak} \approx 5\text{–}15\,\mathrm{A} \]
\[ I_{rms} \approx 2\text{–}3 \times I_{avg}, \text{ and } I^2R \text{ heating follows RMS} \]

"So the transformer has to be rated for the RMS, not the DC — a 1 A supply needs roughly a 2 A transformer winding — and the diode's surge rating matters at power-on, when the output capacitor is empty and looks like a short. The inrush is limited only by the transformer resistance and the wiring, which is why NTC thermistors or inrush resistors exist. And it is why the power factor of a plain capacitor-input rectifier is around 0.6: the current is the wrong shape, not out of phase."

What they are testing: the distinction between average, RMS and peak in a nonlinear circuit. Most people size a rectifier from the DC current, and it is the reason a lot of first-build power supplies run hot.
4 Trap Harder

A Schottky has a lower forward drop, so why not use one everywhere?

How to answer

Three reasons, and the third is the one that catches people.

  • Reverse leakage. Orders of magnitude higher than a pn diode, and it roughly doubles every 10 °C. At 125 °C a Schottky's leakage can be milliamps.
  • Low reverse voltage. Practically limited to a couple of hundred volts, and the low-drop parts are the lowest-voltage ones.
  • Thermal runaway in the reverse direction. Leakage rises with temperature, leakage dissipates \(V_R I_{leak}\), which raises the temperature, which raises the leakage. In a hot, high-reverse-voltage application a Schottky can run away and fail — which is the opposite of the failure mode people expect from a "low loss" part.

"So the trade is forward loss against reverse loss, and it depends on duty cycle and temperature. In a synchronous buck at high duty the diode conducts briefly and blocks for most of the cycle, so leakage can genuinely dominate. I would also mention that in that application the interesting parameter is not the forward drop at all — it is the reverse recovery charge, and a Schottky's near-zero \(Q_{rr}\) is usually the real reason it was chosen."

What they are testing: whether you evaluate a component across its whole operating cycle rather than at one point. Reverse thermal runaway is a genuine failure mode that is not widely known.
5 Trap Harder

You put a zener across a supply for overvoltage protection. It does not protect anything. Why?

How to answer

A zener is a shunt regulator, not a clamp, and it has three problems in this role.

  • Soft knee. A zener does not conduct abruptly at its rated voltage; it has significant dynamic resistance, so the clamped voltage rises with current. A "5.1 V" zener passing an amp might be sitting at 7 V.
  • No energy rating. Zeners are specified for continuous power, not for a joule of transient. A surge that a TVS shrugs off will destroy a similarly-priced zener.
  • Slow, relatively. Fine for a supply excursion, not for ESD.

"For transient protection I would use a TVS, which is built for exactly this — rated in joules and watts of peak pulse power, with a specified clamping voltage at a specified surge current, and a much sharper knee. And I would check the standoff voltage sits above the maximum normal rail, or it conducts all the time and cooks. Zeners are still the right answer for a low-current reference or a fixed bias point, which is what they were designed for."

What they are testing: whether you match a component to a job. "Zener for protection" is a common instinct and usually the wrong part; knowing that the distinction is energy rating and knee sharpness is the answer.
6 Rapid

What is reverse recovery and why does it matter?

Answer

A pn diode stores minority charge while conducting, and that charge has to be swept out before it can block. So at turn-off it conducts backwards for a time \(t_{rr}\), carrying a charge \(Q_{rr}\). In a hard-switched bridge that reverse current flows through the opposite switch while it is turning on, producing a current spike, extra loss and EMI. It is why fast-recovery and ultrafast diodes exist, why Schottkys — majority carrier, no stored charge — are preferred where the voltage allows, and why SiC and GaN are attractive at higher voltages.

7 Rapid

Two diodes in parallel to share current. Does it work?

Answer

Badly, for pn diodes. The forward drop has a negative temperature coefficient, so the one carrying more current gets hotter, its drop falls, and it takes more still — positive feedback, and one device ends up carrying nearly everything. The fixes are small series ballast resistors, or mounting both on the same heatsink so they thermally couple, or choosing a single larger part. Schottkys share somewhat better because their drop is more resistive, but the same caution applies.

If you remember three things

60 mV per decade
0.7 V is a point on a curve, not a property. This is how you move along it.
−2 mV/°C is \(I_S\) winning
Over the rising \(V_T\), and it extrapolates to the bandgap. That is the reference.
Average, RMS and peak differ
A rectifier conducts for 15° of the cycle. Rate the parts for the spike.
