Diodes in Practice — an Overview

This lesson is deliberately a survey, and it is worth saying why. A classical Electronics I spends a fortnight on rectifier design: half-wave, full-wave, centre-tapped, bridge, each with its own ripple derivation. Almost none of that is work an analog engineer does now. You buy a supply module, or an integrated regulator, or a PFC controller with the bridge built in.

What has not gone away is the intuition: how big the input capacitor must be, why the peak current is many times the average, and why the diode you chose is getting hot. That survives, so it is what this lesson covers. The design procedures do not.

1. Rectification, at the level you need it

Three topologies, one paragraph each, then the only equation worth memorising.

TopologyDiodesRipple frequencyDropStill used?
Half-wave1\(f_{line}\)1 × \(V_F\)Rarely. Tiny loads, and RF envelope detectors.
Full-wave, centre-tapped2\(2f_{line}\)1 × \(V_F\)Only where the transformer already has the tap. Low-voltage, high-current.
Bridge4\(2f_{line}\)2 × \(V_F\)Yes — the default, and it is one four-pin part.

The doubled ripple frequency is the reason full-wave wins: you get twice as many recharge events per second, so the same ripple needs half the capacitance.

\[ I = C\frac{dV}{dt} \;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta V = \frac{I\,\Delta t}{C} \] The capacitor supplies the load alone between peaks, so its voltage droops linearly at a rate set by the load current.
\[ \Delta t \approx \frac{1}{f_{ripple}} \;\Longrightarrow\; \boxed{\;V_{ripple} \approx \frac{I_{load}}{f_{ripple}\,C}\;} \] Assuming the recharge pulse is short compared with the period, which it is. This slightly overestimates ripple, which is the right direction for a design margin.
\[ \text{50 Hz mains, bridge, }1\,\mathrm{A},\ C=4700\,\mathrm{\mu F}:\quad V_{ripple} = \frac{1}{100 \times 4700\times10^{-6}} = 2.1\,\mathrm{V} \] Two volts of ripple on a supply you probably wanted flat. This is why the regulator after it exists, and why its dropout voltage has to accommodate the ripple troughs, not the average.

2. The part everyone gets wrong: peak current

The capacitor only draws current while the input is above its stored voltage — a narrow sliver at the top of each half-cycle. All the charge must move in that window.

\[ Q_{out} = I_{load}\cdot\frac{1}{f_{ripple}} \quad\text{must equal}\quad Q_{in} = I_{peak}\cdot t_{cond} \] Charge balance over one cycle. Whatever leaves must arrive.
\[ \frac{I_{peak}}{I_{load}} = \frac{1}{f_{ripple}\,t_{cond}} \]
\[ t_{cond} \approx 1\,\mathrm{ms\ of\ a\ }10\,\mathrm{ms\ half\text{-}cycle}:\quad \frac{I_{peak}}{I_{load}} \approx 10 \] A 1 A load draws 10 A peaks. This is not a corner case, it is normal.
What that ratio explains. Why the diode's surge rating \(I_{FSM}\) matters more than its average rating; why the transformer runs hot at a modest average current; why these supplies have an awful power factor; and why a switch-mode supply with active PFC exists at all. It also explains the loud buzz from a cheap linear supply's transformer — that is 10 A pulses at 100 Hz doing magnetostriction.

4. Clamping and protection — what is actually used

Zener diodes still appear in textbooks as shunt regulators. They are almost never used that way now: a TL431 or a two-terminal reference does the job with an order of magnitude better tempco and load regulation, for similar money. Zeners survive in three roles:

  • Crude overvoltage clamps where accuracy does not matter.
  • Level shifting by a fixed drop, in gate drive and bias strings.
  • The 5–6 V sweet spot, where the avalanche and Zener mechanisms have opposite tempcos and cancel — giving a genuinely stable reference around 5.6 V, which is the basis of buried-Zener references, still the lowest-noise references money can buy.

For protection, the modern parts are different devices with a similar shape:

PartForNote
TVS diodeTransients, ESD, load dumpBuilt for energy, not precision. Specified in joules and in clamping voltage at a stated surge current.
Schottky clamp pairHolding an input inside the railsThe classic pair to \(V_{CC}\) and ground. Watch the leakage into high-impedance nodes.
Series blocking diodeReverse-polarity protectionCosts a \(V_F\). A PMOS with its body diode pointing the right way costs a few tens of millivolts instead, and is the standard modern answer.
Clamping into a high-impedance node is a trap. A BAT54 leaks about \(2\,\mathrm{\mu A}\) at 25 °C and doubles every 10 °C. Clamp the input of a \(10^{12}\,\Omega\) electrometer with one and the leakage is many orders of magnitude larger than the signal current. The protection destroys the measurement it was added to defend — and the fix is a low-leakage part, or a guard ring, or accepting that this input cannot be protected that way.

Problem Set

1Level 1

A bridge rectifier on 60 Hz mains feeds \(500\,\mathrm{mA}\). What capacitance keeps ripple below \(1\,\mathrm{V}\)?

Solution

\[ C \ge \frac{I}{f_{ripple}V_{ripple}} = \frac{0.5}{120 \times 1} = 4170\,\mathrm{\mu F} \]

Use \(4700\,\mathrm{\mu F}\), the next standard value. Then check the ripple current rating — on a supply this size it is usually the ripple current, not the capacitance, that decides which capacitor you can actually buy.

2Level 2

The same supply, but you switch from a bridge to half-wave to save two diodes. What happens, quantitatively?

Solution

Ripple frequency halves from 120 Hz to 60 Hz, so for the same capacitor the ripple doubles to 2 V. To restore 1 V you need \(8340\,\mathrm{\mu F}\) — twice the capacitance.

You saved two diodes worth a few cents and bought a capacitor at twice the size, cost and volume, with double the ripple current through it. You also gained a DC component in the transformer winding, which pushes the core toward saturation. This is why nobody does it.

3Level 3

A 12 V supply uses a bridge of 1N4007s and a \(4700\,\mathrm{\mu F}\) reservoir, feeding 1 A. Estimate the power dissipated in the bridge, and say whether it needs a heatsink.

Solution

Two diodes conduct at a time, each dropping about 1 V at the peak currents involved — not 0.7 V, because the current in the conduction sliver is roughly 10 A, several decades above where 0.7 V applies.

\[ P \approx 2 \times V_F \times I_{load,avg} = 2 \times 1.0 \times 1 = 2\,\mathrm{W} \]

Using the average current is correct for dissipation, because the diode conducts only while current flows and the charge is what it is. A DO-41 axial part has \(R_{\theta JA} \approx 50\,^\circ\mathrm{C/W}\), and 1 W per diode is a 50 °C rise. That is survivable in free air and marginal in a box: use a bridge module on a tab, or Schottkys to halve the drop.

4Level 4

Explain why the reservoir capacitor's ripple current rating, rather than its capacitance, usually decides which part you buy — and why exceeding it destroys the capacitor rather than merely degrading performance.

Solution

The capacitor sees the full pulse current on charge and the load current on discharge, so its RMS ripple current is several times the DC load current — roughly \(I_{load}\sqrt{I_{peak}/I_{load}}\) for short pulses, so about \(3\times\) at a 10:1 peak ratio.

That current flows through the capacitor's equivalent series resistance, dissipating \(I_{rms}^2 R_{ESR}\) inside the can, where the heat has nowhere to go and the electrolyte is. Raised temperature accelerates electrolyte loss, which raises ESR, which raises dissipation. It is a positive feedback loop that ends in an open circuit or a vented can — and it is why electrolytic life is quoted in hours at a stated temperature, halving for every 10 °C above it.

So the selection order is: ripple current first, then ESR, then capacitance. Capacitance is usually the easy one to satisfy.
