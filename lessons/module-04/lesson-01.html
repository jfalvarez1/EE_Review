The pn Junction and the Exponential

You have been using two numbers on trust. Every op-amp lesson quoted \(V_T = 26\,\mathrm{mV}\); every transistor lesson assumed a junction drops about \(0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) and drifts at \(-2\,\mathrm{mV/^\circ C}\). This lesson derives all three, and one structural fact that everything after it depends on: the junction is exponential.

Scope. This is not a semiconductor physics course. Band diagrams, recombination statistics and transport equations are deliberately absent — you will not use them. What is here is the small set of results that a working analog engineer uses constantly, each one derived far enough that you could reconstruct it.

1. Where \(V_T\) comes from

Thermal voltage is not an empirical fudge. It is the ratio of thermal energy to electron charge, and it appears wherever a carrier has to climb a potential barrier by thermal agitation:

\[ V_T = \frac{kT}{q} \] \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant, \(T\) absolute temperature, \(q\) the electron charge. Energy per charge is a voltage.
\[ V_T = \frac{(1.381\times10^{-23}\,\mathrm{J/K})(300\,\mathrm{K})}{1.602\times10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}} = \frac{4.142\times10^{-21}}{1.602\times10^{-19}} \]
\[ V_T = 25.85\,\mathrm{mV} \quad\text{at } 300\,\mathrm{K} \] Everyone says “26 millivolts”. It is 25.85 mV at 300 K (about 27 °C), and it scales with absolute temperature — so at 350 K it is 30.2 mV, a 17% change for a 50 °C rise.
Why you care

\(V_T\) sets the scale of every exponential in analog electronics. It is why a \(60\,\mathrm{mV}\) change in \(V_{BE}\) multiplies collector current by ten, why a \(5\,\mathrm{mV}\) mismatch between two transistors is a \(19\%\) current error, and why a bipolar's \(\gm = \ic/V_T\) has no device parameter in it at all.

2. Doping, and the built-in potential

Pure silicon at room temperature has an intrinsic carrier concentration \(n_i \approx 10^{10}\,\mathrm{cm^{-3}}\) — about one mobile carrier per \(10^{13}\) atoms. Doping replaces a few silicon atoms with ones having a spare electron (\(n\)-type, donor concentration \(N_d\)) or a spare hole (\(p\)-type, \(N_a\)). Under equilibrium the product is fixed:

\[ n\,p = n_i^{\,2} \] Mass action. Raising one carrier population suppresses the other, because recombination scales with the product.
\[ V_{bi} = V_T \ln\!\left(\frac{N_a N_d}{n_i^{\,2}}\right) \] The potential that builds across the junction until diffusion (carriers spreading out) exactly balances drift (the field pulling them back). It is a logarithm because the carrier populations are exponential in potential.
\[ V_{bi} = 0.02585 \ln\!\left(\frac{10^{17}\cdot10^{17}}{(10^{10})^2}\right) = 0.02585\,\ln(10^{14}) = 0.833\,\mathrm{V} \] For typical doping. Note how weakly this depends on doping: a hundredfold change in \(N_a N_d\) moves \(V_{bi}\) by only \(V_T\ln(100) = 119\,\mathrm{mV}\). Junction voltages are all “about 0.7” for the same reason all logarithms are about the same size.
The one fact about \(n_i\) worth carrying. It rises roughly exponentially with temperature, and reverse leakage follows it — leakage current roughly doubles every 10 °C. That single rule explains why an electrometer needs a cool front end, why leakage sets the floor on long integration times, and why datasheets specify \(I_R\) at 25 °C and again at 125 °C.

3. The diode equation, and the 60 mV rule

Forward bias lowers the barrier, and the carrier population that can cross it is exponential in the barrier height. That gives the Shockley equation:

\[ I_D = I_S\!\left(e^{V_D/(n V_T)} - 1\right) \] \(I_S\) is the saturation current, set by geometry and doping; \(n\) is the ideality factor, 1 for an ideal junction and 1.5–2 for a real small-signal diode.
\[ I_D \approx I_S\,e^{V_D/(n V_T)} \quad\text{for } V_D \gg n V_T \] Above about 100 mV the \(-1\) is negligible — you are subtracting one from a number in the thousands. Every forward-bias calculation uses this form.
\[ V_D = n V_T \ln\!\left(\frac{I_D}{I_S}\right) \] Inverted. The junction voltage is logarithmic in current, which is why it barely moves: a decade of current costs one \(\ln 10\).
\[ \Delta V_D \big|_{\times 10} = n V_T \ln 10 = 2.303\,n V_T = \mathbf{59.5\,mV} \ \ (n=1) \] The famous 60 mV per decade. For a real 1N4148 with \(n \approx 1.8\) it is nearer \(107\,\mathrm{mV}\) per decade — and measuring that slope is how you find \(n\), which Lab 27 does.
Result \[ \boxed{\;V_D = n V_T \ln\!\left(\frac{I_D}{I_S}\right),\qquad \frac{\partial V_D}{\partial(\log_{10} I_D)} = 2.303\,nV_T\;} \]

4. The \(-2\,\mathrm{mV/^\circ C}\), derived

This one surprises people. \(V_T\) rises with temperature, so you might expect the forward drop to rise too. It falls, because \(I_S\) rises much faster. Hold the current constant and differentiate:

\[ I_S \propto T^{3}e^{-E_g/kT} \] The saturation current carries both a weak power-law term and a strong exponential in the bandgap \(E_g = 1.12\,\mathrm{eV}\) for silicon. The exponential dominates completely.
\[ \frac{\partial V_D}{\partial T}\bigg|_{I_D} = \frac{V_D}{T} - \frac{3V_T}{T} - \frac{E_g}{qT} \] Differentiate \(V_D = V_T\ln(I_D/I_S)\) with \(I_D\) fixed. The first term comes from \(V_T \propto T\), the other two from \(I_S(T)\).
\[ = \frac{0.65}{300} - \frac{3(0.02585)}{300} - \frac{1.12}{300} = 2.17 - 0.26 - 3.73 \ \ \mathrm{mV/K} \]
\[ \frac{\partial V_D}{\partial T} = -1.83\,\mathrm{mV/^\circ C} \] The bandgap term is the big one and it is negative, so the drop falls. Everyone rounds it to \(-2\,\mathrm{mV/^\circ C}\).
Two circuits live on this number. A diode makes a serviceable temperature sensor, which is how the thermal shutdown inside almost every power IC works. And because it is negative and predictable, it can be cancelled against a term proportional to absolute temperature — which is exactly what a bandgap reference does, and why bandgap references output about \(1.2\,\mathrm{V}\): the silicon bandgap, extrapolated to 0 K.

5. Three models, and what each costs you

You will almost never use the full equation by hand. Pick the simplest model whose error you can live with:

ModelStatementUse it whenTypical error
Ideal switch \(V_D = 0\) when on Supply voltages \(\gg 0.7\,\mathrm{V}\); topology sketches 0.7 V absolute — \(6\%\) on a 12 V rail, \(21\%\) on 3.3 V
Constant drop \(V_D = 0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) when on Almost all hand analysis and biasing \(\pm 100\,\mathrm{mV}\) over a decade of current or 50 °C
Small-signal \(r_d = n V_T / I_D\) AC analysis about a bias point Valid for signals \(\ll V_T\); distorts above ~5 mV

The third deserves its own derivation, because it is the one you will use inside every transistor model for the rest of the course:

\[ r_d \equiv \left(\frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_D}\right)^{-1} \] Small-signal resistance is the reciprocal slope of the I-V curve at the operating point — not \(V/I\), which would be the chord and is a different and wrong number.
\[ \frac{\partial I_D}{\partial V_D} = \frac{\partial}{\partial V_D}\left(I_S e^{V_D/nV_T}\right) = \frac{I_S e^{V_D/nV_T}}{nV_T} = \frac{I_D}{nV_T} \] The derivative of an exponential is itself, over the scale factor — so the slope is proportional to the current already flowing.
\[ \boxed{\;r_d = \frac{nV_T}{I_D}\;} \qquad \text{at } 1\,\mathrm{mA},\ n=1:\ r_d = 25.9\,\Omega \] This is the same relation as a BJT's \(r_e = V_T/I_E \approx 25/I_E(\mathrm{mA})\,\Omega\). It is the same physics: a bipolar's base-emitter junction is a diode.

BAT54Schottky, SOT-23~0.32 V~2 µASmall-signal clamping and OR-ing.
The Schottky trade, in one line. A metal-semiconductor junction has no minority-carrier storage, so it drops half as much and switches far faster — and leaks ten to a thousand times more, with leakage that doubles every 10 °C like everything else. In a hot circuit a Schottky's leakage can exceed the signal you are trying to measure. Half the drop is not free.

7. What bites you

  • 0.7 V is a habit, not a value. A 1N4148 at 1 µA drops about 0.45 V; at 100 mA, nearly 1 V. If your circuit's behaviour depends on the drop to better than \(\pm100\,\mathrm{mV}\), you must use the current you actually have.
  • Two diodes in parallel do not share current. The exponential guarantees it: 20 mV of \(V_F\) mismatch at \(n=1\) puts \(2.2\times\) more current through one than the other, and the hotter one's drop then falls further. Paralleling needs ballast resistors, or one package containing both dice.
  • Reverse recovery is not on the DC curve. A silicon rectifier conducts backwards for tens or hundreds of nanoseconds after the current reverses, while stored charge is swept out. In a switching converter that is a shoot-through current spike and a major loss term — and the reason Schottky or fast-recovery parts exist.
  • The small-signal model dies above a few millivolts. \(r_d = nV_T/I_D\) is the first term of a Taylor series about the bias point. A 10 mV signal on a 26 mV scale is not small, and the second-order term is your distortion.

Problem Set — the junction

All answerable from this lesson. Levels 1–2 check the mechanism; 3–4 ask you to invert it or carry it somewhere it was not derived.

1Level 1

A diode drops \(0.60\,\mathrm{V}\) at \(100\,\mathrm{\mu A}\). With \(n = 1.8\) and \(V_T = 25.9\,\mathrm{mV}\), what does it drop at \(10\,\mathrm{mA}\)?

Solution

Two decades up, at \(2.303\,nV_T\) per decade:

\[ \Delta V = 2\times 2.303(1.8)(0.0259) = 2\times 0.1073 = 0.215\,\mathrm{V} \]

\[ V_D = 0.60 + 0.215 = \mathbf{0.815\,V} \]

Not 0.7. The constant-drop model would have been wrong by 115 mV here, which is the whole reason to know the slope.

2Level 1

Find \(r_d\) for a diode at \(250\,\mathrm{\mu A}\) with \(n = 1\). Then state the largest signal for which the small-signal model is reasonable.

Solution

\[ r_d = \frac{nV_T}{I_D} = \frac{0.0259}{250\times10^{-6}} = 103.4\,\Omega \]

The model is a first-order expansion about the bias point, valid while the signal is small compared with \(nV_T = 25.9\,\mathrm{mV}\). A common working limit is \(\le 5\,\mathrm{mV}\) peak, which keeps second-harmonic distortion around \(v/(4nV_T) \approx 5\%\). “Small signal” means small compared with 26 mV, not small in absolute terms.

3Level 2

A silicon diode is used as a thermometer, biased at a constant \(100\,\mathrm{\mu A}\). It reads \(0.650\,\mathrm{V}\) at 25 °C. What is the temperature when it reads \(0.500\,\mathrm{V}\)?

Solution

\[ \Delta V = 0.500 - 0.650 = -0.150\,\mathrm{V} \]

\[ \Delta T = \frac{-0.150}{-2\times10^{-3}} = 75\,^\circ\mathrm{C} \quad\Rightarrow\quad T = \mathbf{100\,^\circ C} \]

This is exactly how the thermal shutdown in a power IC works, and why it is cheap: the sensor is a junction you already had.

4Level 2

Two 1N4148s are paralleled to share \(200\,\mathrm{mA}\). Their \(V_F\) at a given current differs by \(20\,\mathrm{mV}\). Assuming \(n=1\), how does the current actually divide?

Solution

In parallel they share a voltage, so the currents are in the ratio

\[ \frac{I_1}{I_2} = e^{\Delta V/nV_T} = e^{0.020/0.0259} = e^{0.772} = 2.16 \]

\[ I_1 = 200\times\frac{2.16}{3.16} = 137\,\mathrm{mA},\qquad I_2 = 63\,\mathrm{mA} \]

One diode takes more than twice the other, and it is not finished: the hotter one's \(V_F\) then falls at \(-2\,\mathrm{mV/^\circ C}\), taking more current still. This is thermal runaway in miniature, and the reason paralleled devices need ballast resistance or shared silicon.

5Level 3

A red LED (\(V_F \approx 1.9\,\mathrm{V}\) at 10 mA) is driven from 5 V through a resistor. Size it for 10 mA. Then find the current if the LED is from the other end of the bin and drops \(2.2\,\mathrm{V}\).

Solution

\[ R = \frac{5 - 1.9}{10\,\mathrm{mA}} = 310\,\Omega \ \rightarrow\ \text{use } 330\,\Omega \ (\mathrm{E24}) \]

With \(330\,\Omega\) and \(V_F = 1.9\): \(I = 3.1/330 = 9.4\,\mathrm{mA}\).

With \(V_F = 2.2\): \(I = 2.8/330 = 8.5\,\mathrm{mA}\) — a \(10\%\) change in current for a \(0.3\,\mathrm{V}\) spread.

The lesson is the ratio. The resistor drops 3.1 V, so a 0.3 V uncertainty is a tenth of it. Drive the same LED from 2.5 V and the resistor drops only 0.6 V, so that same 0.3 V spread is half the budget and the current is anyone's guess. Low-voltage LED drive needs a current source, not a resistor — and that is why constant-current LED drivers exist.

6Level 3

Derive the temperature coefficient result of section 4 for a diode biased at \(V_D = 0.75\,\mathrm{V}\) instead of 0.65 V. Does the tempco get better or worse, and why?

Solution

\[ \frac{\partial V_D}{\partial T} = \frac{V_D}{T} - \frac{3V_T}{T} - \frac{E_g}{qT} = \frac{0.75}{300} - \frac{0.0776}{300} - \frac{1.12}{300} \]

\[ = 2.50 - 0.26 - 3.73 = -1.49\,\mathrm{mV/^\circ C} \]

Better — smaller in magnitude. The only term that depends on the operating point is \(V_D/T\), and it is positive, so biasing harder pushes the tempco toward zero. It never reaches it: you would need \(V_D = E_g/q + 3V_T = 1.20\,\mathrm{V}\), which is more than a silicon junction will drop. That number is not a coincidence — it is the bandgap voltage, and it is the output a bandgap reference is built to produce.

7Level 4

You measure a diode at three currents and get \(0.400\,\mathrm{V}\) at \(1\,\mathrm{\mu A}\), \(0.512\,\mathrm{V}\) at \(10\,\mathrm{\mu A}\), and \(0.623\,\mathrm{V}\) at \(100\,\mathrm{\mu A}\). Extract \(n\) and \(I_S\).

Solution

The slope per decade is consistent: 112 mV and 111 mV. Take 111.5 mV.

\[ 2.303\,nV_T = 0.1115 \;\Rightarrow\; n = \frac{0.1115}{2.303(0.0259)} = \mathbf{1.87} \]

Now extrapolate to find \(I_S\), using any point:

\[ I_S = I_D e^{-V_D/nV_T} = 10^{-6}\exp\!\left(\frac{-0.400}{1.87\times0.0259}\right) = 10^{-6}e^{-8.26} = \mathbf{2.6\times10^{-10}\,A} \]

About \(0.26\,\mathrm{nA}\). Note that \(I_S\) is found by extrapolating six decades below where you measured — it is exquisitely sensitive to the fitted slope, and a 1% error in \(n\) moves it by tens of percent. \(I_S\) is a fitting parameter, not something you can measure directly.

8Challenge

A designer proposes measuring temperature by biasing one diode at \(10\,\mathrm{\mu A}\) and an identical one at \(1\,\mathrm{mA}\), and amplifying the difference in their forward drops. Show that this is far better than measuring one diode's absolute drop, and identify what still limits it.

Solution

For two junctions at the same temperature with the same \(I_S\):

\[ \Delta V = nV_T\ln\!\frac{I_1}{I_S} - nV_T\ln\!\frac{I_2}{I_S} = nV_T\ln\!\left(\frac{I_1}{I_2}\right) \]

\(I_S\) cancels completely. That is the whole trick: \(I_S\) is the badly-controlled parameter — it varies with geometry, doping and lot — and it disappears from a difference of two junctions on the same die.

With a ratio of 100 and \(n=1\):

\[ \Delta V = 0.0259\ln(100) = 119\,\mathrm{mV} \ \text{at } 300\,\mathrm{K} \]

and since \(V_T \propto T\), this is proportional to absolute temperature — a PTAT voltage, with a clean \(+0.397\,\mathrm{mV/^\circ C}\) slope and no extrapolated constants in it.

What still limits it: the two diodes must be at the same temperature and must actually match, so this only works on one die; the current ratio must be accurate, since an error in \(I_1/I_2\) enters as \(nV_T\ln(\text{ratio error})\); and \(n\) must be equal for both, which is why the technique uses transistors' base-emitter junctions rather than discrete diodes. Add this PTAT term to a \(V_{BE}\) that falls at \(-2\,\mathrm{mV/^\circ C}\), weighted to cancel, and you have built a bandgap reference — which is the subject of a later lesson and now needs no new physics.

Lab — measure the exponential, and extract \(n\) and \(I_S\)

Time 50 min Skill log-linear fitting; decade sweeps Parts 1N4148, 1N5817 Schottky, resistors, DMM

The diode equation predicts a straight line on semi-log axes with a slope of \(2.303\,nV_T\). This measures that line over five decades using nothing but a supply, a resistor and a voltmeter — and gets both device parameters out of it.

Method. Force a known current with a large series resistor from a high voltage, so \(I \approx (V_{supply} - V_D)/R\) is insensitive to \(V_D\). Then measure \(V_D\). Change \(R\) by decades.

  1. Wire supply → \(R\) → diode → ground. Use \(V_{supply} = 10\,\mathrm{V}\) so the resistor dominates.
  2. For each \(R\) in {10 M, 1 M, 100 k, 10 k, 1 k}, measure \(V_D\) with the DMM across the diode. Compute the actual current as \((10 - V_D)/R\) — do not assume the nominal decade.
  3. Plot \(V_D\) against \(\log_{10} I_D\). It should be a straight line. Fit the slope in mV per decade.
  4. Extract \(n = \text{slope}/(2.303\,V_T)\) with \(V_T = 25.9\,\mathrm{mV}\), then \(I_S = I_D e^{-V_D/nV_T}\) from any point.
  5. Repeat with the Schottky. Compare both the intercept and the slope.
  6. Pinch the silicon diode between your fingers while watching \(V_D\) at the 10 k point. Estimate \(\partial V_D/\partial T\) from the shift and your body temperature.

What you should find. A 1N4148 gives roughly 100–115 mV per decade, so \(n \approx 1.7\)–\(1.9\), and \(I_S\) in the range \(0.1\)–\(3\,\mathrm{nA}\). The Schottky's line sits about \(0.3\,\mathrm{V}\) to the left — a much larger \(I_S\) — with a similar slope. Finger-warming should move \(V_D\) down by roughly \(20\,\mathrm{mV}\) for a \(10\,^\circ\mathrm{C}\) rise.

Where it goes wrong, and why that is the interesting part. At \(10\,\mathrm{M\Omega}\) you are sourcing about \(1\,\mathrm{\mu A}\), and a \(10\,\mathrm{M\Omega}\) DMM in parallel with the diode steals a comparable current. The lowest-current point will sit below the line. That is not a bad measurement — it is the same instrument-loading problem as Lab 1, met again in a different disguise, and the fix is the same: know your meter's input impedance and stop before it dominates. A DMM is not a high-impedance instrument at \(1\,\mathrm{\mu A}\).
