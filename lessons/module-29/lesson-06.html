Layout Strategy: Placement, Partitioning and Flow

Routing gets all the attention because it is the part that looks like work. But by the time you start routing, almost every important decision has already been made: the stackup fixed the physics, and the placement fixed which connections are short and which are long. A good placement makes routing nearly automatic; a bad one cannot be routed well by anyone, and the effort spent trying is wasted.

Placement is most of layout. Spend the time there. Move parts around for an hour before drawing a single trace, and be willing to move them again after the first routing attempt reveals what you got wrong. Re-placing is cheap; re-routing a board built on a bad placement is not.
Floorplanning a kitchen

A kitchen is designed around the work triangle: fridge, sink, hob. Get those three positions right and the cooking flows; get them wrong and no amount of beautiful cabinetry fixes it, because you will walk the wrong path ten thousand times.

A board has the same structure. Connector, converter, processor, memory — the critical loops are decided by where you put things, and the traces are just the walking. Nobody solves a badly planned kitchen by choosing nicer floor tiles.

Where it breaks: a kitchen has one cook and one flow. A board has several simultaneous flows — power, high-speed signals, analogue, thermal — that compete for the same space and sometimes want opposite things. Layout is the negotiation between them, which is why there is rarely one correct answer.

1. The order of operations

StepWhat you doWhat you must not do yet
1. MechanicalBoard outline, mounting holes, keep-outs, connector positions fixed by the enclosure, height restrictionsNothing electrical. These are not negotiable and everything else works around them.
2. StackupAgree it with the fabricator; get impedance-controlled widths backDo not start routing against an assumed stackup
3. PartitionDraw regions on the board: power in, conversion, digital, analogue, RF, I/O. On paper, before the CAD tool.Do not place individual components yet
4. Anchor placementPlace the parts whose position is forced — connectors, the big processor, the magnetics, anything with a heatsinkDo not place passives
5. Critical loopsPlace the parts that form loops that must be small: switching regulator input loop, decoupling, crystal, current-senseDo not compromise these for routing convenience later
6. Everything elseRemaining passives, pull-ups, test points
7. Route power firstWide, short, deliberate. Power is harder to move than signals.Do not let the autorouter near it
8. Route critical signalsClocks, high-speed buses, differential pairs, sensitive analogue
9. Route the restEverything remaining
10. Pour and stitchCopper pours, ground stitching vias, thermal relief decisionsDo not use a pour to fix a routing mistake

Step 3 is the one people skip, and it is the one that matters most. Ten minutes with a pencil deciding where the noisy things live and where the quiet things live saves a week of EMC debugging. It also makes step 5 obvious, because once the regions exist you can see which loops cross them.

2. Partitioning: separation without splitting

The previous lessons established that splitting a ground plane is almost always wrong. Partitioning is how you get the isolation you wanted without the split: separate the currents by geometry, not by cutting copper.

Return currents flow directly under their own traces. So if the digital traces are all in one region of the board, their return currents are all in that region of the plane, and they never visit the analogue region — without any barrier existing. The plane stays solid, every return path is continuous, and the isolation comes for free from the physics.

RegionWhere it goesWhy
Power entry, protection, filteringAt the connector, at the board edgeConducted emissions are filtered before they reach anything, and surge energy is dumped near where it entered
Switching convertersBetween power entry and the loads, away from analogue and RFThe loudest thing on the board. Distance is the cheapest attenuation there is.
Digital / processorCentral, near its memoryThe buses are the densest routing and want the shortest runs
Analogue / convertersOne region, adjacent to its I/O, away from switchers and clocksIts return currents stay local; nothing loud shares its copper
RFIts own corner, with the antenna at the board edgeAntenna placement is dictated by the enclosure and by keeping metal away from it
I/O and cablesGrouped at one edgeCables are the antennas. Put them where the plane is quiet, and never route noisy signals near the cable entry.
The one rule that makes partitioning work: signals crossing between regions should cross at a single, deliberate, narrow interface. If the digital-to-analogue interface is eight signals crossing at one place, their returns are confined there too. If those same eight signals wander across the board individually, the partition has no meaning.

3. The loops that must be small

A handful of loops on any board carry fast, large current changes. These are placed first and are never compromised for routing convenience, because their area determines both the noise they radiate and the noise they suffer.

LoopWhat it isTarget
Buck input loop Input capacitor → high-side switch → low-side switch → back to the capacitor. This loop carries a trapezoidal current with nanosecond edges. The single most important loop on any switching board. As small as physically possible; the input capacitor goes right against the IC's VIN and GND pins, on the same layer, with no vias in the loop.
Boost output loop Switch → diode → output capacitor → back Same reasoning, on the output side instead — a boost has its discontinuous current at the output
Decoupling loop IC power pin → capacitor → ground → back to the IC's ground pin, including the vias Minimise loop inductance, not distance-to-the-pin. See §4.
Gate drive loop Driver output → gate → source → back to the driver's return Large \(di/dt\); a big loop causes gate ringing and can turn a device on when you did not ask
Crystal loop Crystal, its two load capacitors, and the return to the oscillator's ground pin Small and guarded. A crystal is a high-impedance node and picks up anything nearby.
Current sense The two sense connections to the shunt Kelvin connection, routed as a tightly coupled pair from the resistor's own sense pads
The switch node is the other half of the story. The buck switch node swings the full input voltage in nanoseconds, so it is the board's most effective E-field radiator. Keep it small in area — just big enough to carry the current and conduct heat — and never pour it large “for thermal reasons” without accounting for what that copper radiates.

4. Decoupling, honestly

The traditional advice — a 100 nF at every power pin, plus a 10 µF and a 1 nF “for high frequency” — is mostly folklore built on a misunderstanding. Here is what the physics supports.

BeliefWhat is actually true
“Add a small capacitor in parallel for high frequency” The small capacitor has the same loop inductance as the big one, since the loop is set by the vias and the geometry, not the part. Above resonance both are inductors, so the small one adds almost nothing — and the parallel combination creates an anti-resonance between them where the impedance is higher than either alone. Prefer more capacitors of one value over a ladder of values.
“Put the capacitor as close to the pin as possible” Closer to the loop, not the pin. A capacitor 1 mm from the pin whose ground returns through a via 4 mm away has a worse loop than one 3 mm from the pin with its own via right at its pad. Minimise the enclosed area of the whole path.
“More capacitance is better” Only below resonance. Above it, capacitance is irrelevant and inductance is everything. Ten 100 nF parts in parallel give a tenth of the loop inductance as well as ten times the capacitance — the inductance is the reason it works.
“The MLCC value on the BOM is what you get” X5R and X7R lose a large fraction of their capacitance under DC bias — a 10 µF 0603 rated 6.3 V can be under 3 µF at 5 V. Check the bias curve, not the marking.
“Decoupling handles everything up to a gigahertz” Above roughly 100–200 MHz the board contributes almost nothing; the on-die and in-package capacitance is what supplies the current. Your job at those frequencies is to not make things worse.

5. Rules that are myths

Every layout guide contains rules that have outlived their evidence. Knowing which are which saves both board area and argument.

RuleVerdict
The 20-H rule (pull the power plane back 20× the dielectric height from the board edge) Largely a myth. The original simulation has not held up; measured benefit is small and inconsistent, and it costs real board area. Edge stitching vias around the perimeter do far more for edge radiation.
The 3W rule (space traces 3× their width apart) The wrong variable. Crosstalk is governed by \(s/h\), not \(s/w\). On a thin stackup 2W is generous; on a thick one 5W may not be enough. Use spacing relative to the dielectric height.
Split the ground plane for analogue Actively harmful on any board with signals crossing the split. See lesson 1.
45° corners instead of 90° True, but irrelevant. The original reason was acid entrapment in etching, a solved manufacturing problem. Electrically a right-angle corner adds a tiny capacitance that matters only above roughly 10 GHz. Use 45° because it looks tidy and routes shorter, not because you believe it is fixing anything.
Route a guard trace beside a sensitive signal Conditional. A guard trace helps only if it is grounded at both ends and stitched along its length at < λ/10 intervals. Grounded at one end, it is a floating antenna that can make things worse.
Star grounding Correct for its era, wrong for yours. It solves a low-frequency shared-resistance problem. Above a few kHz the return follows inductance, and a star topology forces enormous loops. Correct for a mains-frequency instrument; wrong for anything with a digital edge in it.
A ground plane under a crystal True and worth doing, along with a guarded pour tied to the oscillator's ground pin. A crystal is a genuinely high-impedance node.
Thermal reliefs on ground pins True for manufacturability, and a real trade: they add inductance and resistance. On decoupling capacitor grounds and power connections, use direct connections and let the assembler adjust their profile.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse The buck input capacitor moved 8 mm away to make room for a connector The input loop grows from perhaps 20 mm² to 200 mm², so its inductance rises roughly tenfold to maybe 12 nH. With a 2 A switched current and a 5 ns edge, \(V = L\,di/dt = 12\,\mathrm{nH}\times 4\times10^{8} = \mathbf{4.8\ V}\) of overshoot on the switch node. On a 12 V input that is 17 V on a device rated 20 V — it survives, marginally, until a hot day or a fast part. The board also fails conducted emissions, because that loop is now an efficient magnetic radiator at the switching harmonics. There is no snubber, ferrite or capacitor that recovers this; it is a placement error and only re-placement fixes it. Put the input capacitor first, then place the connector around it.
Abuse Decoupling capacitors placed 1 mm from the pin but sharing one ground via 5 mm away The loop is not 1 mm; it is 1 mm out, 5 mm along the trace to the via, down the via, and back through the plane. Loop inductance is perhaps 4 nH rather than 1 nH, so the self-resonance of a 100 nF part drops from about 16 MHz to 8 MHz, and its impedance at 100 MHz is four times higher. The layout looks textbook-correct in the CAD tool and performs badly. The capacitor's own via at its own pad is worth more than moving it closer to the pin. This is the most common decoupling mistake and it is invisible unless you deliberately trace the return.
Abuse A 10 µF 6.3 V X5R 0603 used on a 5 V rail DC bias derating: X5R dielectric loses capacitance under field, and at 5 V on a 6.3 V-rated 0603 the effective value can fall below 3 µF — under 30% of the marking. The part is not faulty and measures 10 µF at 0 V bias. The bulk decoupling is a third of what the design assumed, and no measurement on an unbiased part reveals it. This shows up as excessive load-transient droop that the designer cannot explain from the BOM. Use a higher voltage rating (a 25 V part at 5 V derates far less), a larger case size, or a different dielectric — and read the bias curve, which vendors publish and almost nobody opens.
Abuse The autorouter run on a mixed-signal board after a good manual placement The router optimises for completion and length. It has no model of return paths, loop area, partitioning, or which nets are sensitive, so it will happily route a clock across the analogue region and a reset line beside it. The board routes to 100% and fails everything else. Autorouters are useful for dense fan-out and for buses whose constraints you have fully expressed as rules; they are not a substitute for judgement about which connections matter. Route power and every critical net by hand first, lock them, and only then let the router finish the remainder.
Abuse A copper pour used to “fill in” an unrouted region, left unstitched and connected at one point An isolated or single-point-connected pour is a resonant structure. It couples to nearby traces capacitively and re-radiates, and at the frequency where it is a quarter wavelength it becomes an efficient antenna driven by whatever it is coupled to. A floating pour is worse than no pour. Either stitch it to ground at intervals below λ/10 at the highest frequency present, or delete it. This applies equally to the decorative ground pours many designers add by reflex on the outer layers — pour and stitch, or do not pour.
