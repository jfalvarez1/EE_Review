Creepage, Clearance and the Safety Standards

Most PCB rules are engineering judgement: get them wrong and the board performs badly. These are not. Spacing rules are the boundary between a product that is certified and one that is not, and between a fault that blows a fuse and one that electrocutes someone. They are also the area where engineers most often work from a half-remembered number, because the standards are numerous, overlapping and partly contradictory.

This lesson gives you the actual numbers, where they come from, and a procedure you can follow.

Two different distances, two different physics. Clearance is the shortest distance through air. Creepage is the shortest distance along a surface. They are dimensioned for different failure mechanisms, they respond to different variables, and one is not a substitute for the other — though creepage can never be less than clearance, since the surface path cannot be shorter than the straight line.
Jumping the gap versus walking around it

Clearance is how far someone could jump: a single violent event, over in microseconds, that either happens or does not. What decides it is how much energy the jump has — the transient voltage — and how thin the air is, which is why altitude matters and humidity mostly does not.

Creepage is how far someone could walk along the ground: slow, cumulative, and it depends on what the ground is made of and how dirty it is. Contamination and moisture build a conductive film, current trickles through it, and over months it carbonises the surface into a permanent track. What decides it is the steady working voltage, the pollution, and the material.

Where it breaks: a jumper cannot benefit from a wall, but a creeping current can be forced to detour by a groove cut in the board. That is why a slot increases creepage and does nothing at all for clearance — the air path is unchanged.

1. The four inputs you need before you can look anything up

InputWhat it isWhere it comes from
Working voltage The highest RMS or DC voltage the insulation actually sees in normal operation. For creepage. Your circuit. Note it is the voltage across the barrier, not the supply voltage.
Transient / impulse voltage The worst surge the insulation must survive. For clearance. Mains nominal voltage plus overvoltage category, via IEC 60664-1 Table F.1. For non-mains circuits: nominal + 1200 V.
Pollution degree How dirty and damp the environment gets. For creepage. The product's enclosure and intended use.
Material group (CTI) How well the insulator resists surface tracking. For creepage. The laminate datasheet. Most FR4 is group IIIa.

The mixed-material trap. An isolated gate driver may be material group I, needing 4 mm of creepage, while the FR4 it sits on is group IIIa, needing 8 mm. The board must meet the board material's requirement. A 4 mm-creepage package does not let you use 4 mm of copper spacing — you still need 8 mm across the laminate, or a slot.

Overvoltage category is about where in the electrical installation the equipment sits, not about the equipment itself. I: circuits with deliberate transient limiting (a 24 V thermostat behind a transformer). II: plugged into an outlet — almost all consumer and IT equipment. III: permanently wired (fuse-panel switches, hardwired machinery). IV: at the origin of the installation (meters, distribution panels). A 230 V product plugged into a wall socket is OVC II, so its rated impulse is 2.5 kV.

This is genuinely useful: high voltage can sometimes be routed on an inner layer where it would not fit on the surface.

Three things in that table are worth extracting. One: the isolator package needs 4 mm but the FR4 needs 8 mm, so the board is the binding constraint, not the part. Two: altitude corrects clearance but not creepage — thinner air breaks down more easily, but surface tracking does not care. Three: IEC 62368-1's clearance is larger than IEC 60664-1's for the same impulse, so which standard applies to your product category changes the answer. Take the largest that applies.

5. When you cannot make the distance

Three legitimate escapes exist, and each has conditions.

TechniqueWhat it buysThe conditions
A slot or groove in the board Lengthens the surface path, so it increases creepage. Also physically interrupts any carbon track that starts to form. No effect whatever on clearance — the air path is unchanged. And the slot must be wide enough to count: IEC 60664-1 §6.2 requires a minimum width of 0.25 mm at pollution degree 1, 1.0 mm at degree 2, 1.5 mm at degree 3. A 0.4 mm slot on a PD2 board buys nothing.
Conformal coating Can move the assembly from pollution degree 2 to degree 1, which collapses the creepage requirement — at 400 V from 4.0 mm to 1.0 mm. The coating must cover both conductors and the whole path between them, be free of pinholes, and be qualified. This is a real manufacturing commitment, not a spray in the lab.
Routine transient (hipot) test in production Permits reduced spacing where only functional isolation is required. Every unit must be tested, typically at \(2V_{working} + 1000\) V. For a 265 VAC input that is 1530 V — which is why 1.5 kV is such a common test voltage. Not available where user safety depends on the barrier.
The slot width rule catches people. Designers cut a 0.5 mm slot, measure the creepage path around it in the CAD tool, and record a pass. If the product is pollution degree 2 the slot must be at least 1.0 mm wide to be counted at all, so the real creepage is still the straight-line surface distance and the design fails at certification — after the tooling is made.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse The product is sold into a humid coastal industrial site after being designed for pollution degree 2 Salt-laden condensation forms a conductive film across the isolation barrier. At 400 V working the film carries a small leakage current continuously; electrolysis and heating carbonise the epoxy, and the carbon track grows from the higher-potential conductor toward the lower one. This is the failure creepage exists to prevent, and it takes months, so it fails in the field and not in qualification. The endpoint is a low-resistance carbon bridge across the barrier: mains on the SELV output. The correct answer is to design to pollution degree 3 (400 V group IIIa: 8 mm rather than 4 mm) or to seal and coat. There is no test you can do in a week that will reveal this.
Abuse A 5 kV lightning-induced surge on a 230 V mains input designed to OVC II (2.5 kV) Twice the rated impulse. The air gap ionises and arcs across the clearance, and once an arc strikes, the mains follows it — the surge only has to start the conduction. An arc across a primary-to-secondary barrier puts mains on the output. This is precisely why the overvoltage category exists and why a product intended for a fixed installation (OVC III, 4 kV) needs more clearance than one that plugs into a wall. If the equipment might see OVC III conditions, design for OVC III — you cannot control where a customer installs it, and the MOV that would clamp it is a component that can fail open.
Abuse The product is used at 4000 m having been designed and certified at sea level Air density falls, so its breakdown strength falls with it. The IEC 60664-1 Table A.2 correction factor at 4000 m is about 1.29; at 5000 m it is 1.48. A 3.6 mm clearance behaves like 2.8 mm at 4000 m. Creepage is unaffected — surface tracking does not depend on air pressure. Clearance is degraded, so the margin against transients is reduced and a surge that would have been survived at sea level can arc. If the product may be used at altitude, apply the factor at design time; retrofitting clearance is a board respin.
Abuse A 0.5 mm slot cut to gain creepage on a pollution degree 2 design The slot is narrower than the 1.0 mm minimum for PD2, so contamination bridges it and the surface path is effectively unbroken. Physically, nothing has changed. The CAD tool measures the path around the slot and reports a pass; the standard does not accept it. The board fails at certification, after tooling. This is a documentation-and-review failure rather than an electrical one, and the defence is knowing the minimum-width rule exists — which is the entire reason it appears in this lesson.
Abuse Spacing taken from IPC-2221B alone on a mains-connected consumer product At 400 V peak, IPC-2221B B2 asks for 2.5 mm. IEC 60664-1 asks for 4.0 mm of creepage at 400 V working, PD2, group IIIa — and 8 mm if the barrier is reinforced. The board is compliant with a standard that explicitly does not address user safety, and non-compliant with the one that does. IPC-2221 governs whether the board functions; IEC 60664-1 and its family govern whether someone can be hurt. Use both, take the larger, and never let the PCB standard be the only one consulted on a product a person can touch.
