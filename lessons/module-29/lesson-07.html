Reading Real Layouts: Three Boards, Measured

The six lessons before this one gave you rules. This one applies them to three boards that actually exist — a 3.3 V LDO, a TPS564201 buck converter, and a four-layer ignition controller — by reading their .kicad_pcb files and measuring. Every number below is extracted from the file, not estimated from a picture.

Two of the three are DRC-clean and would ship. All three have something worth pointing at, and in each case the defect is invisible to every automated check that was run on them. That gap — between passes the rule checker and is correctly designed — is what this lesson is about.

A spellchecker is not an editor

A spellchecker proves every word is a word. It has nothing to say about whether the sentences mean anything, because meaning is not a property it can see.

DRC proves every clearance is met and every net is connected. It has nothing to say about whether the return path is continuous, whether the feedback trace runs under the inductor, or whether the power trace is wide enough for the current the part can deliver — because none of those are geometric rules, they are intentions the file does not record.

Where it breaks: a spellchecker never makes the prose worse. An autorouter optimising for completion genuinely can make the electrical design worse while improving the metric it was given, which is the third board below.

What it gets right

A solid, filled ground pour on the bottom layer with almost nothing routed through it. That is the correct two-layer strategy from lesson 1: the pour is a reference plane rather than a dumping ground, so every top-layer trace has a return path directly beneath it.

Is 0.25 mm wide enough for 500 mA?

\[ A = 9.84\,\mathrm{mil}\times1.378 = 13.6\ \mathrm{mil^2}, \quad I_{10^\circ C} = 0.048\times10^{0.44}\times13.6^{0.725} = 0.875\ \mathrm{A} \] IPC-2221, external, 1 oz. At the actual 500 mA the rise is \(\left(0.5/0.318\right)^{1/0.44} = \mathbf{2.8\,^\circ C}\). Thermally this is comfortable — the trace is barely warm.

The finding: it is not the heat, it is the drop

\[ R_{Vout} = 0.49\,\mathrm{m\Omega}\times\frac{42.3}{0.25} = 0.49 \times 169 = 82.9\ \mathrm{m\Omega} \] 169 squares of 1 oz copper.
\[ V_{drop} = 0.5 \times 0.0829 = \mathbf{41.5\ mV} = 1.26\%\ \text{of } 3.3\,\mathrm{V} \] And here is why it matters more than it looks: the LDO regulates at its own output pin, not at J2. Its feedback network senses the voltage before the 42 mm of trace. So this 41.5 mV is entirely outside the loop and appears at the connector as load regulation error the regulator cannot see and cannot correct.

An LDO's datasheet load regulation might be 10 mV from no load to full load. The board has quietly added four times that, and the measurement that would reveal it — probing at the connector rather than at the IC — is the one nobody does. On a 3.3 V rail feeding logic this is harmless; on a rail feeding an ADC reference it is 1.26% of full scale.

The fix, in order of cost: widen Vout to 0.8 mm (drop falls to 13 mV) — free, the space exists; or shorten it by moving J2 nearer U1 — also free, and better; or use a regulator with a sense pin and Kelvin-connect it at the load. Note also the two via sizes and the 0.250/0.254 mm width mix: 0.254 mm is exactly 10 mil and 0.250 is metric, which is the signature of two different tools having touched the board. Harmless, but it tells you the file has a history.

What it gets right: the switch node is short

5.6 mm. The switch node swings the full 12 V input in nanoseconds and is the board's most effective E-field radiator, so keeping its copper area small is the single most valuable thing a buck layout does. This one does it.

Finding 1: the feedback trace is 27.6 mm and passes the inductor

Read the placement coordinates. U1 sits at x = 69.7 mm. The feedback divider R2/R3 sits at x = 83.1 mm, beside the output capacitors. Between them, at x = 78.8 mm, is L1.

So the feedback signal — a high-impedance node carrying the information the control loop depends on — has to travel about 13 mm back past the inductor, and the routed length is 27.6 mm, meaning it took a detour on the way. An inductor's fringing field is exactly what a high-impedance node picks up, and injected noise on FB is indistinguishable from a real output change: the loop responds to it.

Why this is a real problem and not a nitpick. Noise on the feedback node produces jitter on the switching edges, which produces subharmonic content in the output ripple and a spread spectral signature that fails EMI scans. It is also very hard to diagnose, because the output looks noisy and the obvious response is to add output capacitance — which does nothing, since the noise is being commanded rather than filtered out.

The fix: route FB on the opposite side of the board from the inductor, or take it around rather than past. Better still, keep the divider at the IC and run a single sense trace to the output — the sense trace carries no current, so it can be thin and it can take the long way round while FB itself stays under 3 mm.

Finding 2: 0.508 mm encodes an assumption nobody wrote down

\[ A = 20\,\mathrm{mil}\times1.378 = 27.6\ \mathrm{mil^2}, \qquad k\,A^{0.725} = 0.048\times11.07 = 0.531 \]
\[ I_{10^\circ C} = 0.531\times10^{0.44} = 1.46\ \mathrm{A} \] So 0.508 mm is a 1.5 A trace at a comfortable rise.
\[ \Delta T = \left(\frac{I}{0.531}\right)^{1/0.44}:\quad 2\,\mathrm{A} \to 20^\circ C,\quad 3\,\mathrm{A} \to 51^\circ C,\quad 4\,\mathrm{A} \to \mathbf{98^\circ C} \] The TPS564201 is rated 4 A. At its rated output this trace runs nearly 100 °C above ambient.

Nothing in the board says what current it is for. The DRC passes at any current, because current is not in the file. The 0.508 mm width is a decision that the board will be used at about 2 A, made implicitly, recorded nowhere, and invisible to every check.

The IR drop tells the same story: 18.6 mm at 0.508 mm is 36.6 squares, so 17.9 mΩ. At 2 A that is 36 mV on a 1.8 V rail — 2.0%. At 4 A it is 72 mV, or 4.0%, which is outside the supply tolerance of most things a 1.8 V rail feeds. Write the design current on the schematic. It is the input to half the decisions on the board and it is the one thing the board file cannot hold.

3. A four-layer ignition controller — the one that matters

The same board, 49 components, 135.79 × 84.19 mm, routed two ways: once by an autorouter to 21 of 21 nets connected with zero DRC violations, and once with a human's corrections applied afterwards. Both pass DRC. They are not equally good boards, and the difference is a single decision.

What actually happened

The autorouter was asked for connectivity and DRC cleanliness, and it delivered both, completely. It put its ground pour on B.Cu — which is also one of the two routing layers, carrying 89 track segments — and left In1.Cu and In2.Cu completely empty.

Apply lesson 2's rule to that stackup, layer by layer:

  • F.Cu (184 tracks): the nearest copper is In1, which is bare. Its actual reference is B.Cu, three dielectrics and the full board thickness away — and B.Cu is itself carved up by 89 tracks.
  • B.Cu (89 tracks): its pour is on the same layer as its traces, so the pour is a swiss cheese of copper islands rather than a plane.
  • In1, In2: two entire copper layers, paid for and manufactured, carrying nothing.

It is, electrically, a two-layer board with two wasted layers and no reference plane — and it passes every automated check with a perfect score.

What the human did

Moved the ground planes to In1.Cu and In2.Cu and filled them. Now F.Cu references In1 one dielectric away, B.Cu references In2 one dielectric away, and the arrangement is exactly the SIG–GND–GND–SIG stackup of lesson 2. The +50 vias are the stitching those planes need and the layer changes that reference them.

The lesson is not that autorouters are bad. This one solved a genuinely hard problem — 21 of 21 nets, zero DRC, on a 49-part board — and did it faster than a person would. The lesson is that DRC-clean and correctly-referenced are different properties, and only the first is a rule a checker can hold. Return-path continuity is not a clearance, so nothing in the toolchain was ever going to notice that two of the four layers were empty. That judgement is the part of layout that is still yours.

4. The review checklist these three boards produce

Every item below caught something real on one of the three boards above, and every one of them is invisible to DRC.

AskHow to check it in five minutesFound on
Does every signal layer have an adjacent filled plane? Plot each copper layer on its own and look at it. An empty inner layer is obvious the moment you do, and invisible in the 3D view. Ignition controller
What current is this board for? If it is not written on the schematic, nobody knows. Compute the rise at the part's rated output, not at the current you happen to be testing at. Buck converter
Where does the regulator sense its output? Count the squares between the sense point and the load. That drop is outside the loop and the regulator cannot correct it. LDO
What is the longest high-impedance net? Sort the nets by routed length and look at the top of the list for anything that should have been short — FB, a crystal, a reference, a sense line. Buck converter
Does the feedback trace pass the inductor? Compare x/y coordinates of the IC, the divider and the magnetics. If the magnetics are between the other two, the answer is yes. Buck converter
How many vias, and where? A four-layer board with planes and under a hundred vias is under-stitched. Compare the count before and after someone adds the planes. Ignition controller
Is the pour on a routing layer? Count tracks per layer. A "plane" on a layer carrying 89 traces is not a plane. Ignition controller

Notice what these have in common: every one is answered by reading the file or plotting a layer, and none needs a simulator, an expensive tool or a bench. The obstacle to catching them has never been capability. It is that nobody looks, because the tool already said the board was fine.

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse The buck run at the TPS564201's rated 4 A through its 0.508 mm output trace \(\Delta T = (4/0.531)^{1/0.44} = \mathbf{98\,^\circ C}\) above ambient. In a 40 °C enclosure the copper reaches roughly 138 °C, past FR4's typical 130–180 °C glass transition, and the resistance has risen 39% which pushes it further. The trace does not open — copper is a poor fuse — but the laminate discolours and delaminates beneath it, and the 72 mV of drop puts a 1.8 V rail 4% low. Nothing warns you, because current is not a property the board file has. The fix is a wider trace or a documented current limit; either is fine, and the failure is that neither exists.
Abuse The autorouted ignition board taken to EMC pre-compliance F.Cu's 184 traces have no adjacent plane; their return currents spread through whatever copper they can find on B.Cu, between its 89 traces. Loop areas are orders of magnitude larger than the 0.1 mm dielectric a plane would have given, and each is an antenna. Radiated emissions well above the limit, with no single culprit to find, because every net contributes. No amount of ferrites, shielding or rerouting fixes it; the geometry is set by which layers are empty. The DRC report says zero errors throughout. This is the most expensive kind of defect: cheap to prevent, impossible to patch.
Abuse The buck's output loaded with a fast step while its 27.6 mm FB trace runs past L1 The inductor's fringing field couples into the high-impedance feedback node. The loop cannot distinguish injected noise from a genuine output excursion, so it responds — modulating the duty cycle in step with the switching current it is supposed to be regulating. Output ripple with subharmonic content and jitter on the switch edges. Adding output capacitance does not help, because the disturbance is being commanded rather than filtered — which is exactly why this fault survives so many debugging sessions. Shorten FB or move it away from the magnetics.
Abuse The LDO board's 3.3 V used as an ADC reference 41.5 mV of trace drop between the regulator's sense point and the connector, varying with load current. On a 12-bit converter one LSB is 0.81 mV, so the drop is 51 LSB — and it moves as the load changes. Readings that shift with system activity and no fault anywhere. The regulator is doing exactly what it was told: it holds 3.3 V at its own pin, which is where its feedback network is connected. A reference must be Kelvin-sensed at the point of use, or generated at the point of use.

Do this on your own board

Everything above came out of the board files with two commands. No licence, no plugin.

StepCommand
Plot every copper layer separately kicad-cli pcb export svg --mode-single --fit-page-to-board -l "In1.Cu,Edge.Cuts" -o in1.svg board.kicad_pcb

tools/analyse-kicad-pcb.js in this repository is the script that produced every number in this lesson. It parses the s-expression board file directly, so it works on any KiCad version and needs nothing installed. Point it at your own board and read the net-length list — on most boards something surprising is near the top.
