Impedance Control and the Calculators

Impedance control is where the stackup becomes a number you can check. It is also where a great deal of confident, precise, wrong work gets done, because every CAD tool contains a calculator that will happily give you a trace width to four decimal places from inputs it has no way to verify.

This lesson covers the geometry, the rules of thumb worth carrying in your head, what the closed-form equations actually are and where they stop being valid, and how to work with the one party who genuinely knows the answer: the fabricator.

The calculator is a currency converter

A currency converter does exact arithmetic on a rate you gave it. If the rate is stale the answer is wrong to the last decimal place, and the tool has no way of knowing. It will never say “are you sure about that rate?”

An impedance calculator does exact arithmetic on \(\varepsilon_r\), dielectric height, trace width and copper thickness. Every one of those is a number you supplied or a default you accepted, and every one of them differs from what the fabricator will actually build. The arithmetic is not the uncertain part.

Where it breaks: an exchange rate is a single published number. \(\varepsilon_r\) is not — it varies with frequency, with glass-to-resin ratio, and with direction through the laminate. There is no single correct value to look up, which is why the fabricator's measured data beats any datasheet figure.

The trap that catches every high-speed designer once. Microstrip and stripline propagate at different speeds. If one member of a length-matched pair, or one bit of a parallel bus, spends part of its journey on an outer layer and the rest on an inner layer, matching the physical lengths does not match the electrical lengths. Over 100 mm the difference is \(100 \times (6.9-5.8) = 110\) ps — which is more than the entire timing budget of a DDR interface. Match delay, not millimetres, and keep matched groups on the same layer type.

3. The closed-form equations, and their limits

The Wheeler/Hammerstad microstrip approximations are what almost every calculator implements. They are worth seeing, mostly so you understand what they assume.

\[ \varepsilon_{r,\text{eff}} = \frac{\varepsilon_r+1}{2} + \frac{\varepsilon_r-1}{2} \left(1+\frac{12h}{w}\right)^{-1/2} \] The effective permittivity the wave sees, between air above and laminate below. Note it depends on \(w/h\): a wider trace pushes more field into the laminate, so it is slower as well as lower impedance.
\[ Z_0 = \frac{60}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,\text{eff}}}} \ln\!\left(\frac{8h}{w}+\frac{w}{4h}\right) \qquad (w/h \le 1) \]
\[ Z_0 = \frac{120\pi}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{r,\text{eff}}}\; \left[\frac{w}{h}+1.393+0.667\ln\!\left(\frac{w}{h}+1.444\right)\right]} \qquad (w/h \ge 1) \] Two regimes, two formulas, and they agree only approximately at the boundary — which is itself a hint about how these were produced: curve fits to numerical solutions, not derivations.

Where they stop being valid

AssumptionRealityEffect
The trace is a rectangleEtching produces a trapezoid, typically 1–2 mil narrower at the topImpedance a few Ω higher than calculated. Fabricators compensate by etching wider; your calculator does not know they did.

A 2D field solver — which is what a fabricator and a serious CAD tool use — handles the trapezoid, the soldermask, the plating and the actual laminate data. That is the difference between a formula and a solver, and it is the reason the fabricator's number wins every time.

4. Differential pairs

A differential pair has two impedances, and confusing them is the second most common impedance mistake after ignoring the stackup.

\[ Z_{diff} = 2 Z_{odd} \] \(Z_{odd}\) is what one conductor presents when the pair is driven differentially. \(Z_{diff}\) is what the pair presents between the two conductors, and it is what a spec like “100 Ω differential” means. So a 100 Ω pair is two 50 Ω odd-mode lines.
\[ Z_{odd} < Z_0 \ \text{as the coupling increases} \] Bringing the traces closer raises the mutual capacitance between them, which lowers the odd-mode impedance. So you cannot design a 100 Ω pair as two independent 50 Ω traces and then move them together — the act of coupling them changes the answer.
DecisionTight coupling (s < 2h)Loose coupling (s > 3h)
Immunity to plane discontinuitiesGood — each trace is largely the other's returnPoorer — each relies on the plane
Susceptibility to spacing errorHigh — impedance is sensitive to \(s\)Low — behaves like two independent lines
Routing densityBetterWorse
Skew from length mismatchMore harmful — the pair relies on balanceLess harmful
Common inUSB, HDMI, PCIe on dense boardsBackplanes, long runs, where manufacturing variance dominates

There is no universally correct answer here, which is why practice differs between organisations. The one thing that is always correct: pick a coupling, tell the fabricator the differential target, and let them calculate both the width and the spacing together. Specifying a width from a single-ended calculator and then choosing a spacing by eye produces a pair with neither the impedance you wanted nor the one you think you have.

5. Working with the fabricator

StepWhat to send / ask
1. Before routingAsk for their standard build-up for your layer count, thickness and copper weight. Do not invent a stackup.
2. State the requirement“Layer 1 and layer 6: 50 Ω single-ended ±10%. Layers 1, 3, 6: 90 Ω differential ±10%.” On the fab drawing, not in an email.
3. Let them size itThey return trace widths and pair spacings from their own field solver, with their etch compensation and measured \(\varepsilon_r\) built in.
4. Route to their numbersNot to your calculator's. If the two differ by more than a few percent, ask why — it is usually etch compensation or soldermask, and the answer is educational.
5. Require a couponAn impedance test coupon on the panel, TDR tested, results shipped with the boards.
6. Read the coupon reportIt is the only evidence the board you received matches the board you designed. A surprising number of people never open it.
What tolerance to ask for. ±10% is standard and usually free. ±7% costs a little. ±5% costs real money and may restrict which fabricators can quote. Ask what the interface actually needs: USB 2.0 is forgiving, DDR4 is not, and a 10 Gb/s SerDes channel needs the tolerance and a named low-loss laminate. Do not specify ±5% reflexively; it is a cost with no benefit on most boards.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse A 50 Ω design calculated against an assumed 0.2 mm dielectric, built on 0.36 mm Impedance rises roughly with \(h\) for a fixed width: about 50 Ω becomes about 75 Ω. Against 50 Ω terminations at each end, \(|\Gamma| = 0.2\) at both transitions. Nothing burns; this one degrades quietly, which makes it worse. Reflections close the eye, marginal interfaces fail intermittently and at temperature, and the board is blamed for a “flaky” peripheral. The defence is procedural: a controlled-impedance note and a coupon catch it before assembly. Once the boards exist, no termination change recovers it.
Abuse A 5 Gb/s pair routed over a fibre-weave bundle, with one trace over glass and one over resin for a long run The two conductors see different \(\varepsilon_r\), so they propagate at different speeds — several ps per inch of skew, accumulating along the run. The differential signal acquires a common-mode component. Below about 5 Gb/s, usually tolerable. Above it, the skew closes the eye and the common-mode conversion radiates. Mitigations are geometric, not electrical: route pairs at 10–20° to the weave so both traces average over glass and resin, or specify a spread-weave (mechanically flattened) laminate. Length matching cannot fix it because the lengths are already equal.
Abuse A DDR byte lane length-matched in millimetres, with four bits on layer 1 and four on layer 3 The stripline bits travel at 6.9 ps/mm and the microstrip bits at 5.8 ps/mm. Over a 60 mm run that is 66 ps of skew between the halves of the lane — with every trace exactly the same physical length. The CAD tool reports perfect length matching and the interface fails timing. This is one of the most confusing faults in high-speed design because every check passes. Match delay rather than length, or keep each matched group entirely on one layer type. Modern tools can do delay matching; the default is often length.
Abuse A 28 GHz design on standard FR4, sized from a calculator using \(\varepsilon_r = 4.3\) At 28 GHz the real \(\varepsilon_r\) is lower and the loss tangent is around 0.02, so the dielectric loss alone is severe; the skin depth is 0.4 µm against 1–6 µm of deliberate foil roughness, so conductor loss is far above the smooth-copper prediction. The line is not 50 Ω and the loss is several times what was budgeted. Above about 5–10 GHz, “FR4” ceases to be a material specification — it is a flammability rating covering laminates whose loss tangent varies threefold. Name the laminate, use its measured data, and use a field solver rather than a formula.
