Stackup: The Decision That Constrains Everything

The stackup is chosen in the first hour of a project, usually by accepting whatever the CAD tool defaults to, and it then constrains every routing decision for the rest of the design. Rick Hartley calls it the single most important item in preventing interference, and the one people most often get wrong. It is also almost free to get right: a good four-layer stackup and a bad one cost the same to fabricate.

Hartley's rule, in one line: every signal layer and every routed power trace must have a solid ground reference plane one dielectric away. Not two dielectrics. Not a power plane. A ground plane, immediately adjacent.

Everything below is a consequence of that rule and of the return-path physics from the previous lesson. If a signal's return has to be directly underneath it, then a plane has to be directly underneath it — and the stackup is precisely the document that decides whether one is.

The stackup is the foundation

Routing is the visible work; the stackup is the foundation under the building. Nobody admires it, it is decided before anything else, and once it is poured you are committed. A building on a bad foundation can be reinforced afterwards, expensively and imperfectly — which is exactly what adding ferrites, shields and stitching capacitors to a badly stacked board amounts to.

Where it breaks: a foundation only has to hold weight, so more is always better. A stackup has competing demands — layer count against cost, dielectric thickness against impedance, plane count against routing channels — so “more” is not automatically better and the right answer depends on the board.

1. Why two layers is a different craft

A two-layer board has no plane to reference to, so none of the rules in this module apply to it in their usual form. That does not make two-layer boards bad — it makes them a different discipline, where the job is to construct a return path deliberately rather than to avoid breaking one.

  • Pour ground on the bottom layer and treat it as a plane, keeping it as unbroken as the routing allows.
  • Where a bottom-layer trace must cross the pour, keep it short and stitch the pour around it, so the return detour is bounded.
  • Accept that impedance control is approximate: with a 1.6 mm board, a 50 Ω microstrip needs a trace roughly 3 mm wide, which is usually impractical. Two-layer boards above a few tens of megahertz are a compromise, not a design.

The economic case for four layers has almost disappeared. At prototype quantities the difference is a few dollars per board, and at volume it is a fraction of a cent per square centimetre. If a design has any signal faster than about 10 MHz, or any switching regulator, four layers is the cheaper choice once you count the engineering time that a two-layer EMC problem consumes.

2. Four layers: the argument that actually matters

Two stackups dominate, and the difference between them is the whole Hartley argument.

LayerThe common defaultWhat Hartley recommends
1 (top)SignalSignal
2GNDGND
3PWRGND
4 (bottom)SignalSignal + routed power

Why the default disappoints

In SIG–GND–PWR–SIG, layer 1 is fine: it has a ground plane one dielectric away. Layer 4 references the power plane. That is not automatically fatal — a power plane is an AC ground if it is well decoupled — but it is conditional on decoupling that gets worse with frequency, and it means the return for a layer-4 signal is on the power net. Any signal that changes from layer 1 to layer 4 changes reference from ground to power, and its return now has to get from the ground plane to the power plane, which it can only do through a decoupling capacitor. That capacitor has inductance, so the transition is a real discontinuity.

Why the alternative works

In SIG–GND–GND–SIG both signal layers reference solid ground, both are one thin dielectric from it, and a via changing from layer 1 to layer 4 changes from one ground plane to another — which a single ground via handles perfectly. Power is routed as fat traces or local pours on the outer layers, which is entirely adequate for the currents most boards carry and lets you place the copper where the current actually goes.

The objection, and the answer. The standard objection is that you lose the power plane's “plane capacitance”. For a normal 4-layer stackup that capacitance is worth much less than its reputation — see §4 — and the two ground planes buy you something that is not conditional on frequency. The cases where a solid power plane genuinely earns its layer are high-current digital (an FPGA or SoC drawing tens of amps) and designs with many supply rails, and those boards are not four-layer boards anyway.

3. Six and eight layers

StackupAssessment
SIG–GND–SIG–SIG–GND–SIG Good. Four routing layers, every one adjacent to ground. Layers 3 and 4 are a stripline pair between the planes, so they are shielded from the outside world entirely. Route power as traces on the outer layers. This is the workhorse six-layer stackup.
SIG–GND–SIG–PWR–GND–SIG Good, when you need a power plane. The PWR–GND pair on 4–5 is tightly coupled if you make that dielectric thin. Layer 3 references GND on layer 2. The compromise is that layer 3 has PWR on the other side, so its field sees a plane that is only an AC ground.
SIG–SIG–GND–PWR–SIG–SIG Avoid. Layers 1 and 6 have no adjacent plane at all — their nearest reference is two dielectrics away. This is the classic “symmetrical looks tidy” mistake, and it puts your two most accessible, most-routed layers in the worst possible position for radiation.
SIG–GND–SIG–GND–PWR–SIG–GND–SIG Good eight-layer. Every signal layer adjacent to ground, a dedicated power plane tight against a ground plane, and four routing layers. Standard for dense digital.

The rules that decide it

  1. Every signal layer adjacent to a ground plane. Check each one individually; do not assume symmetry gives it to you.
  2. Pair power and ground planes tightly if you have a power plane at all — 0.075 mm or less between them, not 0.5 mm.
  3. Keep signal-to-reference dielectrics thin, around 0.1 mm. Thin bindings mean lower loop inductance, less crosstalk and narrower traces for the same impedance.
  4. Put the thick dielectric in the middle, where it does no harm. The board must reach its total thickness somewhere; make it the core between two ground planes.
  5. Keep it symmetrical about the centre for mechanical reasons — an asymmetric copper distribution warps during reflow.

4. Plane capacitance: how much is it really worth?

The claim is that a power/ground plane pair forms a capacitor that supplies high-frequency current where discrete capacitors cannot. The capacitance is real. Whether it is useful depends on a number people rarely compute.

\[ C = \varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\frac{A}{d} \] For a 100 × 100 mm plane pair on FR4 (\(\varepsilon_r = 4.3\)) separated by 0.1 mm: \(8.854\times10^{-12}\times 4.3\times 0.01/0.0001 = 3.8\ \mathrm{nF}\).

3.8 nF. That is less than one 0402 ceramic capacitor. Two observations follow:

  • As bulk charge storage it is negligible. A single 100 nF decoupling capacitor holds 26 times more. Nobody should choose a stackup for the capacitance value.
  • As a very low-inductance capacitor it is genuinely special. Its ESL is essentially zero because it has no leads and no vias — the charge is already between the planes, where the die needs it. That is why it matters at frequencies where every discrete capacitor has become an inductor.
\[ f_{SRF} = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{L_{loop}C}} \] A 100 nF 0402 with 0.6 nH of package ESL and perhaps 1.2 nH of via and trace loop inductance resonates at \(1/(2\pi\sqrt{1.8\,\mathrm{nH}\times100\,\mathrm{nF}}) = 11.9\ \mathrm{MHz}\). Above that it is an inductor, and its impedance rises with frequency.

This is the honest picture of decoupling, and it reorders the usual priorities. Above roughly 100 MHz almost no discrete capacitor is helping, because the loop inductance of getting to it dominates. What actually supplies current up there is the on-die capacitance and the package, with the plane pair third. The practical consequence is that the loop inductance of your decoupling matters far more than the capacitance value, which is why capacitor placement and via arrangement earn more than adding a fourth value to the BOM.

So should you keep the power plane? Keep it when the board needs a plane's current-carrying ability — a processor drawing 20 A cannot be fed by traces — or when there are enough rails that routing them all as traces is impractical. Do not keep it for the capacitance. And if you do keep it, make the PWR–GND dielectric as thin as the fabricator offers, because that is the one variable that improves both the capacitance and, more importantly, the plane-pair's impedance.

5. Talking to your fabricator

The most common stackup mistake is not choosing badly — it is designing to a stackup the fabricator does not build. Trace widths calculated against an assumed 0.1 mm prepreg are simply wrong if the board is made with 0.2 mm.

Do thisBecause
Ask for the fabricator's standard stackup for your layer count and thickness before routing They build from a menu of core and prepreg thicknesses. A stackup off that menu costs money and lead time, and often gets substituted anyway.
Let them calculate the trace width for your target impedance They know their laminate's actual \(\varepsilon_r\) at your frequency, their etch compensation, and their plating thickness. Your calculator knows none of those.
Put a controlled-impedance note on the fab drawing with target, tolerance and which layers Without it, impedance is not a requirement and nothing obliges them to hit it. ±10% is standard; ±5% costs more.
Ask for an impedance coupon on the panel It is TDR-tested and shipped with the boards. It is the only evidence you have that the stackup you designed is the stackup you received.
Specify the laminate if it matters “FR4” is a flammability rating, not a material. Its \(\varepsilon_r\) ranges roughly 4.2–4.7 and its loss tangent varies by a factor of three across materials all called FR4. Above about 1 GHz, name the laminate.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse The CAD tool's default 1.6 mm 4-layer stackup accepted unexamined, which typically puts 0.71 mm between layers 1–2 and 3–4 with a thin core in the middle Signal layers are 0.71 mm from their reference instead of 0.1 mm. Loop inductance and radiated field are seven times larger; crosstalk between adjacent traces rises by a similar factor; and a 50 Ω microstrip now needs a 1.3 mm trace, which will not fit under a fine-pitch part. Nothing is damaged and the board works on the bench. It fails radiated emissions, and no amount of routing skill recovers it because the geometry is set by the stackup. This is the single most common serious PCB mistake, and it is made by accepting a default. The correct stackup is thin prepreg outside, thick core in the middle, and it costs the same.
Abuse A 6-layer board stacked SIG–SIG–GND–PWR–SIG–SIG Layers 1, 2, 5 and 6 all lack an adjacent plane. Layer 1's return has to reach layer 3, two dielectrics away, so its field fills the volume containing layer 2 — and layer 2 is a signal layer, so they couple directly. Crosstalk between layers 1 and 2 will be severe and effectively unfixable by spacing, because they are broadside coupled with no plane between them. Two adjacent signal layers with no plane between them should always be routed orthogonally at minimum, and preferably should not exist.
Abuse The fabricator substitutes a different prepreg because yours was out of stock, and does not flag it Dielectric goes from 0.1 to 0.18 mm. Every 50 Ω trace is now about 68 Ω. Every controlled-impedance interface sees a mismatch at both ends; a 68 Ω line into a 50 Ω termination reflects \(|\Gamma| = 0.15\). Marginal interfaces start failing intermittently and at temperature. The defence is procedural, not electrical: a controlled-impedance note makes the substitution their problem rather than yours, and the coupon detects it before the boards are populated.
Abuse Power plane relied on for decoupling above 200 MHz on a stackup with 0.5 mm between PWR and GND The plane pair capacitance is \(\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A/d\) with a five-times-larger \(d\), so a fifth of the value; worse, the plane-pair impedance rises with the separation. Meanwhile every discrete capacitor passed its self-resonance a decade earlier. There is essentially nothing supplying current in that band except the die and its package. Rail collapse during simultaneous switching, seen as jitter and occasional logic failure that nobody can reproduce. The fix is a thin PWR–GND dielectric and low-inductance decoupling placement — not more capacitors of more values.
