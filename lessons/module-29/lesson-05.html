Trace Width, Current and Temperature Rise

“How wide should this trace be?” is the question every online PCB calculator answers, and almost all of them answer it with a curve fit from 1954 that its own standards body has since replaced. This lesson gives you the equation, the reason it was superseded, and the things that actually decide the answer on a real board — which are usually not the ones the calculator asks about.

There are two separate questions and they have different answers. How hot does the trace get? is a thermal question, answered by width, copper weight and what the copper is attached to. How much voltage do I lose? is a resistance question, answered by width, copper weight and length. A trace can be perfectly cool and still ruin your regulation, and on low-voltage rails that is the far more common failure.
The trace is a radiator, not a fuse

People picture a trace as something that survives a current or melts. Almost always the real question is different: the trace is a long thin radiator, generating heat along its whole length and dumping it into the board. How hot it gets depends far more on what it is bolted to than on the copper itself — a trace over a solid plane with vias into it runs far cooler than the same trace on a bare, isolated island.

Where it breaks: a radiator carries no useful signal. A trace's resistance also drops voltage, and on a 1.0 V core rail a 20 mV drop is a 2% error before regulation has done anything. Thermal and electrical consequences must be checked separately; passing one says nothing about the other.

1. The IPC-2221 equation, and its history

\[ A = \left(\frac{I}{k\,\Delta T^{\,0.44}}\right)^{1/0.725} \] \(A\) in square mils, \(I\) in amperes, \(\Delta T\) in °C. \(k = 0.048\) for external layers, \(k = 0.024\) for internal.
\[ w_{\text{mils}} = \frac{A}{1.378 \times (\text{oz})} \] 1 oz/ft² copper is 1.378 mils (35 µm) thick. So a 2 oz trace needs half the width of a 1 oz trace for the same cross-section.

The rule of thumb worth carrying: about 12 mils (0.3 mm) per amp for 1 oz external copper at a 10 °C rise, and it is roughly linear only over a small range — note that 3 A needs 4.5× the width of 1 A, not 3×, because the exponent is 1/0.725 = 1.38, not 1.

Why IPC-2152 replaced it

The IPC-2221 curves descend from a 1954 study on bare copper traces suspended in still air, with no board, no planes and no neighbours. That is not a PCB. IPC-2152 (2009) redid the work experimentally, and found the old curves are wrong in both directions depending on the situation:

  • Conservative for a trace on a board with internal planes, because the planes are a heatsink the old model does not know about. IPC-2152 often permits a trace half the IPC-2221 width.
  • Non-conservative for a trace on a thin board with no planes, in still air, in an enclosure — where the old curves can under-size.
  • The internal/external distinction is backwards for many real boards. IPC-2221 penalises internal layers heavily; in practice an internal trace is sandwiched in laminate that conducts heat away in both directions and is often cooler than the same trace on the surface.
What to do about it. IPC-2152 is chart-based, not a formula, so it does not fit in an online calculator — which is precisely why nearly every calculator still implements IPC-2221. Use IPC-2221 as a starting estimate, know it is approximate, and for anything where the answer matters (a power path, a dense supply, a sealed enclosure) either consult IPC-2152's charts or measure a real board with a thermal camera. And treat any tool that reports a width to three decimal places as reporting false precision.

2. Resistance and IR drop — usually the real constraint

\[ R_\square = \frac{\rho}{t} = \frac{1.72\times10^{-8}}{35\times10^{-6}} = \mathbf{0.49\ m\Omega/\square} \ \ (1\ \mathrm{oz}) \] Sheet resistance. Copper's resistivity divided by its thickness. Half a milliohm per square is the number to memorise, and it makes trace resistance almost trivial to estimate.
\[ R = R_\square \times \frac{\ell}{w} \] Count the squares. A trace 0.3 mm wide and 30 mm long is 100 squares, so 49 mΩ. No calculator needed.
\[ R(T) = R_{20}\left[1 + 0.00393(T-20)\right] \] Copper gains 0.393% per °C. A trace running 40 °C above ambient in a 60 °C enclosure is at 100 °C and is 31% more resistive than the datasheet value — which raises its dissipation, which raises its temperature. Check that the loop converges.

The low-voltage rail problem. A 1.0 V core rail at 5 A, delivered over a trace with 20 mΩ of resistance, drops 100 mV — 10% of the rail — and dissipates 0.5 W. The trace is only mildly warm and passes every thermal check, and the processor browns out. This is why low-voltage high-current rails are routed as planes or wide pours with remote sensing, and why the thermal question is the less interesting of the two on modern digital boards.

3. Vias: current, resistance and inductance

A via is a thin copper tube. Its cross-section is the barrel wall, not the hole.

\[ A_{barrel} = \pi d \cdot t_{plate} \] For a 0.3 mm drill with 25 µm of plating: \(\pi \times 0.3 \times 0.025 = 0.0236\ \mathrm{mm^2}\). For comparison, a 1 oz trace 0.68 mm wide has the same cross-section — so one 0.3 mm via is worth about 0.68 mm of 1 oz trace, which is a handy way to decide how many you need.
\[ R = \frac{\rho \ell}{A} = \frac{1.72\times10^{-8}\times 1.6\times10^{-3}} {2.36\times10^{-8}} = \mathbf{1.17\ m\Omega} \] Through a 1.6 mm board. Small, but ten vias in a power path in parallel give 0.12 mΩ while ten in series along a current path give 11.7 mΩ — and stacked micro-vias in a build-up board are in series.
\[ L \approx 5.08\,h\left[\ln\!\frac{4h}{d} + 1\right]\ \mathrm{nH} \quad (h,d\ \text{in inches}) \] For \(h = 1.6\) mm (0.063″) and \(d = 0.3\) mm (0.012″): \(5.08\times0.063\times[\ln 21 + 1] = \mathbf{1.29\ nH}\). This is the number that matters for decoupling — at 500 MHz that via is \(j4.0\ \Omega\), which is why the via arrangement dominates the capacitor value.

4. Worked example: a 5 V, 3 A supply feeding a load 80 mm away

\[ w = \frac{1}{1.378}\left(\frac{3}{0.048\times10^{0.44}} \right)^{1/0.725} = 53.8\ \mathrm{mil} = 1.37\ \mathrm{mm} \] IPC-2221, external, 1 oz, 10 °C rise. This is the thermal answer.
\[ R = 0.49\,\mathrm{m\Omega}\times\frac{80}{1.37} = 0.49 \times 58.4 = 28.6\ \mathrm{m\Omega} \] 58 squares. Now the electrical answer.
\[ V_{drop} = 3 \times 0.0286 = 86\ \mathrm{mV} \ (1.7\%\ \text{of }5\,\mathrm{V}), \qquad P = 3^2 \times 0.0286 = 0.26\ \mathrm{W} \] Acceptable on a 5 V rail. Note the 0.26 W is spread over 80 mm of copper, which is why the temperature rise is only 10 °C.
\[ \text{Same trace on a 1.0 V rail: } 86\,\mathrm{mV} = \mathbf{8.6\%} \] Identical copper, identical temperature, completely unacceptable. The rail voltage changes the answer even though nothing physical changed — which is the whole point of §2.

And the return path is the half people forget: the current comes back. If it returns through a plane the resistance is negligible; if it returns through another 1.37 mm trace the drop is 172 mV, not 86.

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse A short circuit on a 5 V, 3 A rail whose supply current-limits at 6 A, feeding through the 1.37 mm trace above \(P = 6^2 \times 0.0286 = 1.03\ \mathrm{W}\) in the trace, four times the design figure. Temperature rise scales roughly as \(I^{1.7}\), so a 10 °C rise becomes about 32 °C — warm, not destructive. The trace survives, and that is the correct outcome: the current limit is doing its job and the copper is not the protection. Note the asymmetry — a trace sized for 3 A does not fail at 6 A, it fails somewhere above 15 A. Copper is a poor fuse because its fusing current is far above its comfortable rating and has enormous variance.
Abuse A hard short with no current limit, supply capable of 40 A \(P = 40^2\times0.0286 = 46\ \mathrm{W}\) in 80 mm of copper. Onderdonk's equation puts the 1-second fusing current of this trace at roughly 60 A, so at 40 A it does not open — it sits there glowing, delaminating the laminate and charring the epoxy. The worst outcome of the three: it neither survives nor cleanly fails. A trace that carbonises leaves a conductive char that can sustain an arc. This is exactly why a fuse or an electronic current limit is mandatory upstream of any energy source that can deliver more than the copper can shed. Never let a PCB trace be the current-limiting element.
Abuse The board is used at 85 °C ambient having been designed for 25 °C Copper resistance rises 0.393%/°C, so at 85 °C plus a 10 °C rise the trace is at 95 °C and is 29% more resistive. IR drop rises 29% and dissipation with it, pushing the temperature higher still. The loop converges — but at a higher temperature and a larger drop than designed. On a tight low-voltage rail this alone can take a marginal design out of regulation at temperature, which presents as a product that works on the bench and fails in the field in summer. Compute IR drop at maximum operating temperature, not at 20 °C.
Abuse An IPC-2221 calculator used for a trace on a bare, plane-less section of a thin board in a sealed enclosure The 1954 data assumed a bare conductor in free air with convection on all sides. Here there is no plane to conduct into and no airflow to convect to, so the real rise substantially exceeds the prediction. The calculator is non-conservative in exactly the case that looks most innocuous. IPC-2152 exists because of this. For any board without planes under the power traces, or any sealed enclosure, size from IPC-2152's charts or measure — do not trust a formula that does not ask about the board.

Sources

  • IPC-2221B — Generic Standard on Printed Board Design. The trace-width chart the A = (I/(k·ΔT0.44))1/0.725 fit comes from, with k = 0.048 external and 0.024 internal. The curves date from 1950s measurements on bare boards in still air.
  • IPC-2152 — Standard for Determining Current Carrying Capacity in Printed Board Design. The 2009 replacement, based on new measurements that separated copper thickness, board thickness, plane presence and environment. It generally allows narrower traces than IPC-2221 for the same rise — the older chart is conservative, not wrong, and a great deal of tooling still uses it.
