Return Current: The Idea Everything Else Rests On

Almost every PCB layout rule you have ever been handed — keep ground planes solid, don't cross splits, route differential pairs together, add a via next to every signal via — is a consequence of one fact that is rarely stated plainly:

Current flows in loops. Every signal that leaves a driver must come back. The return current is exactly as large as the signal current, it is just as real, and unlike the signal you did not draw it on the schematic — so the board decides where it goes. Almost all EMI and signal-integrity trouble is the return path taking a route you did not intend.

Get this lesson and the rest of the module is mostly bookkeeping. Skip it and the rules become arbitrary things to memorise, which is exactly how most people learn layout and exactly why most boards have avoidable problems in them.

Water does not vanish at the tap

Nobody plumbs a building with supply pipes only. Water arrives, is used, and leaves through a drain, and the drain is designed with as much care as the supply.

A schematic draws the supply pipes and labels the drain “GND”, as though naming it were the same as routing it. The copper still has to carry that return, and if you have not decided where, it will find its own way — possibly through a neighbouring signal's reference, possibly around the edge of a split, possibly out along a cable and into someone's radio.

Where it breaks: water picks the lowest path and does not care about anything else. Return current picks the path of least impedance, and which term dominates — resistance or inductance — depends on frequency. That frequency dependence is the whole subtlety, and the plumbing has no equivalent of it.

1. The energy is not in the copper

Rick Hartley makes this point harder than anyone, and it is worth stating in his terms: the signal is not the current in the trace, it is the electromagnetic field in the dielectric between the trace and its return path. The copper does not carry the energy; it guides it. The energy travels in the space between the conductors.

Two things follow immediately, and they are the reason the stackup matters more than the routing:

  • The field needs somewhere to be. If there is a solid plane one thin dielectric away, the field is confined to a small volume directly beneath the trace, tightly bound, coupling to nothing. If the nearest plane is three dielectrics away, that same field spreads across a much larger volume — through your neighbours' traces, and out of the board.
  • Radiation is loop area. A signal and its return enclose a loop. That loop is an antenna, and its radiated field is proportional to its area. Keeping the return directly under the signal makes the loop as thin as the dielectric — a few thousandths of an inch — and thereby a hopeless antenna. That is the goal.
\[ L_{loop} \approx \mu_0 \frac{h \cdot \ell}{w} \] Loop inductance of a trace of width \(w\) and length \(\ell\) over a plane at height \(h\). Note what it depends on: the height above the plane, not the thickness of the copper and not the width of the plane. Halve \(h\) and you halve the loop inductance and the radiated field with it.
\[ Z_0 = \sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}} \quad\text{and}\quad t_{pd} = \sqrt{L'C'} \] The same \(L'\) that sets the loop inductance sets the characteristic impedance. Impedance control and EMI control are not two jobs — they are one geometry, seen twice.

2. Least resistance, or least inductance?

This is the single most useful piece of physics in PCB layout, and it has a clean answer.

Consider a trace running from A to B over a ground plane, with the return coming back through the plane. The return current could take the short, wide, low-resistance route straight across the plane, or it could hug the trace. Which it does depends on which term dominates the plane's impedance:

\[ Z = R + j\omega L \] At DC, \(\omega L = 0\) and the current spreads out to minimise \(R\) — it takes the widest path available. As \(\omega\) rises, \(\omega L\) grows without limit while \(R\) stays put, so beyond some frequency the inductive term completely dominates.
\[ f_{cross} = \frac{R}{2\pi L} \] For a copper plane this lands in the low kilohertz. Below about 1 kHz the return spreads; by roughly 100 kHz it is essentially all directly beneath the trace, in a bundle only a few trace-widths wide.
Every digital and RF signal on your board is above the crossover. Even a 1 kHz square wave has edges with megahertz of content, and it is the edge that carries the current the return path has to deal with. So for practical purposes: the return current is directly under the trace, always, and any layout rule can be checked by asking “can it get there?”

That single question answers almost everything:

The rule you were toldWhat it actually means
Never route across a split in the ground plane The return cannot cross the gap. It must detour to wherever the two sides are joined, enclosing a loop that can be tens of square centimetres instead of fractions of one. That loop radiates, and it is in series with your signal as extra inductance.
Put a ground via next to every signal via When the signal changes layers it changes reference planes. The return has to change planes too, and it can only do that through a via or a capacitor. Without one it detours to the nearest one it can find.
Keep the dielectric to the plane thin Directly shrinks the loop area, which lowers inductance, crosstalk and radiation together.
Don't route over a plane's edge or a void Same as a split. The return has to go round.
Route a differential pair tightly coupled Each conductor is the other's return, so the loop is the gap between them. This is why a good pair is tolerant of plane discontinuities that would wreck a single-ended signal.

3. The current distribution, quantitatively

Above the crossover frequency the return current density in the plane beneath a microstrip follows a Lorentzian in the lateral distance \(x\):

\[ J(x) = \frac{I}{\pi h}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + (x/h)^2} \] \(h\) is the height of the trace above the plane. This is a remarkable result: the only length scale is \(h\). The width of the plane does not appear, and neither does the width of the trace.
\[ \int_{-3h}^{3h} J\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi}\tan^{-1}(3) = 0.795 \] Roughly 80% of the return current is within \(\pm 3h\) of the trace centreline. For a 0.1 mm dielectric that is a 0.6 mm wide band — and 97% is within \(\pm 20h\), i.e. 4 mm.

This is worth internalising because it makes the “keep the plane solid” rule quantitative rather than superstitious. A void 5 mm to the side of a trace on a 0.1 mm dielectric is 50\(h\) away and takes essentially nothing from the return. A void 0.2 mm to the side takes a real fraction of it. And a void directly under the trace takes all of it, which is why crossing a split is a different category of mistake from routing near one.

4. Worked example: what a split plane costs

A 50 mm trace carrying a signal with a 1 ns edge, on a 0.1 mm dielectric over a solid plane, versus the same trace crossing a split that forces the return 40 mm out of its way.

First, a distinction that matters and is often blurred. A trace over a solid plane does have inductance — about 0.33 nH/mm for 50 Ω microstrip, so 16 nH over 50 mm. But that inductance is distributed and paired with the line's capacitance; together they are the characteristic impedance, and the signal simply propagates. It does not appear as a lump of \(L\,di/dt\) between two ground points.

What causes ground bounce is the excess loop — the area the return encloses beyond running directly under the trace. On a solid plane that excess is essentially zero. A split creates it out of nothing.

\[ \text{Solid: } A_{excess} \approx 0 \] The return is directly beneath, within a few times the dielectric thickness. There is no uncompensated loop, so no bounce between one part of the plane and another.
\[ \text{Split: } A_{excess} \approx 40\,\mathrm{mm} \times 20\,\mathrm{mm} = 800\ \mathrm{mm^2} = 8\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{m^2} \] The return must run along the split to the bridge and back, enclosing a large flat loop in the plane of the board. Nothing compensates this one.
\[ L_{excess} \approx \mu_0\frac{A}{w} = \frac{4\pi\times10^{-7}\times 8\times10^{-4}}{0.020} = \mathbf{50\ nH} \] Taking the detour's effective width as about 20 mm. Order-of-magnitude, but the order of magnitude is the point.
\[ V = L\frac{di}{dt} = 50\,\mathrm{nH}\times \frac{20\,\mathrm{mA}}{1\,\mathrm{ns}} = \mathbf{1.0\ V} \] One volt of ground bounce, from one signal, on a 3.3 V board — and it is not noise on that net, it is a voltage difference between two parts of what the schematic calls “ground”, seen by everything referenced to either side of it.
And the radiation. An 800 mm² loop carrying harmonic content at 300 MHz is an efficient small loop antenna. In practice a single trace crossing a split plane is one of the most reliable ways to fail a radiated-emissions test, and it is nearly invisible on a schematic review because the schematic says both sides are “GND”.

5. The split-plane argument, settled

Splitting the ground plane to “isolate analogue from digital” is the most persistent piece of bad advice in electronics. It appears in application notes, it is taught in courses, and it is wrong for almost every board built since about 1990.

The claimWhat actually happens
“Splitting stops digital noise reaching the analogue section” It stops DC and low-frequency currents mixing, which was the real problem in the 1970s when the noise was 60 Hz hum and ground resistance was what coupled it. It does nothing about high-frequency coupling, which goes through the fields, not the copper.
“It gives each section a quiet reference” Until one signal crosses the split — and on a real board something always does, often the very ADC data bus you split the plane to protect. That signal's return now runs the length of both sections.
“The datasheet says to” Many do, and many of those figures date from an era of much slower edges. Check the date and the edge rates. Some vendors have quietly revised them.

What to do instead: keep one solid ground plane, and control the currents by placement. Put the analogue parts in one area and the digital parts in another, so that the return currents of each naturally stay under their own traces and never overlap. You get the isolation you wanted without ever creating a discontinuity for a return path to fall into. This is Hartley's position, Bogatin's position, and Ott's position, and they agree for the same reason.

The exception that proves it. There is one case where a genuine plane split is right: a galvanic isolation barrier, where the two sides are at different potentials and safety requires them not to be connected at all. There, nothing crosses the split except through an isolator, so no return path is ever broken. The rule is not “never split” — it is never split a plane that a signal has to cross.

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse The ground plane split for “analogue isolation”, with the SPI bus to the ADC crossing it Every SPI edge's return runs to the bridge and back — say 60 mm each way. Loop inductance rises from a few nH to perhaps 60 nH, and with a 10 mA/ns edge that is 600 mV of bounce injected between analogue and digital ground. The split makes the noise it was drawn to prevent, and makes it worse than no split at all. Nothing is damaged; the ADC simply loses two to three bits of effective resolution and nobody can find out why. Remove the split. This failure is so common that finding a split plane should be the first thing you look for on any board with unexplained converter noise.
Abuse A high-speed trace routed over a plane void left by a connector's mounting hole array Over the void the trace has no reference. Its impedance rises sharply — perhaps 50 Ω to 90 Ω over that stretch — creating a reflection at each end of the void, and its return spreads around the obstruction. At slow edges, nothing observable. As edge rates rise it becomes an impedance discontinuity that shows up as ringing and eye closure. The fix is placement: route around the void, or move the connector. Retuning terminations to compensate is treating a symptom.
Abuse A signal via with no accompanying ground via, on a 6-layer board, changing from layer 1 (ref: layer 2 GND) to layer 6 (ref: layer 5 GND) The return must get from the layer 2 plane to the layer 5 plane. With no via nearby it travels through whatever couples the two planes — the nearest stitching via, or the plane-pair capacitance, which at 500 MHz is a poor conductor over any distance. The added inductance appears in series with the signal and as a common-mode current on both planes. One 0.3 mm ground via, 0.5 mm away, removes it almost entirely. It is the cheapest fix in all of PCB design and the most frequently omitted.
Abuse A cable attached to a board whose ground plane carries bounce The bounce voltage appears between the board's ground and the cable shield, driving the cable as a common-mode antenna. A 1 m cable is a very efficient radiator around 75 MHz. This is why boards fail EMC with cables attached and pass without them. The cure is at the source — reduce the ground bounce — not at the cable. Ferrites on the cable treat the symptom and are the standard sign that the return paths were not designed.
