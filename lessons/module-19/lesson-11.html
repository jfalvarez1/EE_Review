Design: Driving a Higher Voltage from a Lower One

Your controller runs on 3.3 V. The thing you have to switch runs on 24 V, or 48 V, or 400 V. This is one of the most common jobs in practical electronics, it has about six standard answers, and choosing between them badly is how boards end up with parts that get hot, edges that ring, or FETs that never fully turn on.

Three questions, asked before choosing anything. They eliminate most of the options immediately.
  1. Is the load's low side free to sit at your ground? If yes, a low-side switch does the job and everything gets easier. If the load must be grounded — a chassis, a motor frame, a shared return — you are forced high-side and the problem changes completely.
  2. How fast, and how often? A relay coil switched once a minute and a MOSFET switched at 100 kHz are not the same problem. Speed is what makes gate charge, and therefore driver current, matter at all.
  3. Do the two sides share a ground? If not — mains, motor drives, anything with a real isolation requirement — no amount of level shifting will do and you need an optocoupler, a digital isolator or a transformer.
A way to hold the whole problem

A gate is a bucket you fill with charge, and how full it is decides how far the valve opens. Two separate things can go wrong, and they get confused constantly.

Not filling it high enough means the valve is only part open: the FET is a resistor rather than a switch and dissipates continuously. That is a voltage problem, and waiting does not fix it.

Not filling it fast enough means the valve spends a long time part open on every transition. That is a current problem — the bucket does fill, but slowly, and the loss happens during the fill. It only matters if you fill it often.

Where it breaks: a real bucket does not push back. A gate does, through \(C_{GD}\) — during the Miller plateau the level stops rising even though you are still pouring, because the drain is moving and stealing the charge. That plateau is where nearly all the switching loss lives, and the bucket picture cannot show it.

1. The six answers, and when each is right

ApproachUse it whenWatch out for
Low-side N-channel FET The load's return can float. The default answer and the cheapest — the source is at ground, so \(V_{GS}\) is simply your logic level. Needs a logic-level part. A standard FET specified at \(V_{GS} = 10\,\mathrm{V}\) may barely turn on at 3.3 V.
NPN or N-FET plus a pull-up Driving a high-voltage input rather than a load. Inverting, open-drain, trivially cheap. The rising edge is set by the pull-up and the capacitance, so the two edges are wildly asymmetric.
P-channel high-side FET High-side switching at modest speed, up to about 20 V. The gate is referred to the rail, so pulling it down turns it on — which a low-voltage part can do. \(V_{GS}\) must not exceed the gate rating, so above about 20 V a zener clamp stops being optional. Worse \(R_{DS(on)}\) than an equivalent N-channel.
N-channel high-side with a bootstrap High-side switching that has to be fast or efficient. The standard answer in every synchronous buck and half-bridge. Cannot hold on indefinitely — the bootstrap capacitor only recharges while the switch node is low, so 100% duty is impossible without a refresh.
Charge pump High-side, and you need 100% duty or a DC on-state. Slow to start, limited current, more parts. Usually integrated inside a dedicated high-side driver.
Optocoupler or digital isolator The two sides genuinely do not share a ground. Needs an isolated supply on the far side. An optocoupler's CTR degrades with age and temperature — design margin in.

\[ V_{GS} = 3.3\,\mathrm{V} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{a LOGIC-LEVEL FET} \] The first and most-missed decision. Read \(R_{DS(on)}\) off the datasheet's curve at \(V_{GS} = 2.5\) or 3 V, never the headline value at 10 V.
\[ P_{cond} = I^2R_{DS(on)} = (0.5)^2(0.05) = 12.5\,\mathrm{mW} \] With a 50 mΩ logic-level part. Nothing — at a few hertz there is no switching loss worth the name, and conduction is all there is.
\[ V_{DS(max)} > 24\,\mathrm{V} + \text{flyback margin} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{a 60 V part} \] Not 30 V. The inductive kick and any supply transient both land here, and derating a switch to half its rating is ordinary practice.
\[ R_{gate} = 100\,\Omega, \qquad R_{pulldown} = 100\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \] The series resistor limits the GPIO's peak current into the gate capacitance and damps ringing. The pull-down holds the gate off while the controller is in reset — without it the solenoid can energise during boot, which is a safety item rather than an inconvenience.
The component that is not optional

A solenoid is an inductor, and interrupting inductor current makes an arbitrarily large voltage: \(V = L\,di/dt\), with \(di/dt\) set by how fast the FET turns off. With no path for that current the FET avalanches and eventually fails.

\[ \text{Flyback diode across the coil, cathode to } +24\,\mathrm{V} \]

Rate it for the full coil current and choose it faster than the FET turns off. A 1N4007 is too slow for a fast edge; a 1N4148 is fast and too small at 500 mA; an ultrafast part in the right current class is the answer. If release time matters — a solenoid that must drop out quickly — put a zener in series with the diode so the coil de-energises against a higher voltage and therefore faster, paid for with a larger \(V_{DS}\) at the FET.

A 3.3 V GPIO drives a FET whose threshold is specified as "1 to 3 V". The board works on the bench, and a few units in the field run hot and fail. What is the most likely cause?

  1. The GPIO cannot supply enough current, so the edges are slow
    Slow edges cost energy per transition, so this only matters at high switching frequency — and it would affect every unit roughly equally. The clue is that only some fail.
  2. Threshold spread: on the high-\(V_{th}\) parts, 3.3 V leaves almost no overdrive
    Right. \(V_{th}\) is a distribution, not a number, and "1 to 3 V" is a wide one. A part at the 3 V end has 0.3 V of overdrive from a 3.3 V gate — barely on, enormous \(R_{DS(on)}\), dissipating continuously rather than switching. And \(V_{th}\) falls with temperature, so the part gets less bad as it heats, which is why it can pass a short test and fail a long one. Specify against \(V_{th(max)}\), and read \(R_{DS(on)}\) off the curve at your actual \(V_{GS}\).
  3. The flyback diode is too slow
    Worth checking — a slow flyback diode stresses the FET at turn-off — but it would not produce steady heating, and again would not single out a few units.
  4. The gate resistor is too large
    Same reasoning as (a): it changes edge speed, which matters at frequency, and affects all units equally.

"Works on some units" almost always points at a parameter distribution rather than a topology mistake. The design question is which datasheet corner you specified against.

4. When the load must be grounded: high side

Low side on the left: the source sits at ground, so \(V_{GS}\) is just the logic level and there is nothing to arrange. High side on the right: the source rides up to 24 V when the FET turns on, so the gate has to get above that.

\[ \text{Low side: } V_{GS} = V_{GPIO} - 0 = 3.3\,\mathrm{V} \]
\[ \text{High side, N-channel: } V_{GS} = V_{GPIO} - V_{LOAD} = 3.3 - 24 = -20.7\,\mathrm{V} \] Hard off, and it can never turn on. This is why high-side drive is a separate topic rather than a rearrangement of the same circuit.
\[ \text{Need } V_G > 24 + V_{GS(on)} \approx 34\,\mathrm{V} \;-\; \textit{above the supply} \]

The P-channel escape, and its limit

A P-channel FET's gate is referred to its source, which for a high-side switch is the rail. Pulling the gate down turns it on, and pulling something down is exactly what a low-voltage transistor can do.

\[ \text{A small NPN pulls the gate toward ground: } V_{SG} \to 24\,\mathrm{V} \] Which is a problem, not a success — \(V_{GS(max)}\) on most FETs is ±20 V and you have just exceeded it.
\[ \text{Add a 12 V zener gate-to-source, and a resistor gate-to-source} \] The zener clamps \(V_{SG}\) to a safe value; the resistor guarantees the FET is OFF when nothing is driving it. Above about 20 V rails the clamp stops being optional.
Why high side is genuinely harder

A low-side switch is a tap at the bottom of the tank: you stand on the ground next to it and turn it. A high-side switch is a tap at the top, and the handle moves up and down with the water level — so to turn it you have to be standing on the tap itself.

That is exactly what a bootstrap does. The capacitor is a small platform that rides on the source, charged while the source is down, so the driver always has firm footing relative to the pin it is driving. And it explains the limitation in one line: if the source never comes back down, the platform never gets recharged, which is why a bootstrapped high-side switch cannot stay on forever.

Where it breaks: the platform is not free-floating — the bootstrap diode ties it to the driver supply, and that diode's reverse recovery and voltage rating are real design constraints the picture says nothing about.

Abuse

What is done to itWhat physically happensAcceptable outcome
Abuse 10 V applied to the 3.3 V gate input Through the 100 Ω series resistor the gate reaches 10 V. Most logic-level FETs are rated \(V_{GS(max)} = \pm 20\,\mathrm{V}\), so the FET is fine. The casualty is upstream: the GPIO's ESD diode to its own 3.3 V rail conducts, and \(({10-3.3-0.7})/100 = \mathbf{60\ mA}\) flows into a pin rated for about 10 mA. The microcontroller's I/O structure is damaged or the 3.3 V rail is pulled up, which can brown out or latch the whole controller. Raise the series resistor to 1 kΩ and the fault current falls to 6 mA, inside the pin's limit — a one-component change that turns a destroyed controller into a shrug.
Abuse 48 V applied to a 24 V load rail The FET now stands off 48 V against a 60 V rating — survivable on its own. But the flyback spike rides on top of it: even a well-behaved diode leaves 48 V plus a volt or two, and a slow one leaves far more. The solenoid also dissipates four times its rated power. The FET survives; the solenoid coil burns out, and it does so slowly enough that nobody connects the two events. If the supply can plausibly be wrong, a series fuse sized just above the coil's rated current is the cheap answer.
Abuse Flyback diode fitted backwards It is now a forward-biased diode across the 24 V supply. Current is limited only by the supply and the diode's bulk resistance — amps. The diode fails short within milliseconds, then the supply's own protection trips or its trace opens. Loud, immediate and obvious, which is the good kind of failure: the bad version is a diode that survives long enough to look intermittent.
Abuse Load shorted Current is limited by \(R_{DS(on)}\) and the supply: \(24/0.05 = \mathbf{480\ A}\) if the supply could deliver it. It cannot, so in practice the supply collapses or its fuse opens, and the FET sees whatever it can pass in the meantime. Depends entirely on the supply. A current-limited supply protects everything; a large capacitor bank does not, and the FET fails first. If the load can be shorted by a user — an accessible connector — then the switch needs its own current limit, and "the supply will protect it" is a hope rather than a design.
Abuse Gate driven at 100 kHz instead of a few Hz The 4 mA GPIO takes \(Q_g/I = 12\,\mathrm{nC}/4\,\mathrm{mA} = 3\,\mu\mathrm{s}\) to charge the gate. At 100 kHz the period is 10 µs, so the FET is in transition for 60% of every cycle — the region where it has both voltage across it and current through it. The FET overheats within seconds. Nothing about the schematic changed; only the rate did. This is why "it works" at one frequency proves nothing at another, and why the calculator above reports the fraction of the period spent switching rather than just the edge time.
Note which way the damage runs. In the overvoltage case the FET is fine and the controller is destroyed, through a path — the GPIO's ESD diode — that appears on no schematic. A series resistor sized for the fault rather than for the edge is the entire fix, and it costs nothing. Ask of every interface: if the far side is wrong, what current flows, and into what?

6. Build it in Circuit Toy

The gallery has both halves of this already: Low-side Switch + Flyback, High-side PMOS Switch, Bootstrap High-Side Drive, Open-Collector Level Shift, Open-Drain + Pull-up, I2C Level Shifter and Pull-up Sizing.

  1. Open Low-side Switch + Flyback and remove the flyback diode. Watch the drain voltage at turn-off and see how far it goes — that spike is what the diode is for, and it is more convincing measured than described.
  2. Now rebuild the same load as a high-side N-channel switch with the gate driven from 3.3 V. Plot V(gate) − V(source) and watch it sit at roughly −20 V. The trace being flat is the whole lesson of section 4.
  3. Replace the gate source with one that pulses to 34 V — ten volts above the rail, which is exactly what a bootstrap manufactures — and watch the same circuit start working. That one substitution is the content of high-side drive.
  4. Then open Bootstrap High-Side Drive and probe the gate against the switch node, to see the gate riding 10 V above a source that is itself swinging between ground and the rail.
  5. Finally, in Open-Drain + Pull-up, measure the two edge times. The asymmetry — fast falling, slow rising — is the cost of the cheapest level shifter there is.

What to carry forward

Ask where the load's return is
Low side if it can float. That answer costs nothing and eliminates the hard problem.
\(V_{GS}\) is source-referred
A high-side N-channel gate must go ABOVE the rail. Everything else follows.
Two failure modes, not one
Not high enough is a voltage problem; not fast enough is a current problem. Only the second scales with frequency.
Specify against \(V_{th(max)}\)
Threshold is a distribution. "Works on most units" is what designing at typical looks like.
Inductive load, flyback path
Not optional. Faster than the FET turns off, rated for full coil current.
Size the series resistor for the fault
Not for the edge. It is what stands between a wrong voltage and a dead controller.
