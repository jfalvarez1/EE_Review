What the Square Law Gets Wrong

The previous two lessons were built on ID = ½kVov2. It is a good model over maybe two decades of current in a long-channel device, and it is wrong at both ends and wrong again on any modern process. This lesson is where the model fails, what replaces it, and which of those failures you will actually meet.

Why bother, if the square law works well enough? Because two of the things you most want to do — run at very low current to save power, and match two transistors to build a current mirror — live precisely in the regions where it stops being true.

1. Below threshold, a MOSFET is a BJT

The square law says the current is zero for VGS < Vth. It is not. Threshold is not a cliff but the middle of a gentle transition, and below it the channel conducts by diffusion rather than drift — the same mechanism as a bipolar, and with the same exponential law:

ID = I0 · exp( Vov / (n·VT) )    with n ≈ 1.3–1.5
gm = ID / (n VT)  — linear in current, exactly like a BJT

Two consequences worth holding onto. First, the “off” MOSFET in a low-power design is not off; it leaks, and the leak rises exponentially with temperature. Second — and this is the useful one — subthreshold is where a MOSFET gives you its best transconductance per amp. Deliberately operating there is how micropower analog design works, in hearing aids and implantables and anything else running for years off a coin cell.

2. gm/ID: the number designers actually use

Transconductance per unit current is the efficiency of a transistor as an amplifier, and it has a hard ceiling:

subthreshold:   gm/ID = 1/(nVT) ≈ 26–30 V−1 — the maximum any MOSFET can achieve
square law:   gm/ID = 2/Vov — falls as you overdrive harder
a BJT, for comparison:   gm/IC = 1/VT = 38.5 V−1, always

So the bipolar's advantage from the last lesson has a precise interpretation: a BJT sits permanently at the efficiency a MOSFET can only approach, and only by giving up speed. The factor n — the subthreshold slope factor, set by how well the gate controls the channel against the depletion capacitance below it — is the entire gap.

3. Above: velocity saturation

The square law assumes carrier velocity rises in proportion to the field. It does not — above roughly 104 V/cm in silicon, carriers hit a saturation velocity of about 107 cm/s and go no faster however hard you push. Once the channel is in that condition the current stops growing as Vov2 and grows roughly linearly:

ID ≈ W·Cox·vsat·(Vov − Vc/2)   for Vov > Vc
gm → W·Cox·vsat  — constant. More overdrive buys no more transconductance.

The knee voltage Vc = L·Esat scales with channel length, so this matters enormously on a modern short-channel process and hardly at all on a discrete power MOSFET with a long channel. It is the reason textbook square-law intuition and deep-submicron practice diverge: in a 28 nm process a transistor is velocity-saturated over most of its useful range, and gm is set by W alone.

4. Matching: the number that decides whether a current mirror works

Two transistors drawn identically are not identical. Threshold voltage mismatch between a pair follows Pelgrom's law:

σ(ΔVth) = AVT / sqrt(W·L)

with AVT typically 3–10 mV·µm for a modern CMOS process. Read what that says: mismatch improves only as the square root of area. Halving the mismatch costs four times the silicon. There is no clever circuit that evades this; it is why precision analog blocks are physically enormous next to the digital logic beside them.

Turn it into the number you care about. A mirror's output error comes from ΔVth acting through gm:

ΔID/ID = gm·ΔVth/ID = (2/Vov)·ΔVth

So a 5 mV threshold mismatch at Vov = 100 mV gives a 10% current error; the same mismatch at Vov = 500 mV gives 2%. That is the central design trade of the current mirror, and it points the opposite way to gm/ID: low overdrive is efficient and badly matched; high overdrive is well matched and wastes headroom. You will make this trade explicitly in the mirror design lesson.

This is why discrete mirrors are built from matched pairs. Two separate 2N3904s in separate packages may differ by tens of millivolts in VBE and sit at different temperatures. A dual-transistor part on one die — the classic is the MAT12 or a BC847BS — is specified for matching, typically under 1 mV, because that is the only parameter that matters when you are buying two transistors instead of one.

5. Temperature: two effects pulling opposite ways

  • Vth falls by roughly 2 mV per °C. Alone, this would raise the current as the device heats.
  • Mobility falls, roughly as T−1.5, so k falls. Alone, this would lower the current.

Which wins depends on overdrive. At low Vov the threshold shift dominates and current rises with temperature — a positive feedback loop that ends in thermal runaway if the device is dissipating any real power. At high Vov mobility dominates and current falls with temperature, which is self-correcting and is exactly why power MOSFETs can be paralleled without ballast resistors while BJTs cannot.

Between the two lies the zero-temperature-coefficient point, typically around Vov ≈ 0.5–0.8 V. Biasing there gives a drain current that barely moves over temperature, and it is a standard trick in reference circuits.

The paralleling rule this explains. “MOSFETs share current, BJTs do not” is only true above the ZTC point. Parallel two power MOSFETs biased in their linear region — a hot-swap controller holding them partly on, say — and you are below it, the tempco is positive, and the hotter device takes more current until it fails. This is a real failure mode in soft-start circuits, and it surprises people who learned the rule without its condition.

Check yourself

1. You need a current mirror matched to 1%. Your process gives AVT = 5 mV·µm and you plan Vov = 200 mV. What device area do you need?

Answer

1% error needs ΔVth = 0.01 × Vov/2 = 0.01 × 0.1 = 1 mV. Pelgrom: sqrt(WL) = AVT/σ = 5/1 = 5 µm, so WL = 25 µm2. And that is one standard deviation — for a yield spec you would design to 3σ, which needs nine times the area, 225 µm2. The cost of matching is brutal and it is the first thing to check before promising a precision number.

2. A circuit works at 25 °C and its bias current climbs steadily as the board warms, eventually destroying the transistor. Where is it biased, and what is the fix?

Answer

Below the ZTC point — at low overdrive, where the −2 mV/°C threshold shift beats the mobility roll-off, so heating raises current, which raises dissipation, which raises heating. Fixes, in order of preference: bias from a current source rather than a voltage so the loop is broken entirely; raise Vov toward the ZTC point; or add source degeneration, which converts the current rise into a VGS reduction that opposes it. Note the first and third are the same two answers as the common-source biasing lesson — thermal runaway and Vth spread are the same problem in different clothes.
