MOSFET Small-Signal Model and gm

Everything else in this module treats the MOSFET as a switch: on or off, Rds(on) and gate charge. This lesson treats it as an amplifier, which means finding the one number that says how well it amplifies — the transconductance gm.

The result is worth stating before we derive it, because it explains a great deal about why analog circuits look the way they do: a MOSFET's gm grows only as the square root of its current, while a BJT's grows in direct proportion. That single difference decides which device you reach for far more often than any other.

1. From the square law to gm

In saturation, with the channel fully pinched off, the drain current of a long-channel MOSFET follows the square law. Write the overdrive voltage Vov = VGS − Vth, because every useful expression below is written in terms of it rather than VGS:

ID = ½ · k' · (W/L) · Vov2    (saturation, ignoring channel-length modulation)
write k = k'·(W/L), the device's transconductance parameter, in A/V2

Transconductance is the slope of that curve — how much drain current you get per volt of gate. So differentiate:

gm = dID/dVGS = d(½ k Vov2)/dVov = k · Vov
substitute Vov = sqrt(2ID/k)   →   gm = sqrt(2 · k · ID)
or eliminate k instead   →   gm = 2 ID / Vov

Those are the same equation three ways, and which one you reach for depends on what you know. 2ID/Vov is the design form: it says gm is set by the current you bias at and how hard you overdrive the gate.

Compare with the BJT, which is the whole point. For a bipolar, gm = IC/VT with VT = 26 mV — linear in current, and with no device parameter in it at all. Two transistors of wildly different geometry biased at the same current have the same gm. Nothing about a MOSFET works that way.

2. The ratio that decides which device you use

Bias a BJT and a MOSFET at the same current and take the ratio:

gm,BJT / gm,FET = (I/VT) / (2I/Vov) = Vov / (2 VT)

The current cancels. The bipolar's advantage depends only on how hard you are overdriving the FET's gate — and you cannot make Vov small without limit, because below roughly 100 mV the device slides out of strong inversion and the square law stops describing it. With VT = 26 mV, even at that floor the BJT is ahead by 100/(2×26) ≈ 2×, and at a realistic Vov of 200–300 mV it is ahead by 4–6×.

So: if what you need is transconductance per microamp — a low-noise front end, a precision input stage, anything running off a battery — the BJT wins and keeps winning. The MOSFET earns its place on the other axes: no gate current at all, a switch with no offset voltage, and the ability to be built by the million on a die.

3. Worked example: 1 mA, and what it costs to catch up

Take a small-signal MOSFET with k = 25 mA/V2, biased at ID = 1 mA:

gm = sqrt(2 × 0.025 × 0.001) = sqrt(5×10−5) = 7.07 mS
Vov = 2ID/gm = 0.002/0.00707 = 283 mV
a BJT at 1 mA: gm = 0.001/0.026 = 38.5 mS
ratio = Vov/(2VT) = 0.283/0.052 = 5.4×

Now ask the design question: how much current would the FET need to reach the bipolar's 38.5 mS? Invert the square-root law:

ID = gm2 / (2k) = (0.0385)2 / (2 × 0.025) = 29.6 mA

Thirty times the current for the same transconductance. That is the square-root law charging you for the privilege, and it is why the input stage of a precision op-amp is usually bipolar while the input stage of an electrometer — where input current matters more than gm — is always FET.

4. The rest of the model: ro and the capacitances

gm alone would give infinite gain, so the model needs the output resistance. Channel-length modulation makes ID creep up with VDS:

ID = ½kVov2(1 + λVDS)  →  ro = 1/(λID) = VA/ID
intrinsic gain = gmro = (2ID/Vov)(VA/ID) = 2VA/Vov

The current cancels again. Intrinsic gain does not depend on bias current — for either device. It is the ceiling on the voltage gain a single transistor can produce, and no amount of biasing moves it. For the BJT the same algebra gives VA/VT, which with VA = 100 V is about 3800, against 2×20/0.283 ≈ 141 for our FET. Another order of magnitude to the bipolar.

The capacitances are what end the story at high frequency:

  • Cgs — gate to source, the larger of the two, and a load on whatever drives the gate.
  • Cgd — gate to drain. Small, and the dangerous one: it bridges input to output, so the Miller effect multiplies it by the stage's gain. This is exactly the problem the cascode exists to solve, and it is why datasheets call it Crss, the reverse transfer capacitance.
Datasheet translation. A switching MOSFET datasheet does not print Cgs and Cgd. It prints Ciss, Coss and Crss, measured with the drain shorted to the source for AC. Convert them: Crss = Cgd, Ciss = Cgs + Cgd so Cgs = Ciss − Crss, and Coss = Cds + Cgd.

5. What bites you

  • gm is not a device constant. A datasheet quotes gfs at one specific ID. Bias at a tenth of that current and you have about a third of the gm, not a tenth and not the quoted value. Always rescale by sqrt.
  • Vth spread is enormous on discrete parts. A 2N7000 is specified 0.8 V to 3.0 V — a range of nearly 4:1 across parts you may buy on one reel. Any circuit that sets bias by applying a fixed VGS will not survive a change of supplier. Bias with feedback, or with a current source, and let VGS land where it lands.
  • The square law is a long-channel approximation. Below about 100 mV of overdrive the device is in subthreshold and behaves exponentially, like a BJT. In short-channel processes, velocity saturation makes ID closer to linear in Vov, so gm stops rising. The next lesson is entirely about where this model fails.
  • Body effect raises Vth when the source is not at the bulk. In a source follower or a stacked stage this is not a correction, it is a first-order design problem.

Check yourself

1. A datasheet quotes gfs = 100 mS at ID = 200 mA. You intend to bias at 2 mA. What gm should you expect?

Answer

gm ∝ sqrt(ID), so scale by sqrt(2/200) = sqrt(0.01) = 0.1. Expect about 10 mS — a tenth of the quoted figure for a hundredth of the current. A BJT would have fallen by the full factor of 100. Note also what this implies about k: k = gm2/(2ID) = 0.01/0.4 = 25 mA/V2, the value used in the worked example above.

2. You need gm = 20 mS and you may spend 5 mA. What k must the device have, and what overdrive results?

Answer

k = gm2/(2ID) = 0.0004/0.01 = 40 mA/V2. Then Vov = 2ID/gm = 0.01/0.02 = 500 mV. That is a comfortable overdrive — safely in strong inversion, and leaving room in the supply. Had the answer come out at 50 mV you would have had to accept a larger device or more current, because the square law you designed with would no longer be true.
