The Common-Source Amplifier

This is the FET's workhorse voltage amplifier, and the direct counterpart of the bipolar common-emitter stage: signal in at the gate, out at the drain, inverted. Given gm from the previous lesson, the gain is one line. The interesting part — and where a first attempt usually fails — is biasing it so that the gain equation is still true with the transistor you actually bought.

1. The circuit

R1/R2 set the gate voltage. RS stabilises the bias and is bypassed by CS so it does not cost gain at signal frequencies. RD converts drain current back into a voltage.

2. The gain, in one line

Small-signal: the gate draws no current, the transistor is a current source of gmvgs, and that current flows through whatever is at the drain.

Av = − gm · (RD ∥ ro)  ≈  −gmRD   when ro ≫ RD
Zin = R1 ∥ R2   — the gate itself contributes nothing
Zout = RD ∥ ro  ≈  RD

The minus sign is real: rising gate voltage means more drain current, more drop across RD, and a falling drain voltage. The stage inverts.

Against the common-emitter. Same equation, Av = −gmRC, and that is not a coincidence — both are transconductors feeding a resistor. Two differences matter. The FET's Zin is set purely by your bias resistors, because no gate current flows; the BJT's is βre in parallel with them, which is finite and varies with β. But the FET's gm is 4–6× lower at the same current, so the same gain costs you a larger RD — and a larger RD costs headroom and bandwidth.

3. If RS is not bypassed, it eats the gain

Leave CS out and the source is no longer at signal ground. Source current now develops a voltage that subtracts from vgs — local negative feedback, called source degeneration:

Av = −gmRD / (1 + gmRS)
when gmRS ≫ 1  →  Av → −RD/RS

Look at what that limit says: the gain no longer contains gm at all. It is a ratio of two resistors — components you can buy at 1% while gm varies by tens of percent across parts and temperature. You have traded gain for predictability, which is the same bargain the whole feedback stage of the path is about, appearing here in its smallest possible form.

In the worked design below, gmRS = 7.07, so leaving CS off drops the gain from −10.6 to −1.3. That is the usual reason a first common-source stage measures almost no gain: the bypass capacitor was forgotten, or it is too small at the frequency of interest.

4. Worked design: Av = −10 from a 12 V supply

Device: k = 25 mA/V2, Vth = 2.0 V nominal. Budget ID = 1 mA.

gm = sqrt(2kID) = sqrt(2 × 0.025 × 0.001) = 7.07 mS   and   Vov = 2ID/gm = 283 mV
RD = |Av|/gm = 10/0.00707 = 1414 Ω  →  use 1.5 kΩ (E24), giving Av = −10.6
RS = 1 kΩ → VS = 1.0 V   (chosen for bias stability, see below)
VG = VS + Vth + Vov = 1.0 + 2.0 + 0.283 = 3.28 V
divider from 12 V: R1 = 2.2 MΩ, R2 = 820 kΩ → VG = 12 × 820/3020 = 3.26 V

Headroom check. VD = 12 − (1 mA × 1.5 k) = 10.5 V, and VS = 1.0 V, so VDS = 9.5 V — far above Vov = 0.28 V, so the device is comfortably in saturation with room for a large output swing. Output can swing down to about VS + Vov = 1.3 V before the transistor leaves saturation and the stage starts clipping.

Why the divider resistors are megohms. Nothing flows into the gate, so you may use very large values — and you should, because they are the entire input impedance of the stage. 2.2 M ∥ 820 k = 597 kΩ. Try that with a BJT and base current through megohm resistors would move the bias voltage more than the signal does.

5. Solving the bias, and why RS saves it

With a source resistor the drain current appears on both sides of the equation — more current raises VS, which reduces Vov, which reduces the current. That is the negative feedback doing the stabilising, and it makes the bias a quadratic:

ID = ½k·Vov2   with   Vov = VG − IDRS − Vth
½kRSVov2 + Vov − (VG − Vth) = 0
Vov = [ −1 + sqrt(1 + 2kRS(VG − Vth)) ] / (kRS)

Put the numbers in and the difference is stark. Take the design above and fit a part from the low end of the tolerance band, Vth = 1.5 V instead of 2.0 V:

The fair comparison applies a fixed VGS = Vth + Vov = 2.283 V directly to the gate — the value that produces exactly 1 mA in the nominal part. Not the divider's 3.26 V, which is 1 V higher precisely because RS drops that volt.

Half a volt of threshold shift — well inside a single part number's guaranteed range — multiplies a fixed-VGS bias by nearly eight. At 7.66 mA the drop across RD is 11.5 V from a 12 V supply: the transistor has left saturation, the drain is on the floor, and the stage has no gain at all. With RS the same shift costs 45%, which the design absorbs without leaving saturation.

Note the nominal row reads 0.98 mA, not 1.00. That is the E24 divider: 2.2 M and 820 k give 3.26 V rather than the 3.283 V the algebra asked for. Designing with real component values and then checking what you actually get is the habit; the 2% shortfall here is harmless, and knowing it is 2% is the point.
The rule this earns: never set a MOSFET's operating point by applying a fixed VGS. Set the current — with a source resistor, or better, with a current source — and let VGS land wherever that particular die needs it to. The current mirror later in the path is this idea taken to its conclusion.

6. What bites you

  • The bypass capacitor is a high-pass filter. CS only bypasses RS above f = 1/(2πCS·(1/gm)). Note it is 1/gm, not RS, that sets that corner — the source looks back into 1/gm = 141 Ω here, so a 10 µF cap corners at 113 Hz, not at 16 Hz. Sizing it against RS is a common way to lose all the bass in an audio stage.
  • Miller multiplies Cgd by the gain. With Av = −10.6 and Crss = 5 pF, the gate sees 5 × 11.6 = 58 pF. Into a 597 kΩ bias network that is a pole at 4.6 kHz — an audio amplifier with no treble, caused entirely by an effect that is invisible at DC.
  • RD trades gain against headroom and bandwidth. Doubling RD doubles the gain, halves the available output swing, and halves the bandwidth into a given load capacitance. There is no setting that wins all three.
  • Large-value bias dividers pick up everything. A 597 kΩ input impedance is a wonderful thing for the source and an antenna for mains hum and capacitive coupling. This is where guard traces and shielding start mattering.

Check yourself

1. Your common-source stage measures a gain of −1.3 instead of the −10.6 you designed. What is the most likely single cause?

Answer

The source bypass capacitor is missing, open, or far too small for the frequency you are testing at. Undegenerated gain is −gmRD = −10.6; degenerated it is −gmRD/(1+gmRS) = −10.6/8.07 = −1.31. The measured value is not a random shortfall — it is exactly the degenerated answer, which is what identifies the fault.

2. You need the same −10 gain but from a 3.3 V supply. What breaks, and what do you change?

Answer

Headroom. The old design spends 1.0 V on RS and 1.5 V on RD, which does not fit under 3.3 V once you also need VDS > Vov and some output swing. Reduce the current so the same gain needs less voltage drop: at ID = 250 µA, gm = sqrt(2×0.025×0.00025) = 3.54 mS, so RD = 10/0.00354 = 2.8 kΩ and the drop is only 0.71 V. Halving current halves the drop for the same gain, because gm falls only as the square root while RD can rise in proportion. That is the square-root law finally working for you.
