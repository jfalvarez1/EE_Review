Design: A Current Mirror from a Datasheet

This is the end of the path, and it is a different kind of lesson. Everything before it asked “how does this work”. This one asks “which part do I buy, and will it still meet spec at 70 °C with the transistor I actually receive” — which is the question engineering consists of.

The specification. A 1.00 mA current source, accurate to ±5%, from a 12 V rail, with the output free to sit anywhere between 1 V and 8 V. Built from discrete parts you can order today.

We will find that the obvious circuit misses this spec by a factor of seven, identify which of four error terms is responsible, and fix it two different ways — one that costs a better part and one that costs headroom.

1. The circuit, and the four things wrong with it

Q1 is diode-connected, so it sits at whatever VBE passes IREF. Q2 sees the same VBE and, if the two devices are identical, passes the same current. RE1 and RE2 are the fix developed in section 4; set them to zero for the basic mirror.

Every error in this circuit is a way in which “identical” and “the same VBE” fail to be true:

2. The term that kills it: VBE mismatch

Collector current is exponential in VBE, so a mismatch does not produce a proportional error — it produces an exponential one:

IC = IS·exp(VBE/VT)  →  I2/I1 = exp(ΔVBE/VT)
for small mismatch:   ΔI/I ≈ ΔVBE / VT = ΔVBE / 26 mV

Now open a datasheet. The 2N3904's does not specify VBE matching between two separate devices at all, because nobody sane relies on it. Measured, two parts from the same bag typically differ by 5–20 mV. Take 10 mV:

ΔI/I = 10 mV / 26 mV = 38%

Against a ±5% specification. You cannot build a current mirror from two separate transistors, and this is why — not tolerance stack-up, not sloppiness, but 26 mV being a very small number to be compared against.

The same trade as the MOSFET. For a FET pair the equivalent is ΔI/I = (2/Vov)·ΔVth. The bipolar's VT is fixed at 26 mV and cannot be raised; the FET's Vov is yours to choose, which is the one matching advantage the FET has. Section 4 gives the BJT the same freedom artificially.

Three orders of magnitude of matching for two orders of magnitude of money. Note what you are actually buying with the MAT12: not a better transistor — its β and VA are unremarkable — but two transistors on one piece of silicon, adjacent, at the same temperature, specified for their difference. The parameter you are paying for is one that does not exist for a single device.

Thermal coupling is half the reason. Two TO-92 parts 10 mm apart on a board will differ by degrees once anything nearby dissipates power, and at 2 mV/°C a 5 °C difference is 10 mV of fresh mismatch — as large as the initial error, and it drifts while you watch. On one die that difference is millikelvins. This is also why you glue discrete pairs together with a spot of epoxy when you cannot afford a matched part.

4. The fix that costs headroom instead of money

Put a resistor in each emitter. Now the mismatch competes against I·RE instead of against VT:

ΔI/I = ΔVBE / (VT + I·RE)  ≈  ΔVBE/(I·RE)   when I·RE ≫ VT

The emitter resistors are local negative feedback — the same degeneration as the common-source lesson, doing the same job: trading away a device parameter you cannot control for a resistor ratio you can buy at 1%. With 1 mA through 470 Ω, I·RE = 470 mV, and the 10 mV mismatch of two separate 2N3904s becomes:

ΔI/I = 10 mV / (26 + 470) mV = 2.0%

From 38% to 2%, for two resistors and half a volt of headroom. That half-volt is the real cost: it comes off your output compliance range, and in a 3.3 V system it may be more than you can spare. Money or headroom — the mirror makes you choose.

5. The other two errors, and when they matter

Base current. Both bases are fed from IREF, so the output is short by two base currents:

IOUT/IREF = 1/(1 + 2/β)  →  error ≈ −2/β

At β = 150 that is −1.3%, and it is systematic — always low, never high — so unlike mismatch you can simply trim it out. But β is specified 100 to 300 on a 2N3904, so the error is somewhere between −2% and −0.7% and you do not know where. The classic fix is a third transistor as a beta helper, dividing the error by another factor of β.

Early effect. Q1 sits at VCE = VBE ≈ 0.65 V because it is diode-connected. Q2 sits wherever the load puts it. That difference, and any movement of it, changes the current:

ΔI/I = ΔVCE/VA = 7 V / 100 V = 7%

Over the specified 1–8 V compliance range this is the largest remaining term once matching is solved, and no amount of matching touches it — it is not a mismatch, it is both devices responding correctly to different collector voltages. The fix is a cascode, which holds Q2's collector voltage fixed and lets the cascode device absorb the swing. That is the same cascode from the blocks stage of the path, arriving here as the answer to a specific numerical problem.

Note the second row: degeneration alone fixes matching and the design still fails, because the dominant error moved to the Early effect. Fixing the largest error term promotes the second-largest to being your problem, and an error budget is the only way to see that coming rather than discovering it on the bench.

Which of the last two you choose is not an electronics question. The BC847BS row costs $0.15 and half a volt of compliance; the MAT12 row costs $10 and keeps the headroom. At ten thousand units the resistors win; for one instrument where the output must swing to the rail, the matched pair does.

Check yourself

1. Your mirror is accurate at 1 mA but 2% low at 100 µA, using the same parts. Why?

Answer

Two candidates, and the numbers separate them. Degeneration is the likely one: ΔI/I = ΔVBE/(VT + IRE), and dropping the current by 10× drops IRE by 10× too — at 100 µA through 470 Ω that is 47 mV against VT's 26 mV, so the degeneration has almost stopped working. The other candidate is β, which falls at low current on every datasheet's hFE curve (a 2N3904 is specified 40 minimum at 100 µA against 100 at 10 mA), making the 2/β term worse. Both are real; the fix for the first is a larger RE, and for the second a beta helper.

2. Why does the MAT12 datasheet specify ΔVBE in microvolts when no single-transistor datasheet specifies VBE better than about ±100 mV?

Answer

Because they are different quantities. Absolute VBE depends on IS, which depends on absolute geometry and doping and varies wildly between wafers and lots — and it does not matter, because the diode-connected side self-adjusts to whatever it is. What matters is the difference between two devices, which on one die shares almost every process variable and so can be controlled to microvolts. This is the general principle behind all precision analog: absolute values are cheap and bad, ratios and differences on one die are expensive and excellent. It is why the instrumentation amplifier earlier in the path lives or dies on resistor matching rather than resistor accuracy.
