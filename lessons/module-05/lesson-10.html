The Cascode Amplifier

Two lessons have now ended by pointing here. The small-signal lesson showed that a single transistor's gain is capped at \(\gm\ro = \va/\vt\) and that no amount of bias current moves it. The Miller lesson showed that a common-emitter stage turns a 300 MHz transistor into a 215 kHz amplifier. The cascode fixes both, with one extra transistor and no extra current.

It is one idea: stop the collector from moving. Stack a common-base stage on top of a common-emitter one. The lower device still does all the amplifying, but its collector now looks into a very low impedance instead of a load resistor, so the voltage there barely changes. Everything the cascode is good for follows from that single sentence.

Most treatments give one reason for the cascode and leave the reader thinking it is a bandwidth trick. It is two independent wins that happen to share a circuit, and they are worth separating because a design can want either one without the other.

1. The circuit

Q1 amplifies. Q2's base is held at a fixed DC voltage by R3/R4 and pinned to AC ground by CB, so Q2's emitter — and therefore Q1's collector — is a low-impedance node that hardly moves. The same current flows through both devices; the cascode costs no extra bias current at all. The two lines crossing near the top left are not connected; every real junction carries a dot.

Read it as two stages you already know:

DeviceConfigurationWhat it contributes
Q1Common-emitter All the transconductance. It converts \(v_{in}\) into a signal current \(\gm v_{in}\).
Q2Common-base No gain of its own — it passes that current straight through (\(\alpha \approx 1\)) and delivers it to the load at a much higher impedance.

Q2 is a current buffer. It takes a current in at low impedance and puts the same current out at high impedance, which is precisely the opposite of what an emitter follower does for voltage.

You build this and measure Q1's collector sitting at 0.1 V — Q1 is saturated and the stage has no gain. Q2's base is at 1.0 V and the emitter of Q1 is at 0.6 V. What is wrong?

  1. RC is too large, so the whole stack has run out of supply
    RC sets the DC collector voltage of Q2, not of Q1. Q1's collector is pinned by Q2's emitter, one VBE below Q2's base, and changing RC does not move it.
  2. CB is shorted, pulling Q2's base down
    A shorted CB would hold Q2's base at AC ground — which is exactly what it is supposed to do. It would not change the DC voltage at all.
  3. Q2's base is biased too low for Q1 to stay in the active region
    Q1's collector sits at VB2 − VBE2 = 1.0 − 0.7 = 0.3 V, while its emitter is at 0.6 V — the collector is below the emitter, which is hard saturation. Q2's base needs at least VE1 + VCE1 + VBE2 = 0.6 + 0.3 + 0.7 = 1.6 V, and comfortably more in practice. This is the headroom cost from section 5 arriving as a bench symptom.
  4. Q2 is dead, so no current reaches the load
    A dead Q2 would leave Q1's collector floating high toward the rail through RC, not pinned low. The symptom would be the opposite of the one described.

The general rule worth carrying: on a cascode, always check Q1's collector before anything else. It is the node that both devices fight over, and almost every cascode that does not work has it in the wrong place.

2. Win one: the Miller effect is gone

From the Miller lesson, the input capacitance of a stage is \(C_{in} = C_\pi + C_\mu(1 + A_1)\), where \(A_1\) is the gain of the device carrying that \(C_\mu\). In a cascode, Q1's load is not \(R_C\) — it is the impedance looking into Q2's emitter:

\[ R_{\text{load of }Q_1} = r_{e2} = \frac{\vt}{I_E} \approx \frac{1}{\gm_2} \] The input resistance of a common-base stage, from the common-base lesson. At 1 mA that is \(26\,\Omega\), not \(6\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\).
\[ A_1 = -\gm_1 \cdot \frac{1}{\gm_2} = -\frac{\gm_1}{\gm_2} = -1 \] Both devices carry the same current, so their transconductances are equal and the ratio is exactly one. This is why the cascode works so cleanly on a BJT: \(\gm\) depends only on current.
\[ C_{in} = C_\pi + C_\mu(1 + 1) = C_\pi + 2C_\mu \]
\[ = 10 + 8 = 18\,\mathrm{pF} \quad\text{against}\quad 10 + 732 = 742\,\mathrm{pF} \] The same devices from the Miller lesson. A factor of 41 in input capacitance, and therefore in bandwidth.
The bandwidth, worked

\[ f_{-3dB} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_S C_{in}} = \frac{1}{2\pi(1000)(18\,\mathrm{pF})} = 8.8\,\mathrm{MHz} \]

Against \(215\,\mathrm{kHz}\) for the plain stage, at exactly the same voltage gain. The gain did not disappear — it moved upstairs to Q2, which is common-base and has no Miller path of its own, because its input and output sit on opposite sides of the device with nothing bridging them.

3. Win two: the output resistance, and the ceiling nobody mentions

This is the half that gets left out, and it is the half that matters for building op-amps. Looking into the cascode's output, Q2 has \(\ro_1\) sitting in its emitter — and a resistor in the emitter is degeneration, which raises output resistance:

\[ R_{out} = \ro_2 + \bigl(1 + \gm_2 \ro_2\bigr)\,\bigl(\ro_1 \parallel r_{\pi 2}\bigr) \] The standard degenerated-output result: the emitter resistance is multiplied up by the device's own intrinsic gain.
\[ \ro_1 \parallel r_{\pi 2} = 80\,\mathrm{k} \parallel 3.878\,\mathrm{k} = 3.699\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \]
\[ R_{out} = 80\,\mathrm{k} + (1 + 3095)(3699) = 11.5\,\mathrm{M}\Omega \] Against \(\ro = 80\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) for a single transistor: 144 times higher.
Now push \(\ro_1\) to infinity and watch what happens. If the lower device were perfect, \(\ro_1 \parallel r_{\pi 2} \to r_{\pi 2} = \beta/\gm_2\), and: \[ R_{out} \to \gm_2 \ro_2 \cdot \frac{\beta}{\gm_2} = \boxed{\;\beta\,\ro\;} \] A bipolar cascode's output resistance is capped at \(\beta\ro\) — \(150 \times 80\,\mathrm{k} = 12\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) here, which is why the calculation above landed at 11.5 M and not higher. No amount of improvement in the lower device gets past it, because \(r_\pi\) shunts the degeneration away.
Which is why MOS cascodes are different

A MOSFET gate draws no current, so there is no \(r_\pi\) to do the shunting, and the same expression gives \(R_{out} \approx \gm_2\ro_2\ro_1\) with no ceiling at all — the intrinsic gain squared. That is the reason analog CMOS leans so heavily on cascoding while bipolar designs reach for it less often: on a FET it buys you \((\gm\ro)^2 \approx 10^4\), on a BJT it buys you \(\beta\) and then stops.

The counter is that a BJT starts from a far higher \(\gm\ro\) in the first place. Neither device wins outright, which is the honest answer and the reason both are still made.

Interactive: where the output resistance stops improving

Before you touch the sliders. You halve the bias current, which doubles \(\ro\) on both devices. What happens to the cascode's output resistance?

  1. It doubles, like \(\ro\) did
    This would be true if \(R_{out}\) were simply proportional to \(\ro\). It is not — the ceiling \(\beta\ro\) doubles too, but \(R_{out}\) is the product of a gain term and a resistance term and both move.
  2. It roughly doubles, because the ceiling \(\beta\ro\) doubled
    Right, and the reason is the interesting part. \(\gm\ro = \va/\vt\) does not change with current at all — that was the result from the small-signal lesson. So the multiplier stays put and only the resistance being multiplied grows. The ceiling \(\beta\ro\) moves in exact proportion to \(\ro\), and \(R_{out}\) tracks it. Lowering the current raises the output resistance, which is why precision current sources and op-amp gain stages run at microamps rather than milliamps.
  3. It quadruples, since both \(\ro_1\) and \(\ro_2\) doubled
    Tempting, and it would be right for a MOSFET cascode where \(R_{out} \approx \gm\ro_2\ro_1\) with no \(r_\pi\) to interfere. On a bipolar, \(r_\pi\) shunts \(\ro_1\) away and caps the whole thing at \(\beta\ro\) — so only one factor of \(\ro\) survives.
  4. It barely changes, because it is capped
    The cap is real but it is not a fixed number — it is \(\beta\ro\), and \(\ro\) just doubled. A ceiling that moves with the thing it is limiting still lets you climb.

Now drag bias current down and watch both the curve and the red ceiling line rise together. Then drag \(\beta\) up and watch the ceiling lift while the curve keeps climbing behind it — that is the MOSFET case.

The curve is \(R_{out}\) against the lower device's \(\ro_1\). Watch it bend over and flatten at \(\beta\ro_2\) — that flat is the ceiling derived above, and it is the whole difference between a bipolar cascode and a MOS one. Set \(\beta\) very high (the FET limit) and the ceiling lifts away.

4. The trap: a cascode only helps if the load lets it

The stage's gain is \(A_v = -\gm_1 (R_{out} \parallel R_L)\). The cascode raised \(R_{out}\) from \(80\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) to \(11.5\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\), but that term is in parallel with whatever you hang on the output:

LoadPlain CECascodeGained
\(R_L = 6\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) resistor \(-\gm(80\mathrm{k}\parallel 6\mathrm{k}) = -216\) \(-\gm(11.5\mathrm{M}\parallel 6\mathrm{k}) = -232\) 7%. Nothing.
\(R_L = 1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) \(-\gm(80\mathrm{k}\parallel 1\mathrm{M}) = -2865\) \(-\gm(11.5\mathrm{M}\parallel 1\mathrm{M}) = -35{,}600\) 12×
Current-source load, \(10\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) \(-3070\) \(-207{,}000\) 67×
With a resistor load, the cascode buys you bandwidth and essentially no gain. That is not a disappointment, it is the correct reading: the two wins are independent, and which one you get depends on the load. A cascode into \(6\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) is a bandwidth circuit. A cascode into a current source is a gain circuit. The op-amp input stages in the next module are the second kind, which is why they pair a cascode with an active load rather than a resistor — there is no point building one without the other.

5. What it costs: headroom

Nothing is free. Q1 needs enough \(\vce\) to stay out of saturation, and Q2's base has to sit a \(V_{BE}\) above that:

\[ V_{B2} = V_{E1} + V_{CE1(\min)} + V_{BE2} \approx V_{E1} + 0.3 + 0.7 \]
\[ V_{out(\min)} = V_{B2} - V_{BE2} + V_{CE2(\min)} = V_{E1} + 0.3 + 0.3 \] The output cannot go below roughly \(0.6\,\mathrm{V}\) above the emitter, against \(0.3\,\mathrm{V}\) for a plain CE stage.

So a cascode costs about 300 mV of output swing at the bottom. On a ±15 V supply that is invisible. On a 3.3 V rail, where you might have 2.5 V of usable swing, it is 12% of your signal range — and on a 1.8 V rail it starts to dominate the design. This is the single reason cascoding fell out of favour as supply voltages dropped, and the reason the folded cascode exists.

The folded cascode, in one paragraph

Instead of stacking Q2 on top of Q1, use a device of the opposite type and fold the signal path sideways: Q1's collector current is pulled from a current source tied to the positive rail, and a pnp common-base stage carries it down to the output. The signal still passes through a common-base current buffer and gets all the same benefits, but the two devices no longer stack in the same voltage budget — each has its own path to a rail. You pay for it in current: the folded version draws the bias for both branches rather than reusing one. That is the trade at low supply voltages, and it is why nearly every modern low-voltage op-amp input stage is folded.

Problem Set — the cascode

1Level 1

A cascode runs at \(\ic = 500\,\mathrm{\mu A}\) with \(C_\mu = 3\,\mathrm{pF}\) and \(C_\pi = 8\,\mathrm{pF}\). What is its input capacitance, and what would the same devices give in a plain common-emitter stage with \(A_v = -120\)?

Solution

Cascode: \(C_{in} = C_\pi + 2C_\mu = 8 + 6 = \mathbf{14\,pF}\).

Plain CE: \(C_{in} = 8 + 3(1 + 120) = 8 + 363 = \mathbf{371\,pF}\).

A factor of 26. Note the cascode figure does not depend on the gain at all, which is the point — you can raise the stage gain freely without paying for it in bandwidth.

2Level 1

Why does Q2 contribute no Miller multiplication of its own?

Solution

Miller requires an impedance bridging the input and the output of an inverting stage. Q2 is common-base: its input is the emitter and its output is the collector, and \(C_\mu\) sits between base and collector — that is, between AC ground and the output. It loads the output like an ordinary capacitance to ground and bridges nothing.

This is the same reason the common-base amplifier has such good frequency response, and the cascode is really just a way of getting that property without also inheriting common-base's \(26\,\Omega\) input impedance.

3Level 2

Find \(R_{out}\) for a cascode with \(\ic = 200\,\mathrm{\mu A}\), \(\va = 60\,\mathrm{V}\), \(\beta = 200\). Then confirm it against the \(\beta\ro\) ceiling.

Solution

\[ \gm = \frac{200\,\mathrm{\mu A}}{25.85\,\mathrm{mV}} = 7.74\,\mathrm{mA/V},\quad \ro = \frac{60}{200\mathrm{\mu}} = 300\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \]

\[ r_\pi = \frac{200}{7.74\times10^{-3}} = 25.8\,\mathrm{k}\Omega,\quad \ro\parallel r_\pi = 23.8\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \]

\[ \gm\ro = \frac{60}{0.02585} = 2321 \]

\[ R_{out} = 300\mathrm{k} + (2322)(23.8\mathrm{k}) = \mathbf{55.6\,M\Omega} \]

Ceiling: \(\beta\ro = 200 \times 300\,\mathrm{k} = 60\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\). The answer sits just below it, as it must — and note that lowering the current raised \(R_{out}\), because \(\ro \propto 1/\ic\) while the ceiling \(\beta\ro\) rises with it.

4Level 2

A cascode with \(R_{out} = 11.5\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) and \(\gm = 38.7\,\mathrm{mA/V}\) drives the input of the next stage, which has \(r_\pi = 3.9\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). What gain do you get, and what was the cascode worth?

Solution

\[ A_v = -\gm(R_{out}\parallel r_{\pi,\text{next}}) = -0.0387 \times (11.5\mathrm{M}\parallel 3.9\mathrm{k}) \approx -0.0387 \times 3899 = -151 \]

A plain CE stage into the same load would give \(-0.0387 \times (80\mathrm{k}\parallel 3.9\mathrm{k}) = -0.0387 \times 3719 = -144\).

The cascode was worth 5%. The next stage's \(r_\pi\) is the load, and \(3.9\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) swamps everything. This is the trap from section 4 in its most common real form, and the fix is not a better cascode — it is an emitter follower between the two stages, or a next stage with a FET input.

5Level 3

Design the bias for a cascode on a 5 V rail: \(\ic = 1\,\mathrm{mA}\), \(R_E = 200\,\Omega\), \(R_C = 2\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). Choose \(V_{B2}\) and state the output swing you end up with.

Solution

\(V_{E1} = 1\,\mathrm{mA}\times 200 = 0.2\,\mathrm{V}\), so \(V_{B1} = 0.9\,\mathrm{V}\).

Give Q1 a comfortable \(V_{CE1}\) of about 0.5 V rather than the bare 0.3 V minimum, so \(V_{C1} = 0.7\,\mathrm{V}\) and:

\[ V_{B2} = V_{C1} + V_{BE2} = 0.7 + 0.7 = \mathbf{1.4\,V} \]

Q2's emitter then sits at 0.7 V. The DC collector voltage is \(5 - 1\mathrm{mA}\times 2\mathrm{k} = 3.0\,\mathrm{V}\), and the output can swing down to \(V_{B2} - V_{BE2} + V_{CE2(\min)} = 0.7 + 0.3 = 1.0\,\mathrm{V}\) and up to nearly 5 V.

Usable swing: about \(3.0 - 1.0 = 2.0\,\mathrm{V}\) down and \(2.0\,\mathrm{V}\) up. Workable — but note that a plain CE stage here would swing to \(0.5\,\mathrm{V}\), so the cascode cost half a volt out of five. That is the headroom argument in numbers.

6Level 4

The cascode removes Miller at the input. What sets its bandwidth instead, and why is a cascode driving a high-impedance load often slower than the input-pole calculation suggests?

Solution

With the input pole pushed out to 8.8 MHz, the output pole takes over. It sits at:

\[ f_{p,out} = \frac{1}{2\pi (R_{out}\parallel R_L) C_L} \]

and the whole point of a cascode is to make \(R_{out}\parallel R_L\) enormous. With \(R_L = 1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) and only \(5\,\mathrm{pF}\) of load capacitance:

\[ f_{p,out} = \frac{1}{2\pi(1\times10^{6})(5\times10^{-12})} = 32\,\mathrm{kHz} \]

Far slower than the plain CE stage it replaced. Raising the output impedance necessarily lowers the output pole for a given load capacitance — you cannot have a high-gain node and a fast node at once, because they are the same node.

This is not a flaw in the analysis, it is the fundamental trade, and it is why a cascode gain stage is nearly always followed by a buffer: the emitter follower after the gain node exists to drive the capacitance so the gain node does not have to. In an op-amp that is exactly the job of the output stage, and it is also why a single high-impedance node is chosen as the dominant pole rather than fought — Miller compensation, from the frequency lesson, deliberately makes that node slow.

Lab — build both, and find the bandwidth by ear of the sweep

Time 70 min Skill stacking a bias, bandwidth measurement, isolating a probe artefact Parts 2× 2N3904, resistors, 10 µF and 0.1 µF caps, generator, scope

The claim to test is a strong one: the same devices, the same current, the same gain, forty times the bandwidth. Measuring it needs care, because the thing that limits the cascode is very often your scope probe rather than your circuit.

Circuits. Build the common-emitter stage from the Miller lab first: \(V_{CC} = 12\,\mathrm{V}\), \(R_C = 6\,\mathrm{k}\), \(R_E = 600\,\Omega\) bypassed, divider 47 k / 5.6 k, biased at about 1 mA, driven through \(R_S = 1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). Then convert it: insert Q2 between Q1's collector and \(R_C\), hold Q2's base at about 6 V with a 10 k / 10 k divider, and bypass that base to ground with \(0.1\,\mathrm{\mu F}\).

  1. Measure the CE stage's midband gain at 1 kHz and its −3 dB frequency. Expect roughly ×10 and a couple of hundred kHz.
  2. Convert to the cascode. Check the DC first: Q1's collector should sit about 0.7 V below Q2's base, and the output should be near mid-supply. If Q1's collector is at 0 V you have Q2's base too low and Q1 is saturated.
  3. Re-measure the midband gain. It should be within a few percent of the CE figure — that is the control for the experiment. If the gain changed a lot, something else changed too and the bandwidth comparison is not valid.
  4. Measure the cascode's −3 dB frequency.
  5. Prove you measured the amplifier and not the probe. Touch a second scope probe to the output node. If the bandwidth halves, you were measuring probe capacitance. Then deliberately add 100 pF to the output and check the corner falls by about the ratio you would predict from \(R_C \parallel \ro\).
  6. Vary \(R_S\) between \(100\,\Omega\) and \(10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) for both circuits. The CE stage's bandwidth should move nearly in inverse proportion; the cascode's should barely move at all.

What you should find. Identical midband gains, and a bandwidth ratio of tens. Step 6 is the real evidence: the CE stage's bandwidth tracks \(1/R_S\) because its dominant pole is \(R_S C_{in}\) with a large Miller-inflated \(C_{in}\); the cascode's does not, because its \(C_{in}\) is small enough that the output pole took over and the source impedance stopped mattering. A measurement that changes when it should and stops when it should is much stronger evidence than a single number.

Where it goes wrong, and why that is the interesting part. You will probably not see 40×. A 10× probe is around 10 pF, and against \(R_C \parallel \ro \approx 5.6\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) that is a pole at 2.8 MHz — below where the cascode's input pole now sits, so the probe becomes the bandwidth and the ratio you measure will be closer to 10×. That is not a failed experiment, it is problem 6 arriving on the bench: once you remove the input pole, the output node is the slow one, and a scope probe is a load capacitance like any other.

To see more of the improvement, lower \(R_C\) to \(1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) and accept the lower gain. The output pole moves up by six times and the cascode gets room to show what it can do.
