BJT as a Switch

When used as a switch, the BJT operates between cutoff (OFF) and saturation (ON). Understanding switching behavior, base drive requirements, and timing is essential for digital and power applications.

Key Insight: In saturation, VCE drops to 0.1-0.3V regardless of load current. This requires "overdriving" the base with more current than β would suggest. This is called "forced beta."

Forced Beta and Overdrive

To ensure saturation, we intentionally provide more base current than needed:

Minimum IB for saturation: IB(min) = IC / βmin

Overdrive factor (ODF): Typically 2-10×

Design IB: IB = IC / β_forced where β_forced = βmin / ODF

Switching Times

BJT switching isn't instantaneous. Understanding timing is critical for high-speed applications:

td (delay)
Time to reach 10% IC
tr (rise)
10% to 90% IC
ts (storage)
Base charge removal
tf (fall)
90% to 10% IC
Storage Time Problem: In saturation, excess carriers flood the base region. When turning off, these must recombine before the transistor turns off. This is the "storage time" - often the slowest part of switching.

Reducing Storage Time

Baker Clamp

Schottky diode from base to collector prevents deep saturation.

VCE ≈ 0.4V (higher but faster)

Speed-up Capacitor

Capacitor in parallel with RB provides current spike at turn-on/off.

C ≈ ts / RB

Reduced Overdrive

Less base current = less stored charge. Trade-off with VCE(sat).

ODF = 2-3 for speed

Schottky Transistor

Integrated Schottky (like 74LS series). Built-in anti-saturation.

ts virtually eliminated

Baker Clamp Circuit

Without clamp: VCE(sat) = 0.1-0.2V, ts = 100-500ns

With clamp: VCE = VBE - VD(Schottky) ≈ 0.7 - 0.3 = 0.4V

Transistor stays at edge of saturation. Minimal storage time!

Driving Inductive Loads

When switching off an inductor, the collapsing magnetic field creates a voltage spike that can destroy the transistor:

Voltage spike without diode: V = L × di/dt (can be hundreds of volts!)

With flyback diode: VCE limited to VCC + Vf ≈ VCC + 0.7V

Always use a flyback diode when switching relays, solenoids, motors, or any inductive load. Use a fast diode (1N4148 for small loads, 1N4007 for larger ones, or Schottky for fastest turn-off).

Trade-off: Higher VCE(sat) means more power dissipation. Darlingtons also have slower switching due to additional charge storage.

Common Switching Applications

LED Driver

PWM dimming. Low VCE(sat) important for efficiency. Current set by resistor.

Relay Driver

Inductive load - always use flyback diode. ULN2003 popular for multiple relays.

Motor Control

H-bridge for direction control. PWM for speed. Consider MOSFET for higher currents.

Level Shifting

3.3V MCU to 5V/12V logic. Open-collector output with pull-up.
