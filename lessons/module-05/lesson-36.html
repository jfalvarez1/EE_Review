Choosing a Transistor from a Datasheet

Thirty-odd lessons have explained how a bipolar transistor works. None of them have helped you pick one. That is a different skill, and it is the one you use every working day: a distributor lists tens of thousands of parts, and the datasheet is organised for the manufacturer's convenience rather than yours.

The governing principle. A transistor datasheet is a contract about limits, not a description of a device. Everything guaranteed is a “no worse than”: breakdown no lower than, saturation no higher than, current gain no less than. Design against the limits and the part you receive will work. Design against the middle and roughly half of them will not.

This lesson works one selection all the way through — a 500 mA low-side switch driven from a microcontroller pin — and the answer turns out to be that a bipolar transistor is the wrong choice. Reaching that conclusion before building it is the entire point.

1. β is the least trustworthy number in electronics

Current gain is the first parameter anyone looks up and the last one that should be relied on. Four independent things move it:

CauseSizeConsequence
Manufacturing spread 3:1 within a grade is normal. A 2N3904 is specified 100–300 at 10 mA — a factor of three, guaranteed. Any circuit whose gain depends on \(\beta\) has a 3:1 gain tolerance.
Collector current \(\beta\) is a hump. It falls at low current (recombination in the base-emitter depletion region) and collapses at high current (high-level injection). A 2N3904 guaranteed 100 min at 10 mA is guaranteed only 30 min at 100 mA. The beta you looked up is not the beta at your operating point. Read the current in the test conditions.
Temperature Rises roughly 0.5%/°C, so about +50% from 25 to 125 °C and down by a third at −40. Circuits that only just work cold, or thermally run away hot.
\(V_{CE}\) A weak rise through the Early effect. Usually negligible; mentioned so you recognise it in a curve.
The two-sided design rule

A robust bipolar circuit must work at \(\beta_{min}\) and at \(\beta = \infty\). The first is obvious. The second catches people: a base bias network sized to supply exactly the base current a typical part needs will shift its operating point badly on a high-\(\beta\) part, because the base draws less and the divider sits higher.

The standard defence is to make the divider current about ten times the expected base current, so a 3:1 change in base current moves the divider by a few percent instead of a few tens of percent. That is what the “stiff divider” rule in the biasing lesson is actually protecting against.

Graded parts are free precision. BC547A, B and C are the same die sorted into \(\beta\) bands (roughly 110–220, 200–450, 420–800) and cost the same. If you need a narrower spread, buy a grade rather than a better part. The catch is availability: distributors stock the middle grade, and a design that requires the C grade is a design with one supplier.

2. Three breakdown voltages, and which one binds

A datasheet gives several, they differ by a factor of two or more, and using the wrong one is how transistors die in the field rather than on the bench.

SymbolConditionTypical for a 40 V partWhen it applies
\(BV_{CBO}\)Emitter open60 V The highest. Rarely your case.
\(BV_{CES}\)Base shorted to emitter~50 V If you have a base-emitter resistor, you are nearer this.
\(BV_{CEO}\)Base open40 V The lowest, and the one to design against unless you can prove the base is held.
\(BV_{EBO}\)Base-emitter, reverse5–6 V Shockingly low, and routinely violated.
\(BV_{EBO}\) is the one that gets violated. Five or six volts is less than most logic swings and far less than any inductive kick. Reverse-biasing the base-emitter junction past it causes avalanche, which does not usually destroy the part outright — it degrades \(\beta\) permanently, a little more each time. So the circuit works, then works less well, then fails months later with no single event to blame. A diode across the base-emitter junction, or a series base resistor, costs a cent and prevents it.

The same logic applies to the current rating: \(I_{C,max}\) is usually set by the bond wire, not the silicon, and it is a fusing limit rather than a performance one. The number that actually stops you is almost always thermal.

3. Saturation, and the forced-β trap

This is where most switch designs go wrong, and it is a trap made entirely of test conditions.

\(V_{CE(sat)}\) is quoted at a stated \(I_C\) and a stated \(I_B\). The ratio of those two is the forced \(\beta\), and datasheets almost always use 10 — ten times more base current than a typical part needs:

\[ \beta_{forced} \equiv \frac{I_C}{I_B} \]
\[ \text{2N2222A: } V_{CE(sat)} \le 1.0\,\mathrm{V} \text{ at } I_C = 500\,\mathrm{mA},\ I_B = 50\,\mathrm{mA} \] Forced \(\beta\) of 10. Drive it with less base current and that guarantee simply does not apply.
\[ \text{saturation requires } \beta_{forced} \ll \beta_{min}\text{ at that } I_C \]
Why the number is 10 and not 2

Deep saturation costs storage time — charge sitting in the base that has to be removed before the transistor can turn off — so a forced \(\beta\) of 10 is a compromise between a low \(V_{CE(sat)}\) and a usable turn-off delay. In a switching application you may deliberately choose a higher forced \(\beta\), accepting more saturation voltage to get out of saturation faster. That is what a Baker clamp does more elegantly: a Schottky from base to collector that steals base current before the device saturates fully.

4. Worked selection: a 500 mA switch from a 3.3 V GPIO

The requirement. Switch a 500 mA, 12 V load (a fan, a relay bank, an LED string) on and off from a microcontroller pin that can source 8 mA. Ambient up to 60 °C. Through-hole is fine.

Work the constraints in order:

\[ BV_{CEO} \ge 12\,\mathrm{V} \times 2 = 24\,\mathrm{V} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{a 40 V part} \] Twice the rail is the usual habit, and more if the load is inductive — a relay coil will produce far more than 12 V on turn-off unless you catch it with a flyback diode, which you must.
\[ I_C = 500\,\mathrm{mA} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{a 800 mA part. 2N2222A fits both.} \]
\[ \beta_{forced} = \frac{500\,\mathrm{mA}}{8\,\mathrm{mA}} = 62.5 \] And here the design fails.
\[ \beta_{min}\text{ at } I_C = 500\,\mathrm{mA} \text{ is } \mathbf{40} \] Not the 100–300 quoted at 10 mA. The hump has already fallen away by half an amp, and the datasheet says so in a line most people do not read.
\[ I_{C,max} = \beta_{min}I_B = 40 \times 8\,\mathrm{mA} = 320\,\mathrm{mA} \] The worst-case part cannot pass the required current at all. It does not saturate; it sits in the active region as a current source.

Now find out what that costs. With a load sized for 500 mA at 12 V (\(R_L = 24\,\Omega\)), a part stuck at 320 mA drops:

\[ V_{CE} = 12 - (0.320)(24) = 12 - 7.68 = 4.32\,\mathrm{V} \]
\[ P_D = V_{CE}I_C = 4.32 \times 0.320 = 1.38\,\mathrm{W} \]
\[ \Delta T = P_D\,R_{\theta JA} = 1.38 \times 200\,\mathrm{°C/W} = 276\,\mathrm{°C} \] TO-92 in free air. Add the 60 °C ambient and the junction is asked to reach 336 °C against a 150 °C limit.
The answer is: do not use a BJT here

The circuit delivers 64% of the required current and destroys itself doing it, and every step of that was predictable from the datasheet in four lines of arithmetic. A typical part with \(\beta = 150\) would have passed 1.2 A worth of drive demand, saturated happily, dissipated 150 mW, and worked perfectly on your bench. This is the failure mode that ships.

A logic-level MOSFET with \(R_{DS(on)} = 50\,\mathrm{m}\Omega\) at \(V_{GS} = 3.3\,\mathrm{V}\) needs no gate current at all, and dissipates \(I^2R = 0.5^2 \times 0.05 = 12.5\,\mathrm{mW}\). It is also cheaper. The only things to check are that the threshold is genuinely specified at 3.3 V rather than 4.5 (this is what "logic level" means and many parts are not), and the gate charge if you intend to switch it fast.

If a bipolar is required — for cost, availability or radiation tolerance — the fixes are a Darlington (\(\beta\) of thousands, at the cost of a 0.9 V saturation voltage and 450 mW of dissipation), or driving the base from the 12 V rail through a resistor rather than from the GPIO, using the GPIO only to control a small pilot transistor.

5. Thermal: the rating that is a fiction, and the one that is not

“\(P_D = 625\,\mathrm{mW}\)” on the front page of a datasheet is true at \(T_A = 25\,\mathrm{°C}\) and useless everywhere else. The honest specification is the derating figure next to it, and it is just the thermal resistance inverted:

\[ T_J = T_A + P_D\,R_{\theta JA} \] The whole of thermal design, in one line. Everything else is finding an honest \(R_{\theta JA}\).
\[ P_{D,max}(T_A) = \frac{T_{J,max} - T_A}{R_{\theta JA}} \]
\[ \text{TO-92, } R_{\theta JA} = 200: \quad P_{D,max}(25°) = \frac{150-25}{200} = 625\,\mathrm{mW} \] So the front-page number was never a separate fact — it is this formula evaluated at 25 °C.
\[ P_{D,max}(60°) = \frac{150-60}{200} = 450\,\mathrm{mW} \] Nearly a third gone, at an ambient any enclosure reaches.
\(R_{\theta JA}\) is a property of your board, not of the part. The quoted figure assumes a specific test board — often JEDEC 1S0P or 2S2P, stated in a footnote — and a real design with less copper is worse, sometimes by 2×. \(R_{\theta JC}\), junction to case, is a genuine property of the package and is the number to use when you are adding your own heatsink. Add \(R_{\theta JC} + R_{\theta CS} + R_{\theta SA}\) for the full path: junction to case, case to sink through the thermal interface, sink to air.

And design to a junction temperature well below the maximum. \(T_{J,max}\) is a destruction limit; every ten degrees below it roughly doubles the part's life. Common practice is to design for \(T_J \le 110\,\mathrm{°C}\) against a 150 °C rating, leaving margin for the ambient you did not anticipate.

6. A selection checklist

In the order that eliminates parts fastest.

  1. \(BV_{CEO} \ge 2\times\) your rail, more with inductance. And check \(BV_{EBO}\) against any reverse drive.
  2. \(\beta_{min}\) at your \(I_C\), not at the headline current. Then check the circuit at \(\beta = \infty\) too.
  3. Forced \(\beta \le \beta_{min}/3\) if you need saturation. If your drive cannot supply it, you have the wrong device class and should stop here.
  4. Thermal. \(T_J = T_A + P_D R_{\theta JA}\), with \(P_D\) computed at the worst-case \(\beta\), not the typical one. Target 110 °C.
  5. Speed, only if it matters. \(f_T\) at your bias current, from the curve; \(C_{obo}\) for Miller; storage time if you are switching hard.
  6. Availability and second source. A perfect part with one supplier and a 40-week lead time is not a solution. Check that at least two manufacturers make a pin- and spec-compatible part.
  7. Package. Last, because it is usually decided by step 4 rather than by preference.
What the datasheet will not tell you: the Early voltage. \(V_A\) is almost never specified on a discrete datasheet, even though it sets the intrinsic gain \(\gm\ro = V_A/\vt\) that the small-signal lesson derived. You extract it from the output characteristics graph: take two points on one \(I_B\) curve, and \(V_A = I_C\,\Delta V_{CE}/\Delta I_C - V_{CE}\), or more simply extrapolate the curve backwards to where it crosses zero current. For a small-signal npn you will find somewhere between 60 and 150 V. If your design depends on it, measure it — nobody has guaranteed it.

Problem Set — choosing

1Level 1

A 2N3904 is quoted \(h_{FE} = 100\)–300 at \(I_C = 10\,\mathrm{mA}\) and \(h_{FE} \ge 30\) at \(I_C = 100\,\mathrm{mA}\). You need it to saturate at 80 mA. What base current do you provide?

Solution

Use the 100 mA figure, since 80 mA is far closer to it than to 10 mA: \(\beta_{min} = 30\). For saturation, forced \(\beta\) should be a third of that or less:

\[ \beta_{forced} \le 10 \;\Rightarrow\; I_B \ge \frac{80\,\mathrm{mA}}{10} = \mathbf{8\,mA} \]

Using the headline \(\beta = 100\) would have suggested 0.8 mA, and the part would have sat in the active region dissipating heat. The error is a factor of ten and it comes entirely from reading the wrong row.

2Level 1

A TO-92 part with \(R_{\theta JA} = 200\,\mathrm{°C/W}\) and \(T_{J,max} = 150\,\mathrm{°C}\) must run at 85 °C ambient. What may it dissipate, and what if you want \(T_J \le 110\,\mathrm{°C}\)?

Solution

\[ P_{max} = \frac{150 - 85}{200} = 325\,\mathrm{mW} \]

\[ P_{110} = \frac{110 - 85}{200} = \mathbf{125\,mW} \]

Designing for life rather than for survival cost 61% of the allowed dissipation. That is normally the right trade, and it is why datasheet \(P_D\) figures are so rarely achievable in a product.

3Level 2

A circuit reverse-biases a transistor's base-emitter junction to 8 V during turn-off, and \(BV_{EBO}\) is 6 V. The circuit works. Explain why this is still a defect, and give the cheapest fix.

Solution

Exceeding \(BV_{EBO}\) puts the emitter junction into avalanche. The current is small and nothing fails immediately, so the circuit works — but avalanche creates interface traps in the emitter-base depletion region, and those permanently reduce \(\beta\), cumulatively, a little on every cycle.

So the part degrades over months. \(\beta\) falls, the stage's bias shifts or its drive becomes marginal, and the product fails in the field with no single event to blame and nothing reproducible on a bench. It is one of the harder failure modes to diagnose precisely because the circuit passed every test.

Cheapest fix: a small-signal diode from base to emitter, cathode to base, so reverse drive is clamped at one diode drop. Costs a cent. A series base resistor limiting the avalanche current is a partial fix and does not stop the degradation, only slows it.

4Level 2

You need a switch for a 24 V relay coil drawing 150 mA, driven from a 5 V logic output that can supply 20 mA. Work the checklist and pick a device class.

Solution

Breakdown: a relay coil is inductive. Without a flyback diode the turn-off spike is unbounded; with one it is clamped to about \(V_{CC} + 0.7\). So: fit the diode, then \(BV_{CEO} \ge 48\,\mathrm{V}\) gives comfortable margin. A 60 V or 80 V part.

Forced \(\beta\): \(150/20 = 7.5\). Comfortably under 10, so a bipolar works here — provided \(\beta_{min}\) at 150 mA is at least about 25, which most 500 mA parts meet.

Thermal: saturated at \(V_{CE(sat)} \approx 0.3\,\mathrm{V}\), \(P_D = 0.3 \times 0.150 = 45\,\mathrm{mW}\). Trivial in any package.

Verdict: unlike the worked example, a bipolar is the right answer, because the current is lower and the drive is stronger — the forced \(\beta\) landed on the right side of 10. Note that the decision turned on a single ratio, and that the flyback diode is not optional.

5Level 3

An amplifier stage is biased with a divider drawing 10× the expected base current, using a part with \(\beta\) guaranteed 100–300. Estimate how much the emitter current shifts across the \(\beta\) range, and compare with a divider drawing only 1×.

Solution

Let the divider's Thevenin resistance be \(R_B\) and its open-circuit voltage \(V_B\). Base current loads it, so the actual base voltage is \(V_B - I_B R_B\), and \(I_B = I_E/(\beta+1)\).

With the divider at 10× the base current, \(R_B\) is such that \(I_B R_B \approx V_B/10\) at \(\beta = 100\). Going to \(\beta = 300\) cuts \(I_B\) by a factor of three, so the loading term falls from \(V_B/10\) to \(V_B/30\) — the base voltage rises by about \(V_B \times (1/10 - 1/30) = 0.067\,V_B\), roughly 7%. With an emitter resistor setting the current, the emitter current shifts by rather less than that, since \(V_{BE}\) absorbs part of it.

With a 1× divider, the loading term is \(V_B\) itself at \(\beta = 100\) falling to \(V_B/3\) — a swing of order \(0.67\,V_B\), which is not a bias network at all, it is a bias lottery.

This is the quantitative version of the “stiff divider” rule: the sensitivity to \(\beta\) is roughly the ratio of base current to divider current, and 10× buys you a 10× reduction in it. Going to 100× buys another factor of ten and costs supply current and input impedance, which is why 10 is the usual compromise.

6Level 4

A design uses a bipolar in the linear region as a current sink, dropping 20 V at 200 mA in a TO-220 with a heatsink giving \(R_{\theta JA} = 10\,\mathrm{°C/W}\) total. Ambient 50 °C. The thermal numbers pass. Explain why the design may still fail, and what chart you must check.

Solution

Thermally: \(P_D = 20 \times 0.2 = 4\,\mathrm{W}\), \(T_J = 50 + 4 \times 10 = 90\,\mathrm{°C}\). Comfortable.

But average thermal resistance assumes the die is isothermal, and in linear operation it is not. At high \(V_{CE}\) the current crowds into whichever region of the die is hottest, because \(V_{BE}\) falls with temperature so the hot region takes more current, which makes it hotter still. That positive feedback is second breakdown, and it destroys the die locally while the case temperature reads perfectly normal.

The chart to check is the Safe Operating Area. At high \(V_{CE}\) the SOA boundary falls below the constant-power hyperbola, and that extra slope is the second-breakdown limit. A part rated for tens of watts may permit only a couple at 20 V DC.

The same trap is worse on MOSFETs than people expect. Modern trench and superjunction devices have a strongly negative temperature coefficient of threshold at low currents, so they too crowd, and their linear-mode SOA can be a tiny fraction of their switching rating. If you are using a MOSFET in linear mode — a hot-swap controller, a load bank, a soft-start — you must select on the SOA chart at your pulse width, and older planar devices are frequently the right choice over newer, better-looking ones.

Lab — measure β where the datasheet does not

Time 60 min Skill curve tracing by hand; spread measurement; saturation testing Parts 5× 2N3904, resistors, two supplies, DMM, thermometer or hair dryer

The datasheet gives \(\beta\) at two currents. You are about to need it at a third. This measures the whole hump, across five parts, and then checks the forced-\(\beta\) rule by deliberately breaking it.

Circuit. Base fed from a supply through \(R_B\), collector from 10 V through \(R_C = 100\,\Omega\). Measure \(I_B\) as the drop across \(R_B\), and \(I_C\) as the drop across \(R_C\). \(\beta = I_C/I_B\).

  1. For one part, set \(I_C\) to 100 µA, 1 mA, 10 mA, 50 mA and 100 mA by adjusting \(R_B\). At each point record \(I_B\) and compute \(\beta\).
  2. Plot \(\beta\) against \(\log I_C\). You should see the hump: rising, a broad peak, then falling.
  3. Mark the two datasheet points on your plot. Are they where you measured?
  4. Repeat at \(I_C = 10\,\mathrm{mA}\) for all five parts. Compute the spread as \(\beta_{max}/\beta_{min}\) across your sample.
  5. Warm one part to about 60 °C and re-measure at 10 mA. Compute the percentage change per °C.
  6. The forced-\(\beta\) test. Set \(I_C\) to 50 mA by choosing \(R_C\) and the supply, then drive the base at forced \(\beta\) of 50, 20, 10 and 5. Measure \(V_{CE}\) at each. Plot \(V_{CE}\) against forced \(\beta\).

What you should find. A hump peaking somewhere around 10–30 mA, with \(\beta\) perhaps half its peak value at 100 µA and again at 100 mA. Your five parts at 10 mA will cluster far more tightly than 100–300 — probably within 20% of each other — because they came from one reel, one wafer, possibly one row. That tight cluster is a trap: it tells you about your reel, not about the part number, and the next reel can sit anywhere else inside the guaranteed band. It is the same lesson as the op-amp offset lab, and it is worth meeting twice.

In step 6, \(V_{CE}\) should fall sharply as forced \(\beta\) drops below about 20 and then flatten — more base current past that point buys almost nothing except storage time.

Where it goes wrong, and why that is the interesting part. At \(I_C = 100\,\mathrm{mA}\) the part is dissipating real power and will self-heat while you take the reading. \(\beta\) rises with temperature, so the reading climbs as you watch, and where you stop determines your answer. There is no correct stopping point: this is a genuinely ambiguous measurement, which is exactly why datasheets specify \(h_{FE}\) with pulsed test conditions — typically a 300 µs pulse at under 2% duty — so the die has no time to warm. Look for that footnote on the datasheet. Your DC measurement and their pulsed one are measuring different things, and yours is the one that matches how your circuit actually runs.
