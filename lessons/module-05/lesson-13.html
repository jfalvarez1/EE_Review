Active Loads

The cascode lesson ended on a warning: raising a stage's output resistance buys you nothing unless the load is high impedance too. A cascode into \(6\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) is worth 7%. This lesson builds the load that makes it worth 67×, and in doing so removes the ceiling that has limited every amplifier in this module so far.

The idea in one sentence. A resistor's AC resistance and its DC voltage drop are the same number. A current source's are not. Replace the load resistor with a current source and you can have a megohm of small-signal resistance while dropping a fraction of a volt.

1. Why a resistor load caps your gain at the supply voltage

From the small-signal lesson, a common-emitter stage's gain has a form that hides nothing:

\[ A_v = -\gm R_C = -\frac{\ic}{\vt}R_C = -\frac{\ic R_C}{\vt} = -\frac{V_{R_C}}{\vt} \] The gain is the DC voltage dropped across the collector resistor, divided by 26 mV.
\[ V_{R_C} < V_{CC} \;\Rightarrow\; |A_v| < \frac{V_{CC}}{\vt} \]
\[ V_{CC} = 10\,\mathrm{V} \;\Rightarrow\; |A_v| < 387 \] And that is the absolute limit, reached only by dropping the entire supply across \(R_C\) and leaving the transistor no room to exist. Leave half the supply for headroom and swing and you are at 193.

That is a hard wall and it has nothing to do with the transistor. A better device does not help. More current does not help — raising \(\ic\) forces \(R_C\) down to keep the same drop, and the product is unchanged. The gain of a resistively loaded stage is set by your power supply.

You want more gain from a resistively loaded common-emitter stage. Which of these actually gets you there?

  1. Raise the bias current
    \(\gm\) rises in proportion, but to keep the same DC collector voltage you must lower \(R_C\) in the same proportion. Gain is \(V_{R_C}/\vt\) and \(V_{R_C}\) did not change, so neither did the gain.
  2. Buy a transistor with a higher \(\beta\)
    \(\beta\) does not appear anywhere in \(A_v = -\gm R_C\). It sets the input resistance \(r_\pi\), not the gain. This is one of the most common misconceptions about bipolar amplifiers.
  3. Raise the supply voltage
    Right — and that is the depressing part. The only lever on \(|A_v| = V_{R_C}/\vt\) is the voltage you can afford to drop, which is bounded by the supply. Doubling \(V_{CC}\) doubles the gain. It is also why valve amplifiers running at 300 V got respectable gain from mediocre devices, and why a 1.8 V chip cannot.
  4. Cool the circuit, since \(\vt\) falls with temperature
    Genuinely true and genuinely useless: \(\vt \propto T\), so cooling from 300 K to 250 K raises the gain by 20%. Nobody refrigerates an amplifier for 20%, and it is not a design lever — but it is worth recognising that the gain of every bipolar stage drifts with temperature for exactly this reason.

The escape is not a bigger supply. It is to stop making the load a resistor.

2. What a current source looks like to a signal

An ideal current source passes a fixed current regardless of the voltage across it. Its \(I\)–\(V\) curve is horizontal, so its slope resistance \(\partial V/\partial I\) is infinite — while the voltage across it can be anything at all, including very small.

A real one is a transistor, and its slope resistance is exactly the \(\ro\) we already know:

\[ \ro = \frac{\va}{\ic} = \frac{50\,\mathrm{V}}{1\,\mathrm{mA}} = 50\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \] Using \(\va = 50\,\mathrm{V}\), typical of the lateral pnp that will do the job. Its Early voltage is worse than an npn's; that turns out to matter.
\[ V_{\text{drop}} = V_{EC(\text{sat})} + \text{margin} \approx 0.3\text{ to }1\,\mathrm{V} \] And here is the whole trick. To get \(50\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) from a resistor at 1 mA you would drop 50 volts. The current source gives the same slope resistance for well under one.
The gain that results

The stage now sees its own \(\ro\) in parallel with the load's:

\[ A_v = -\gm\,(\ro_n \parallel \ro_p) = -0.03868 \times (80\mathrm{k} \parallel 50\mathrm{k}) = -0.03868 \times 30.8\mathrm{k} \]

\[ A_v = \mathbf{-1190} \]

Against \(-182\) for the same transistor at the same current with a \(5\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) resistor and half the supply spent on it. A factor of six and a half, and — the part that matters — it no longer depends on the supply voltage at all. Run this stage from 3 V and the gain is the same.

If both devices had the same Early voltage the expression collapses to something worth memorising:

\[ A_v = -\gm\frac{\ro}{2} = -\frac{\va}{2\vt} \approx -1550 \quad (\va = 80\,\mathrm{V}) \]

Half the intrinsic gain of a single device. You cannot do better than \(\gm\ro\) with one transistor, and an active load gets you within a factor of two of it — which is why every integrated gain stage is built this way and essentially none use a resistor.

3. The circuit

Q1 is the amplifier. Q2 and Q3 are a pnp current mirror: Q2 is diode-connected and sets the current from \(I_{REF}\), Q3 copies it and delivers it to Q1's collector as the load. Note what is not here — there is no collector resistor, and the output node sits near mid-supply while dropping only \(V_{EC}\) across the load device.

The mirror is doing two jobs at once, and it is worth separating them:

JobWhich property does it
Set the DC operating current The mirror ratio: \(I_{C3} = I_{REF}\) if the devices match
Present a high AC load \(\ro_3 = \va/\ic\), which has nothing to do with the ratio

Those are independent, which is exactly what a resistor cannot offer. With a resistor, choosing the DC current chooses the AC resistance and you get no say.

4. The problem you have just created

The output node is now a megohm-class node with two current sources fighting over it — Q1 pulling down, Q3 pushing up. If those currents are not exactly equal, the difference has nowhere to go but into the node's own resistance:

\[ \Delta V_{out} = \Delta I \cdot (\ro_n \parallel \ro_p) \]
\[ \Delta I = 1\% \text{ of } 1\,\mathrm{mA} = 10\,\mathrm{\mu A} \]
\[ \Delta V_{out} = 10\,\mathrm{\mu A} \times 30.8\,\mathrm{k}\Omega = 0.31\,\mathrm{V} \] Tolerable. Now make the mismatch 5%, which is realistic for a simple mirror once base currents and \(V_{EC}\) differences are counted: 1.54 V, on a node that only has a few volts of room.
Put a cascode on it and open-loop operation becomes impossible. With \(R_{out}\) at \(11.5\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) from the cascode lesson, that same \(10\,\mathrm{\mu A}\) of mismatch asks for 57 volts of output shift. The node simply slams into whichever rail the imbalance points at, and sits there.

This is not a flaw to be engineered around. It is the reason every high-impedance gain node in real analog design lives inside a feedback loop: the loop adjusts the input by whatever tiny amount is needed to force the two currents equal. An op-amp's enormous open-loop gain and its uselessness open-loop are the same fact seen from two sides.

You build this stage open-loop on a bench. The output sits hard at \(0.2\,\mathrm{V}\) — Q1 is saturated — no matter what you do to the input within a few millivolts. Everything is soldered correctly. What is happening?

  1. Q3 has failed open, so nothing is pulling the output up
    Possible in principle, but then the output would be at ground rather than at \(V_{CE(\mathrm{sat})}\), and no input would do anything at all. The symptom described — sensitive to input, but only over a few millivolts — says the circuit is working.
  2. Nothing is wrong. The gain is 1200 and there is no feedback, so a few millivolts of input offset saturates it
    Right. With \(|A_v| \approx 1200\) and a supply of a few volts, the input window that keeps the output in range is a couple of millivolts wide. Your signal generator's DC offset, or the mismatch between Q1's current and the mirror's, is larger than that. The stage is behaving exactly as designed and the design is unusable open-loop. Close a loop around it and it becomes an op-amp.
  3. \(I_{REF}\) is set too low, starving the output
    A low \(I_{REF}\) would reduce the mirror's current and push the output up toward the positive rail as Q1 wins the tug of war, not down into saturation.
  4. The pnp devices are mismatched, so the mirror ratio is wrong
    This is a real effect and it contributes — but it is a cause of the offset, not a fault. Even a perfectly matched mirror would saturate here, because the gain multiplies any residual offset, including the input source's own.

The bench technique that makes this measurable: close a loop with a large resistor from output back to input to set the DC point, and inject the signal through a capacitor. You then measure the AC gain of a stage whose DC is stabilised — which is what the lab at the end does.

5. Where you have already seen this

An active load is not an exotic technique. It is the standard input stage of every op-amp you have used, where it does a third job as well:

UseWhat the mirror adds
Single common-emitter gain stage Gain of \(\va/2\vt\) instead of \(V_{R_C}/\vt\)
Differential pair load Gain, and differential-to-single-ended conversion. The mirror reflects the current from one side and adds it to the other, so both halves of the signal reach the output instead of one — worth a factor of two on top of everything else.
Op-amp second stage The high-impedance node that Miller compensation deliberately makes slow, from the frequency lesson
Cascoded gain stage \(\gm\ro^2\)-class resistance, usable only inside a loop
The three lessons join up here

The small-signal lesson said one transistor cannot beat \(\gm\ro\). The cascode lesson raised the source resistance toward that limit and warned that the load would waste it. This lesson raises the load. Put all three together — a cascoded stage with a cascoded active load, inside a feedback loop — and you have described the input stage of essentially every precision op-amp made since 1970.

Problem Set — active loads

1Level 1

A stage runs at \(\ic = 200\,\mathrm{\mu A}\) with an npn (\(\va = 100\,\mathrm{V}\)) loaded by a pnp mirror (\(\va = 40\,\mathrm{V}\)). Find the voltage gain.

Solution

\[ \gm = \frac{200\times10^{-6}}{0.02585} = 7.74\,\mathrm{mA/V} \]

\[ \ro_n = \frac{100}{200\mathrm{\mu}} = 500\,\mathrm{k}\Omega,\quad \ro_p = \frac{40}{200\mathrm{\mu}} = 200\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \]

\[ A_v = -7.74\mathrm{m} \times (500\mathrm{k} \parallel 200\mathrm{k}) = -7.74\mathrm{m} \times 143\mathrm{k} = \mathbf{-1106} \]

Note the pnp's poorer \(\va\) dominates the parallel combination. The load is usually the limit, not the amplifier.

2Level 1

What resistor would give the same \(143\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) of load resistance at \(200\,\mathrm{\mu A}\), and what would it cost in supply voltage?

Solution

\[ V = IR = 200\times10^{-6} \times 143\times10^{3} = \mathbf{28.6\,V} \]

You would need a 30 V supply to do with a resistor what the mirror does with less than one volt across it. That single comparison is the entire argument for active loads, and it gets worse as the current falls.

3Level 2

Show that the gain of an actively loaded stage is independent of bias current, and say what you do lose by running it at a lower current.

Solution

\[ A_v = -\gm(\ro_n \parallel \ro_p) = -\frac{\ic}{\vt}\cdot\frac{1}{\frac{\ic}{\va_n} + \frac{\ic}{\va_p}} = -\frac{1}{\vt}\cdot\frac{\va_n\va_p}{\va_n + \va_p} \]

\(\ic\) cancels completely: the gain depends only on the two Early voltages and \(\vt\).

What you lose: bandwidth and drive. \(\gm\) falls with current, so the stage's ability to charge the next stage's capacitance falls with it, and the output pole \(1/2\pi R_{out}C_L\) moves down as \(R_{out}\) rises. You also lose noise performance. The gain is free; the speed is not.

4Level 2

A differential pair with an active-load mirror is said to have twice the gain of the same pair with two resistor loads taken single-ended. Where does the factor of two come from?

Solution

With two resistors and a single-ended output you use one side and discard the other: half the signal current reaches the output.

The mirror takes the current from the left half, reflects it, and pushes it into the same node the right half is pulling. The two halves are in antiphase, so reflecting one makes them add rather than cancel:

\[ i_{out} = \gm\frac{v_{id}}{2} + \gm\frac{v_{id}}{2} = \gm v_{id} \]

So the effective transconductance of the whole pair is \(\gm\), not \(\gm/2\). The mirror recovered the half you were throwing away, and the conversion from differential to single-ended came free with it.

5Level 3

Your actively loaded stage has \(|A_v| = 1200\) on a 5 V supply, and you want the output to rest at 2.5 V. How accurately must the mirror current match the stage current, expressed as a percentage?

Solution

To keep the output within, say, \(\pm 1\,\mathrm{V}\) of centre with \(R_{out} = 30.8\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\):

\[ \Delta I \le \frac{1\,\mathrm{V}}{30.8\,\mathrm{k}\Omega} = 32.5\,\mathrm{\mu A} \]

\[ \frac{32.5\,\mathrm{\mu A}}{1\,\mathrm{mA}} = \mathbf{3.3\%} \]

Which sounds achievable until you remember what sets it: \(\vbe\) mismatch between the mirror pair (a 1 mV mismatch is already 3.9%), base current error (\(2/\beta\), about 1.3%), and the Early effect from the two devices sitting at different \(V_{CE}\). Getting to 3% open-loop needs a matched pair and care; getting to 0.1% needs feedback. This is the calculation that explains why open-loop precision analog does not exist.

6Level 4

A colleague proposes improving the stage by cascoding both the amplifier and the load, reaching \(R_{out} \approx 5\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\). The gain becomes about 190,000. What breaks, and what would you actually do?

Solution

Three things break, in order of severity.

1. DC operating point. \(10\,\mathrm{\mu A}\) of mismatch now asks for 50 V of output shift. The node is at a rail before you begin. Only a feedback loop can hold it, and the loop must have enough gain to correct the offset — which it does, because the offset and the gain come from the same place.

2. Bandwidth. The output pole is \(1/2\pi R_{out}C_L\). At \(5\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) with just \(2\,\mathrm{pF}\) of load that is \(16\,\mathrm{kHz}\). The stage has enormous gain and almost no bandwidth — and their product, \(190{,}000 \times 16\,\mathrm{kHz} = 3\,\mathrm{GHz}\), is unchanged, because \(\gm/2\pi C_L\) is what it always was. Raising output resistance moves gain and bandwidth around; it never creates gain-bandwidth.

3. Headroom. Two cascodes cost roughly 600 mV of the available swing, which on a low supply is most of what you had.

What you would actually do: exactly this, but inside a loop, and follow it with a buffer so the huge \(R_{out}\) never has to drive a real load. That is a two-stage op-amp: a cascoded, actively loaded gain node whose only job is gain, followed by an output stage whose only job is current. Neither could work alone, which is why op-amps have both.

Lab — measure a gain too large to measure directly

Time 70 min Skill closing a DC loop to make an open-loop measurement possible Parts 2N3904, 2× 2N3906 (or a BC857BS dual pnp), resistors, 10 µF caps, generator, scope

You cannot put a signal into a gain of 1200 on a 10 V supply and expect the output to stay on the screen — 8 mV of input covers the whole rail. The technique for measuring a large gain is to stabilise the DC without stabilising the AC, and it is worth having in your hands.

Circuit. Q1 npn common-emitter, emitter to ground through \(100\,\Omega\). Collector to the collector of Q3, a 2N3906. Q3's emitter to \(+10\,\mathrm{V}\); its base tied to the base and collector of Q2, a second 2N3906 whose emitter also goes to +10 V and whose collector goes to ground through \(9.3\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) to set \(I_{REF} \approx 1\,\mathrm{mA}\).

The DC loop. Connect a \(1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) resistor from the output node back to Q1's base, and inject the signal into the base through \(10\,\mathrm{\mu F}\) from a \(1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) source. At DC the \(1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) forces the output to sit roughly a \(\vbe\) above ground; at 1 kHz the capacitor dominates and the loop is open.

  1. Wire it without the input capacitor first and confirm the DC: Q1's collector should settle somewhere in the middle rather than at a rail. If it sits at a rail, the feedback resistor is not connected.
  2. Add the input coupling. Apply 1 kHz at the smallest amplitude your generator will produce — you want microvolts at the base, so use a divider: \(10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) into \(10\,\Omega\) gives you a thousandth of the generator's output.
  3. Increase the input until the output is a clean 2 V peak-to-peak. Measure the voltage across the \(10\,\Omega\) — that is your real input. Gain is the ratio.
  4. Now swap the mirror for a plain \(5\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) resistor from the collector to +10 V and repeat. Same transistor, same current, same everything else.
  5. Compare. Then compute \(\va\) for your pnp from the measured active-load gain: \(\va_p\) follows from \(A_v = -\gm(\ro_n \parallel \ro_p)\) once you know \(\gm\) and can estimate \(\ro_n\).

What you should find. Several hundred to about a thousand with the mirror, and roughly 180 with the resistor. You will almost certainly measure less than the predicted 1190, and the reason is worth chasing rather than shrugging at: your scope probe is 10 pF, and against \(30\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) that is a pole at 530 kHz — not the problem at 1 kHz. The real culprits are the \(1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) feedback resistor still loading the output a little at 1 kHz, and the next stage (your probe's 10 MΩ) in parallel with 30 kΩ, which costs 0.3%.

Where it goes wrong, and why that is the interesting part. Two separate 2N3906s will mismatch by 5–20 mV of \(\vbe\), which is 20–100% of current error — so your mirror may deliver 1.5 mA when you asked for 1 mA, and the output will rest well off centre. That is the matched-pair argument from the current mirror lesson arriving with consequences. Using a dual pnp in one package (BC857BS) will visibly improve where the output rests, and is the cheapest lesson in this lab.

If the output will not stay put at all, lower the feedback resistor to \(100\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). You will lose some measured gain to the loading — compute how much, rather than guessing.
