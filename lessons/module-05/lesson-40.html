Interview Prep — BJTs

Bipolar questions are where analog interviews get serious. Op-amp questions can be answered from two rules; these cannot. You are expected to carry a small-signal model in your head, put numbers to it without a calculator, and know which approximations you just made.

The four numbers you must have memorised. Everything below is built from them, and being slow on any one of them costs you the whole question.
Thermal voltage
VT = 26 mV at room temperature
Transconductance
gm = IC/VT = 38.5 mA/V at 1 mA
Emitter resistance
re = VT/IE = 26 Ω at 1 mA
VBE tempco
−2 mV/°C at fixed current

The move that answers most of these

Almost every bipolar amplifier question reduces to one sentence: gain is the ratio of the impedance at the collector to the impedance at the emitter.

\[ A_v = -\frac{R_C}{r_e + R_E} \]

With the emitter fully bypassed \(R_E = 0\) and this becomes \(-R_C/r_e = -g_mR_C\). With degeneration it becomes \(-R_C/R_E\) and stops depending on the transistor at all — which is the entire reason anyone degenerates. Say that sentence early and most of the follow-ups answer themselves.

Part 1 — Derive it, no formula sheet

1 Derive Warm-up

Where does \(g_m = I_C/V_T\) come from? Derive it.

How to answer
\[ I_C = I_S\,e^{V_{BE}/V_T} \] Start from the physics, not from the model. The exponential is the only thing you have to assume.
\[ g_m \equiv \frac{\partial I_C}{\partial V_{BE}} = \frac{I_S}{V_T}e^{V_{BE}/V_T} \]
\[ = \frac{I_C}{V_T} \] The exponential reappears as \(I_C\) itself. Transconductance depends only on the collector current — not on geometry, not on the part number, not on \(\beta\).

"Which is a remarkable result: every bipolar transistor in the world at 1 mA has a \(g_m\) of 38.5 mA per volt. It is set by physics, not by the manufacturer. That is why bipolar gain stages are predictable in a way MOSFET ones are not, where \(g_m = \sqrt{2\mu C_{ox}(W/L)I_D}\) and you need to know the process and the geometry."

What they are testing: whether you know that \(g_m\) is a derivative of the exponential and not a datasheet parameter. Volunteering the MOSFET comparison unprompted is the strongest possible answer to this question, because it shows you know what the property is worth.
2 Derive Standard

Common-emitter stage, \(I_C = 1\,\mathrm{mA}\), \(R_C = 5\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), emitter bypassed. Find the gain. Now remove the bypass capacitor with \(R_E = 470\,\Omega\). Find it again, and tell me why anyone would do that.

How to answer
\[ g_m = \frac{1\,\mathrm{mA}}{26\,\mathrm{mV}} = 38.5\,\mathrm{mA/V}, \qquad r_e = 26\,\Omega \]
\[ A_v = -g_mR_C = -(0.0385)(5000) = -192 \] Or equivalently \(-R_C/r_e = -5000/26 = -192\). Same thing, and the second form is faster in your head.
\[ A_v = -\frac{R_C}{r_e + R_E} = -\frac{5000}{26+470} = -10.1 \] A factor of nineteen thrown away.

"You trade gain for everything else. The undegenerated gain of 192 depends on \(r_e\), which depends on \(I_C\), which drifts with temperature and varies part to part — so that 192 might be 150 or 250 on the bench. With 470 ohms of degeneration the gain is \(-R_C/R_E \approx -10.6\) to first order, set by two resistors, and it is stable, linear and repeatable. It also raises the input impedance from \(r_\pi\) to \(r_\pi + (\beta+1)R_E\) and improves the bandwidth by reducing the Miller multiplication."

What they are testing: the arithmetic takes ten seconds. The question is whether you can articulate the trade — four separate benefits bought with one resistor. This is the most common BJT interview question there is.
3 Derive Standard

Emitter follower. Derive the voltage gain and the output impedance. Why use a stage whose gain is less than one?

How to answer
\[ A_v = \frac{R_E}{r_e + R_E} \approx 1 \quad\text{when } R_E \gg r_e \]
\[ R_{out} = r_e + \frac{R_S}{\beta+1} \] The source impedance is divided by \(\beta+1\) looking in at the emitter. That division is the whole point of the stage.
\[ R_{in} = r_\pi + (\beta+1)(R_E \| R_L) \] And the load impedance is multiplied by \(\beta+1\) looking in at the base. Same factor, opposite direction.
\[ R_S = 10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega,\ \beta = 100,\ I_C = 1\,\mathrm{mA}: \quad R_{out} = 26 + \frac{10000}{101} = 125\,\Omega \]

"It is a current amplifier, not a voltage one. A 10 k source that could not drive a 100 ohm load at all now drives it through a 125 ohm output impedance. Gain of one in voltage, gain of \(\beta+1\) in current, and that is what you came for."

What they are testing: whether you can state the impedance transformation in both directions. The follow-up is often "so why not always use one?" — and the answers are the \(V_{BE}\) drop it costs you in headroom, the fact that it can oscillate (question 12), and that it does not fix the DC level.
4 Derive Harder

Derive the Miller effect. Then: a common-emitter stage with \(A_v = -100\) and \(C_\mu = 4\,\mathrm{pF}\) is driven from 1 kΩ. What is the bandwidth?

How to answer
\[ I_{in} = \frac{V_{in} - V_{out}}{Z_\mu} = \frac{V_{in}(1 - A_v)}{Z_\mu} \] The far end of the capacitor is not still — it is moving in the opposite direction and \(|A_v|\) times as far, so far more current flows than the capacitance alone suggests.
\[ Z_{in,\,\text{eff}} = \frac{V_{in}}{I_{in}} = \frac{Z_\mu}{1-A_v} \;\Longrightarrow\; C_{in} = C_\mu(1 + |A_v|) \]
\[ C_{in} = 4\,\mathrm{pF}\times 101 = 404\,\mathrm{pF} \] Four picofarads became four hundred. This is why a high-gain common-emitter stage is slow.
\[ f_{-3\mathrm{dB}} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_S C_{in}} = \frac{1}{2\pi(1000)(404\times10^{-12})} = 394\,\mathrm{kHz} \]

"And the fix is the cascode: put a common-base stage on top so the collector of the input transistor barely moves. \(A_v\) at that node drops to about \(-1\), the Miller multiplier becomes 2 instead of 101, and the bandwidth goes up by roughly fifty times for the cost of one transistor and some headroom."

What they are testing: Miller is the single most-asked bipolar concept after \(g_m\), because it explains why textbook gain stages are slow and it motivates half the topologies in this module. Being able to say "and that is what the cascode is for" is what they are waiting to hear.
5 Derive Harder

A basic two-transistor current mirror. Derive the error between \(I_{out}\) and \(I_{ref}\) caused by base currents. Then tell me the two other error sources.

How to answer
\[ I_{ref} = I_{C} + 2I_B = I_C + \frac{2I_C}{\beta} \] Both bases are fed from the reference branch, so the reference current carries two base currents that never reach the output.
\[ \frac{I_{out}}{I_{ref}} = \frac{1}{1 + 2/\beta} \]
\[ \beta = 100: \quad \frac{1}{1.02} = 0.980 \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{2% low} \]

The other two, in order of how much trouble they cause:

  • \(V_{BE}\) mismatch. The relationship is exponential, so \(I_{out}/I_{ref} = e^{\Delta V_{BE}/V_T}\). A mismatch of just 1 mV — trivial for discrete parts — gives \(e^{1/26} = 1.039\), a 4% error. Two discrete transistors from the same reel can easily differ by 5 mV, which is 20%.
  • Early effect. The output transistor's collector is at a different voltage from the diode-connected one, and \(I_C\) rises with \(V_{CE}\) as \(1 + V_{CE}/V_A\). With \(V_A = 100\,\mathrm{V}\) and 10 V of difference, that is another 10%.

"So base current is the smallest of the three and the one everyone quotes. The fixes stack: a beta-helper transistor divides the base-current error by another \(\beta\); emitter degeneration of a few hundred millivolts turns the exponential mismatch into a linear one and is the standard answer for discretes; and a cascode holds \(V_{CE}\) constant to kill the Early effect."

What they are testing: whether you rank the errors correctly. Most candidates derive the \(2/\beta\) term and stop, which is the smallest one. Naming \(V_{BE}\) mismatch as dominant for discrete parts, with the exponential sensitivity, is what a designer says.
6 Derive Harder

Differential pair with tail current \(I_{EE}\). Derive the differential gain. What is the maximum differential input before it stops being linear?

How to answer
\[ I_{C1} = \frac{I_{EE}}{1 + e^{-V_{id}/V_T}}, \qquad I_{C2} = \frac{I_{EE}}{1 + e^{+V_{id}/V_T}} \] The tail current splits according to the ratio of two exponentials.
\[ I_{C1} - I_{C2} = I_{EE}\tanh\!\left(\frac{V_{id}}{2V_T}\right) \] The tanh is worth knowing by name — it is why a diff pair is a soft limiter and why it is used as a multiplier core.
\[ \text{For } V_{id} \ll 2V_T: \quad A_v = -g_mR_C, \quad g_m = \frac{I_{EE}}{2V_T} \] Each side carries \(I_{EE}/2\), so \(g_m\) per device is \(I_{EE}/2V_T\).
\[ \text{Linear to about } \pm V_T = \pm 26\,\mathrm{mV}; \text{ fully switched by } \pm 4V_T \approx \pm 100\,\mathrm{mV} \]

"About 26 millivolts of differential input and you are already at a percent or so of distortion. That tiny linear range is why every practical diff pair is either degenerated — which trades gain for range one-for-one — or wrapped in feedback. And it is also why the same circuit makes an excellent limiter and an excellent current-steering switch."

What they are testing: the number \(\pm26\,\mathrm{mV}\). Candidates who have only seen the small-signal model often believe a diff pair is linear over volts. Knowing it is tens of millivolts, and that degeneration is the price you pay to widen it, is the practical content.
7 Derive Nasty

Design a bias network for \(I_C = 1\,\mathrm{mA}\) from a 12 V supply that holds current stable as \(\beta\) varies from 50 to 300 and temperature from 0 to 70 °C. Then justify every choice.

How to answer

Voltage divider bias with emitter degeneration, and the whole design is three rules of thumb.

\[ V_E \approx 1\,\mathrm{V} \quad (\approx V_{CC}/10) \] Big enough that the −2 mV/°C drift of \(V_{BE}\) is small against it. Over 70 °C, \(V_{BE}\) moves 140 mV; against 1 V that is a 14% current shift, against 100 mV it would be 140%.
\[ R_E = \frac{V_E}{I_E} = \frac{1\,\mathrm{V}}{1\,\mathrm{mA}} = 1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \]
\[ V_B = V_E + V_{BE} = 1.7\,\mathrm{V} \]
\[ I_{divider} \approx 10 I_B = 10\cdot\frac{1\,\mathrm{mA}}{50} = 200\,\mu\mathrm{A} \] Ten times the WORST-CASE base current, which means the lowest \(\beta\). Size the divider stiff against the worst case and the best case looks after itself.
\[ R_2 = \frac{1.7}{200\mu} = 8.5\,\mathrm{k}\Omega, \qquad R_1 = \frac{12-1.7}{200\mu} = 51.5\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \] Nearest E24 values: 8.2 k and 51 k.
\[ R_C: \ V_C = V_{CC}/2 \approx 6\,\mathrm{V} \Rightarrow R_C = \frac{12-6}{1\,\mathrm{mA}} = 6\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \to 5.6\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \] Collector at mid-supply for maximum symmetrical swing.

"The emitter resistor is doing the stabilising: it is local negative feedback on the DC operating point. If \(I_C\) rises for any reason, \(V_E\) rises, \(V_{BE}\) falls because the base is held by a stiff divider, and \(I_C\) comes back down. Beta and temperature both drop out of the answer. The cost is one volt of headroom and, if you leave it unbypassed, most of your gain."

What they are testing: whether you can design rather than analyse, and whether you use worst case rather than typical. The trap is sizing the divider against \(\beta = 300\); do that and the circuit fails on the \(\beta = 50\) parts. Say "worst case" out loud when you pick it.

Part 2 — What does this one do?

8 Trace it Warm-up

An NPN with its base shorted to its collector. What is it?

How to answer

A diode — and a better one than a diode.

With \(V_{CB} = 0\) the transistor is on the edge of the active region, so it still follows \(I_C = I_Se^{V_{BE}/V_T}\). You get a junction whose exponential is clean over many decades, and whose \(V_{BE}\) can be matched to the other transistors on the same die.

"It is the input side of every current mirror, and it is the standard way to build a log converter or a temperature-proportional voltage, because the exponential is more ideal than a real diode's. The one thing to know is that its dynamic resistance is \(r_e = V_T/I\) — 26 ohms at a milliamp — not zero."

What they are testing: a quick one, usually asked as a lead-in to current mirrors. The phrase "diode-connected" should come out immediately.
9 Trap Standard

Somebody wires an NPN backwards — collector where the emitter should be. Does it work?

How to answer

Yes, badly. This is reverse-active mode and the transistor still amplifies, with a \(\beta_R\) of about 2 to 5 instead of 100 to 300.

The asymmetry is physical: the emitter is doped far more heavily than the collector so that injection into the base dominates, and the collector is made large to handle dissipation. Swap them and you are injecting from the lightly-doped side into a region that was not designed to collect.

"There is one place people do it on purpose: the reverse \(V_{BE}\) breakdown is around 6 to 7 volts, so a reverse-connected base-emitter junction makes a cheap zener. It also has a much lower saturation voltage, a few millivolts, which is occasionally exploited in chopper circuits. But avalanching a base-emitter junction permanently degrades \(\beta\) and the noise figure, so if the part has to amplify afterwards, do not."

What they are testing: whether you understand a transistor as a doped structure rather than as a symbol. "It works but poorly, because the doping is asymmetric" is the answer; "it does not work" and "it works fine" are both wrong.
10 Trace it Standard

A common-emitter stage biased at \(I_C = 2\,\mathrm{mA}\) with \(R_C = 10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) from a 12 V supply. What is the output doing?

How to answer

Nothing. The transistor is saturated.

\[ V_{R_C} = I_CR_C = (2\,\mathrm{mA})(10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega) = 20\,\mathrm{V} \]
\[ 20\,\mathrm{V} > 12\,\mathrm{V} \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{impossible} \] The supply cannot deliver that drop, so the collector goes as low as it can — \(V_{CE(sat)}\), about 0.2 V — and the transistor is a closed switch, not an amplifier.

"So the actual collector current is limited to about \((12-0.2)/10\mathrm{k} = 1.18\,\mathrm{mA}\) by the resistor, not by the base drive, and there is no gain and no swing. To bias at 2 mA with 10 k you would need a supply above about 20 volts, or you keep 12 V and drop \(R_C\) to around 3 k."

What they are testing: whether you check the DC operating point before doing any AC analysis. Interviewers plant impossible bias points deliberately. Multiplying \(I_C R_C\) and comparing it to the supply is a three-second check that should be reflexive — do it before you write a single small-signal equation.
11 Trace it Harder

A class B push-pull output stage: NPN and PNP emitter followers, bases tied together, driven from a signal source. Sketch the output for a small sine input.

How to answer

Crossover distortion — a flat dead zone about 1.4 V wide in the middle of every cycle.

Neither transistor conducts until its \(V_{BE}\) exceeds roughly 0.7 V. Between \(-0.7\) and \(+0.7\) V at the bases, both are off and the output does not move at all. For a 10 V signal that is a modest distortion; for a 100 mV signal the output is entirely dead zone and nothing comes out.

"The fix is class AB: bias the two bases about 1.4 V apart so both devices idle at a small quiescent current and the handover is continuous. You do it with two diodes, or better a \(V_{BE}\) multiplier, mounted on the same heatsink as the output devices — because if the bias network does not track the output transistors' temperature, the quiescent current rises with heat, which raises the dissipation, which raises the heat. That is thermal runaway and it destroys output stages."

What they are testing: two things at once. That distortion is worst for small signals, which is counterintuitive and is why crossover distortion is so audible. And whether you get to thermal tracking unprompted — mentioning that the bias reference must be thermally coupled to the output devices is a strong marker for having built a power stage rather than read about one.
12 Trap Nasty

An emitter follower buffering a signal to a coaxial cable. It oscillates at 200 MHz. A follower has a gain of less than one — how can it oscillate?

How to answer

Because at high frequency the input impedance of an emitter follower has a negative real part.

Looking into the base you see \(r_\pi\) in parallel with \(C_\pi\), and the emitter load reflected up by \(\beta+1\). But \(\beta\) is complex and falling above \(f_\beta\), so the reflected impedance acquires a phase shift. With a capacitive load on the emitter — a cable is a capacitor at these frequencies — and an inductive source at the base, the input resistance goes negative over a band. A negative resistance in series with an inductance and a capacitance is an oscillator.

"It is the classic one. The standard fix is a small base-stopper resistor, 10 to 100 ohms, right at the base pin, which adds enough positive real part to swamp the negative one. A ferrite bead does the same job. It costs you a little bandwidth and it is why you see an unexplained small resistor at the base of nearly every discrete follower in production hardware."

What they are testing: this separates people who have debugged hardware from everyone else. "Gain less than one so it cannot oscillate" is intuitive and wrong, and an emitter follower oscillating into a cable is one of the most common real failures in discrete design. If you can name the base stopper you have almost certainly hit this yourself.
13 Trap Nasty

A current source made from a transistor with a fixed base voltage and an emitter resistor. Its output current drifts 15% when the board warms up. Why, and what do you do?

How to answer
\[ I_E = \frac{V_B - V_{BE}}{R_E} \]
\[ \frac{dV_{BE}}{dT} = -2\,\mathrm{mV/°C} \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta V_{BE} = -100\,\mathrm{mV} \text{ over } 50\,\mathrm{°C} \]
\[ V_B = 1.4\,\mathrm{V},\ V_{BE} = 0.7 \Rightarrow V_{R_E} = 0.7\,\mathrm{V}; \quad \frac{0.1}{0.7} = 14\% \] The drift lands entirely on the emitter resistor, and its size relative to \(V_{R_E}\) is the error.

Three fixes, in increasing order of goodness:

  • Raise \(V_{R_E}\). At 2 V across \(R_E\) the same 100 mV is only 5%. Costs headroom, fixes nothing fundamental.
  • Generate \(V_B\) from a diode-connected transistor at the same temperature, so the two \(V_{BE}\) drops track and cancel. That is a current mirror, and it is why mirrors are the default.
  • Use a reference whose tempco is deliberately opposite — a bandgap, where the \(-2\,\mathrm{mV/°C}\) of \(V_{BE}\) is cancelled by the \(+0.086\,\mathrm{mV/°C}\) of \(\Delta V_{BE}\) scaled up by about 23.

"The general principle is that you never fight a \(V_{BE}\); you cancel it against another one at the same temperature. Almost every good bipolar bias circuit is an application of that idea."

What they are testing: whether −2 mV/°C is a number you can immediately turn into a percentage error. The ratio \(\Delta V_{BE}/V_{R_E}\) is the whole answer and it should take fifteen seconds.

Part 3 — Rapid fire

14 Rapid

\(g_m\) at 100 µA? At 10 mA?

Answer

3.85 mA/V and 385 mA/V. It is linear in \(I_C\), so scale from 38.5 mA/V at 1 mA and do not compute anything.

15 Rapid

How do you tell from a measurement whether a BJT is saturated?

Answer

\(V_{CE}\) below about 0.2 V, or equivalently \(V_C < V_B\) for an NPN — the collector junction has gone forward-biased. In saturation the collector current no longer follows the base current, so \(\beta\) appears to collapse; that is the symptom people usually notice first.

16 Rapid

Why is \(\beta\) a bad thing to design around?

Answer

It varies 3:1 across parts of the same type, another 2:1 over temperature, and it changes with collector current. Datasheets bin it for that reason. Any circuit whose behaviour depends on \(\beta\) is a circuit that works on your bench and not in production — which is why we use degeneration and mirrors, both of which arrange for \(\beta\) to cancel.

17 Rapid

What is the Early effect and where does it hurt?

Answer

Collector current rises with \(V_{CE}\) because the depletion region widens and the effective base narrows: \(r_o = V_A/I_C\). It sets the maximum gain of a single stage, \(g_mr_o = V_A/V_T\) — about 4000 for \(V_A = 100\,\mathrm{V}\), and no cleverness with resistors beats it. It also degrades current mirrors, and the cascode is the fix in both cases.

18 Rapid

BJT or MOSFET for a low-noise, low-offset input stage at moderate impedance?

Answer

BJT. Higher \(g_m\) for the same current, better matching, lower voltage noise and much less \(1/f\) noise. You take the input bias current as the price. Go MOSFET when the source impedance is high enough that \(I_BR_S\) beats the noise advantage, or when you need a switch, or when you need zero gate current.

If you remember six things

Gain is an impedance ratio
\(-R_C/(r_e+R_E)\). Everything else is a special case of that.
Check the DC first
\(I_CR_C\) against the supply. An impossible bias point makes all the AC algebra worthless.
\(g_m\) is physics
\(I_C/V_T\), the same for every bipolar transistor ever made. 38.5 mA/V at 1 mA.
Miller explains slow
\(C_\mu(1+|A_v|)\), and the cascode is why the fix exists.
Never trust \(\beta\)
3:1 across parts. Degenerate, mirror, or feed back until it cancels.
Cancel a \(V_{BE}\), never fight it
−2 mV/°C against another junction at the same temperature. That is the whole trick.
