The Miller Effect, Derived

The previous lesson added two capacitances to the hybrid-π model. One of them, \(C_\pi\), sits from base to emitter and does what you would expect. The other, \(C_\mu\), bridges base to collector — from the input of the amplifier to its output — and does something far stranger.

A few picofarads of \(C_\mu\) will cost you more bandwidth than everything else in the circuit combined. This lesson derives exactly why, and the derivation is short: three lines of algebra with a consequence that shapes the entire design of high-frequency amplifiers.

The result, stated first. An impedance bridging the input and output of an inverting amplifier of gain \(-A\) appears at the input as that impedance divided by \((1+A)\). For a capacitor, dividing the impedance means multiplying the capacitance. A 4 pF collector-base capacitance in a stage with a gain of 182 behaves like 732 pF across the input.

1. The derivation

Take any amplifier with voltage gain \(A_v = v_{out}/v_{in}\), and bridge an impedance \(Z\) from its input node to its output node. Ask the only question that matters: how much current does the source have to supply into that node?

\[ i_{in} = \frac{v_{in} - v_{out}}{Z} \] Ohm's law across the bridging element. Nothing has happened yet.
\[ i_{in} = \frac{v_{in} - A_v v_{in}}{Z} = \frac{v_{in}(1 - A_v)}{Z} \] Substitute \(v_{out} = A_v v_{in}\). This is the whole trick: the far end of the impedance is not sitting still, it is being driven backwards by the amplifier.
\[ Z_{in} \equiv \frac{v_{in}}{i_{in}} = \frac{Z}{1 - A_v} \]
\[ \text{for an inverting amplifier } A_v = -A: \qquad \boxed{\;Z_{in} = \frac{Z}{1 + A}\;} \] The sign is everything. An inverting gain makes \(1 - A_v = 1 + A\), a large positive number, so the impedance shrinks.

Now make \(Z\) a capacitor. Its impedance is \(1/sC\), and dividing an impedance by \((1+A)\) is the same as multiplying its capacitance by \((1+A)\):

\[ Z_{in} = \frac{1}{(1+A)\,sC} = \frac{1}{s\,C_{\text{eff}}} \]
\[ \boxed{\;C_{\text{eff}} = C\,(1 + A)\;} \] The Miller capacitance. This is the entire effect, and it took four lines.
The physical picture, which is worth having

The capacitor is not being charged through \(v_{in}\) volts. It is being charged through \(v_{in} - v_{out} = v_{in}(1+A)\) volts, because while the input goes up the output goes down. A capacitor forced to swing \((1+A)\) times harder draws \((1+A)\) times the current, and a component that draws \((1+A)\) times the current for the same input voltage is, by definition, \((1+A)\) times the capacitance.

Nothing about the physical capacitor changed. What changed is what is on the other side of it.

2. The output side, which is usually forgotten and usually harmless

The same bridging element also loads the output. Repeat the derivation looking the other way:

\[ i_{out} = \frac{v_{out} - v_{in}}{Z} = \frac{v_{out}\left(1 - \tfrac{1}{A_v}\right)}{Z} \]
\[ Z_{out} = \frac{Z}{1 - 1/A_v} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad C_{\text{eff,out}} = C\left(1 + \frac{1}{A}\right) \]
\[ A = 182 \;\Rightarrow\; C_{\text{eff,out}} = 4\,\mathrm{pF} \times 1.0055 = 4.02\,\mathrm{pF} \] Essentially just \(C\) itself. The output sees the capacitor almost unchanged.

So the effect is spectacularly asymmetric: \(\times 183\) at the input, \(\times 1.006\) at the output. That asymmetry is why the source impedance driving the stage matters so much more than the load, and it is the first thing to check when an amplifier is slower than it should be.

The Miller split. The two circuits have the same input and output currents, so nothing has been approximated yet — except the assumption that \(A\) is a single number, which section 4 takes apart.

3. What it costs: a worked bandwidth

Use the common-emitter stage from the small-signal lesson, unchanged, and add the capacitances a real 2N3904 has.

Given: \(\ic = 1\,\mathrm{mA}\), \(\gm = 38.68\,\mathrm{mA/V}\), \(A_v = -182\), \(C_\mu = 4\,\mathrm{pF}\), \(C_\pi = 10\,\mathrm{pF}\), driven from a source resistance \(R_S = 1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), and a device \(f_T\) of \(300\,\mathrm{MHz}\).

\[ C_{M} = C_\mu (1 + A) = 4\,\mathrm{pF} \times 183 = 732\,\mathrm{pF} \]
\[ C_{in,total} = C_\pi + C_M = 10 + 732 = 742\,\mathrm{pF} \] \(C_\pi\) is 1.3% of the problem. Everything else is \(C_\mu\) wearing a disguise.
\[ f_{-3\mathrm{dB}} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_S C_{in,total}} = \frac{1}{2\pi (1000)(742\times10^{-12})} \]
\[ f_{-3\mathrm{dB}} = 215\,\mathrm{kHz} \]
Read that number against the device

A transistor with a \(300\,\mathrm{MHz}\) transition frequency has produced an amplifier good to \(215\,\mathrm{kHz}\). A factor of 1400 has gone missing, and all of it went into four picofarads.

This is the single most important reason that high-frequency circuits do not look like textbook common-emitter stages. Everything in the rest of this module — cascode, common-base, differential pairs with cascode loads — is in some way a response to this calculation.

Cross-checking with open-circuit time constants

The Miller estimate above quietly assumed the input pole dominates. The standard hand method for confirming that is to sum the time constant each capacitor sees with the others open-circuited, which requires no assumption about which one wins:

\[ \tau = R_{\pi 0}C_\pi + R_{\mu 0}C_\mu, \qquad R_{\pi 0} = R_S \parallel r_\pi \approx R_S \]
\[ R_{\mu 0} = R_S + R_L + \gm R_S R_L \] The resistance seen by \(C_\mu\) with \(C_\pi\) removed: the two end resistances in series, plus the term the controlled source adds — which is the Miller effect arriving by a different road.
\[ R_{\mu 0} = 1000 + 4706 + (0.03868)(1000)(4706) = 187.7\,\mathrm{k}\Omega \]
\[ \tau = (1000)(10\,\mathrm{pF}) + (187.7\,\mathrm{k})(4\,\mathrm{pF}) = 10 + 751 = 761\,\mathrm{ns} \]
\[ f_{-3\mathrm{dB}} \approx \frac{1}{2\pi\tau} = 209\,\mathrm{kHz} \] Against 215 kHz from the Miller shortcut. They agree to 3%, which is the check: the input pole really does dominate here.

4. Where the derivation is not quite honest

Step two substituted \(v_{out} = A_v v_{in}\) with \(A_v\) a fixed number. But \(A_v\) rolls off with frequency — and it rolls off because of the very capacitance being analysed. The derivation used a result that its own conclusion undermines.

That circularity is fine, with a caveat you should be able to state:

Miller is a low-frequency approximation, valid below the pole it predicts. At and above that pole the gain has fallen, so \(C_{\text{eff}}\) is smaller than \(C(1+A)\), and the true response rolls off slightly more gently than the single-pole estimate. Use Miller to find the bandwidth; do not use it to predict the shape of the response two decades past it.

The exact analysis solves both node equations without assuming anything. Writing them out for the base node \(v_1\) and collector node \(v_2\):

\[ \frac{v_1 - v_s}{R_S} + \frac{v_1}{r_\pi} + sC_\pi v_1 + sC_\mu(v_1 - v_2) = 0 \]
\[ \gm v_1 + \frac{v_2}{R_L} + sC_\mu(v_2 - v_1) = 0 \]
\[ \frac{v_2}{v_1} = \frac{sC_\mu - \gm}{1/R_L + sC_\mu} \] Rearranged from the second equation alone, and it already contains the surprise.

The right-half-plane zero

That numerator vanishes at \(sC_\mu = \gm\), which is a zero at

\[ s_z = +\frac{\gm}{C_\mu}, \qquad f_z = \frac{\gm}{2\pi C_\mu} = \frac{0.03868}{2\pi(4\times10^{-12})} = 1.54\,\mathrm{GHz} \]

Positive. A zero in the right half plane, and it is there for a physical reason worth understanding: \(C_\mu\) is a direct path from input to output that bypasses the transistor. At low frequency the transistor's inverting path dominates and the output is out of phase with the input. At high enough frequency the capacitor's non-inverting feedthrough wins. Somewhere between, the two paths are equal and opposite, and the output is exactly zero.

Why a right-half-plane zero is bad news, in one sentence. It adds gain like a normal zero but subtracts phase like a pole — the worst of both, for a feedback loop. For a discrete BJT it sits at \(\gm/C_\mu\), typically well above \(f_T\), so it can usually be ignored; in a CMOS op-amp's Miller-compensated second stage the same zero lands right in the middle of the loop's crossover, which is why those designs put a nulling resistor in series with the compensation capacitor to move it.

5. The four fixes, and what each costs

\(C_{\text{eff}} = C_\mu(1 + \gm R_L)\) has three variables in it. Every cure attacks one of them, or attacks \(R_S\) instead.

Note the pattern in the last row: gain and bandwidth trade against each other within a stage, but cascading changes the exchange rate. That observation, made precisely, is the gain-bandwidth product, and it is why a two-stage amplifier is not simply a worse one-stage amplifier.

Problem Set — Miller

All answerable from this lesson and the small-signal model before it.

1Level 1

An inverting amplifier has \(A_v = -50\) and a \(3\,\mathrm{pF}\) capacitance from input to output. What capacitance does the source see, and what does the load see?

Solution

\[ C_{in} = 3(1 + 50) = 153\,\mathrm{pF} \]

\[ C_{out} = 3(1 + 1/50) = 3.06\,\mathrm{pF} \]

A factor of 51 at one end and 1.02 at the other, from the same component.

2Level 1

A stage has \(C_\pi = 15\,\mathrm{pF}\) and \(C_\mu = 2\,\mathrm{pF}\). At what voltage gain does \(C_\mu\) contribute as much input capacitance as \(C_\pi\)?

Solution

\[ C_\mu(1+A) = C_\pi \;\Rightarrow\; 1 + A = \frac{15}{2} = 7.5 \;\Rightarrow\; A = 6.5 \]

At a gain of six and a half. Any amplifier worth building has more gain than that, so in practice \(C_\mu\) is always the dominant input capacitance — even though it is the smaller component by a factor of seven.

3Level 2

Show that for a common-emitter stage the Miller input pole can be written \(f_{-3dB} \approx 1/(2\pi R_S C_\mu \gm R_L)\) when the Miller term dominates, and state what this says about the gain-bandwidth product of the stage.

Solution

With \(A = \gm R_L \gg 1\) and \(C_M \gg C_\pi\):

\[ C_{in} \approx C_\mu \gm R_L, \qquad f_{-3dB} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_S C_\mu \gm R_L} \]

Multiply by the gain:

\[ A \cdot f_{-3dB} = \gm R_L \cdot \frac{1}{2\pi R_S C_\mu \gm R_L} = \frac{1}{2\pi R_S C_\mu} \]

The gain cancels entirely. The gain-bandwidth product of a Miller-limited stage depends only on the source resistance and \(C_\mu\) — not on the bias current, not on the load, not on how much gain you asked for. That is why lowering the gain buys bandwidth one-for-one, and why the only escapes are a smaller \(C_\mu\), a stiffer source, or a topology with no Miller path at all.

4Level 2

An op-amp uses a \(30\,\mathrm{pF}\) compensation capacitor across a second stage with a gain of 500. What capacitance appears at that stage's input, and why would a designer deliberately create such an enormous number?

Solution

\[ C_{eff} = 30\,\mathrm{pF} \times 501 = 15\,\mathrm{nF} \]

Fifteen nanofarads from thirty picofarads of silicon — a capacitor that would be physically impossible to integrate is synthesised by the gain around it. That is the entire point: Miller compensation buys a very low dominant pole for very little die area.

The designer wants the pole low, because a single dominant pole well below every other pole is what makes the op-amp unconditionally stable. The bandwidth being deliberately thrown away here is the same bandwidth that was accidentally lost in the common-emitter example. Same physics, opposite intent.

5Level 3

The worked example gave \(215\,\mathrm{kHz}\) from a \(300\,\mathrm{MHz}\) device. Suppose you insert an emitter follower between the source and the stage, dropping the effective \(R_S\) from \(1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) to \(30\,\Omega\). Find the new bandwidth, then explain why it is not \(1000/30 = 33\) times better.

Solution

\[ f = \frac{1}{2\pi (30)(742\times10^{-12})} = 7.15\,\mathrm{MHz} \]

That is 33× better, so the arithmetic does hold — but only for this pole. The reason it will not be observed is that at 7 MHz other poles have arrived: the follower has its own output pole, the load capacitance works against \(R_L = 4.7\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) giving a pole at \(1/(2\pi \cdot 4706 \cdot C_L)\) which is only \(3.4\,\mathrm{MHz}\) for a 10 pF probe, and \(\beta\) is falling. Fixing the dominant pole promotes whatever was second to be the new limit, which is the general lesson: a bandwidth calculation is only ever valid until it succeeds.

6Level 3

Using open-circuit time constants, find the bandwidth of a cascode built from the same devices: \(C_\pi = 10\,\mathrm{pF}\), \(C_\mu = 4\,\mathrm{pF}\), \(\gm = 38.68\,\mathrm{mA/V}\), \(R_S = 1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), \(R_L = 4.706\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). The lower device now sees a load of \(1/\gm_2\).

Solution

The lower transistor's load is \(1/\gm = 25.9\,\Omega\), so:

\[ R_{\mu 0} = R_S + \frac{1}{\gm} + \gm R_S \frac{1}{\gm} = 1000 + 25.9 + 1000 = 2026\,\Omega \]

\[ \tau = (1000)(10\,\mathrm{pF}) + (2026)(4\,\mathrm{pF}) = 10 + 8.1 = 18.1\,\mathrm{ns} \]

\[ f_{-3dB} = \frac{1}{2\pi(18.1\,\mathrm{ns})} = 8.79\,\mathrm{MHz} \]

Against 209 kHz for the plain stage: a factor of 42, for one extra transistor, with the voltage gain unchanged. Note also that \(C_\pi\) is now the larger of the two terms — the cascode has shrunk the Miller term until it stopped being the problem, which is exactly what a good fix looks like.

7Level 4

Derive the frequency of the right-half-plane zero from the physical argument (the capacitor's feedthrough current cancelling the transistor's current), rather than from the node equations. Then explain why a series resistor \(R_z\) with the capacitor moves it, and find the \(R_z\) that sends it to infinity.

Solution

Two currents arrive at the collector node from the base signal \(v_1\): the transistor's, and the capacitor's feedthrough. Output is zero when they cancel:

\[ \underbrace{\gm v_1}_{\text{transistor, into the node}} = \underbrace{sC_\mu v_1}_{\text{feedthrough, out of it}} \;\Rightarrow\; s_z = \frac{\gm}{C_\mu} \]

With \(R_z\) in series, the feedthrough branch has impedance \(R_z + 1/sC_\mu\), so its current is \(v_1/(R_z + 1/sC_\mu)\). Setting that equal to \(\gm v_1\):

\[ \frac{1}{R_z + 1/sC_\mu} = \gm \;\Rightarrow\; R_z + \frac{1}{sC_\mu} = \frac{1}{\gm} \]

\[ s_z = \frac{1}{C_\mu\left(\tfrac{1}{\gm} - R_z\right)} \]

At \(R_z = 1/\gm\) the denominator vanishes and the zero goes to infinity. Push further, \(R_z > 1/\gm\), and \(s_z\) turns negative — the zero crosses into the left half plane, where it adds phase and can be used to cancel a troublesome pole. This is exactly the nulling resistor used in Miller-compensated op-amps, and \(R_z\) is typically set a little above \(1/\gm\) on purpose.

Lab — measure Miller multiplication, then defeat it

Time 75 min Skill bandwidth measurement; source-impedance sensitivity; building a cascode Parts 2× 2N3904, resistors, caps, signal generator, scope with 10× probes

Miller is invisible if you measure the wrong thing. The tell is that the bandwidth depends on the source resistance, strongly, in a circuit where nothing about the source should matter. This lab uses that as the measurement.

Circuit. The common-emitter stage from the previous lab, unchanged: \(V_{CC} = 10\,\mathrm{V}\), \(R_C = 5.1\,\mathrm{k}\), \(R_E = 1\,\mathrm{k}\) bypassed, biased at 1 mA. The only new part is a switchable series resistor between the generator and the coupling capacitor.

  1. Confirm the bias at 1 mA and the midband gain at 1 kHz, as before. Keep the output amplitude small so you stay linear.
  2. With \(R_S = 100\,\Omega\), sweep the generator up in frequency and find where the output falls 3 dB (to 0.707 of midband). Record it.
  3. Repeat with \(R_S = 1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), then \(10\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). Three points.
  4. Plot \(1/f_{-3dB}\) against \(R_S\). It should be a straight line. Its slope is \(2\pi C_{in}\) — extract \(C_{in}\).
  5. Compute the \(C_\mu\) that implies: \(C_\mu = (C_{in} - C_\pi)/(1+A)\), using your measured gain. Compare with the 2N3904 datasheet's \(C_{obo}\), typically 3.5–4 pF.
  6. Now halve the gain by adding \(R_E'\) of about \(26\,\Omega\) unbypassed. Repeat step 3 at \(R_S = 1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\). The bandwidth should roughly double.
  7. Build the cascode: a second 2N3904 with its emitter at the first one's collector, its base held at about 5 V by a divider and bypassed to ground with \(0.1\,\mathrm{\mu F}\), and \(R_C\) moved to its collector. Nothing else changes. Measure the bandwidth at \(R_S = 1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\).

What you should find. A bandwidth in the low hundreds of kHz at \(1\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\), moving roughly in inverse proportion to \(R_S\) — the straight line in step 4 is the actual evidence that a single capacitance at the input is responsible. An extracted \(C_\mu\) within a factor of about 1.5 of the datasheet. Halving the gain should roughly double the bandwidth, confirming that the gain and the bandwidth trade one for one. The cascode should be dramatically faster — tens of times — at the same voltage gain, which is the result worth having.

Where it goes wrong, and why that is the interesting part. The cascode measurement is the one that will disappoint, and the reason is your probe. A 10× probe is about \(10\,\mathrm{pF}\); across \(R_C \parallel \ro = 4.7\,\mathrm{k}\Omega\) that is a pole at \(3.4\,\mathrm{MHz}\), and you will measure it instead of the amplifier. Once you remove the input pole, the probe becomes the bandwidth. Two ways to tell: touch a second probe to the same node and watch the bandwidth halve — a real amplifier pole would not care — or add a deliberate 100 pF to the collector and check that the bandwidth falls by 11×, as a probe-dominated pole must.

This is the same lesson as problem 5 arriving on the bench: fixing the dominant pole promotes whatever was second, and the instrument is very often what was second.
