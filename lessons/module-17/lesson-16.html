DNL, INL, and Missing Codes

Differential and Integral Nonlinearity are the fundamental metrics for characterizing data converter accuracy. Understanding their relationship is essential for selecting the right converter for your application.

DNL
Step-to-step error vs ideal
INL
Cumulative deviation from line
Missing Code
DNL ≤ -1 LSB
Key Insight: DNL measures local accuracy (how well each step matches the ideal 1-LSB increment), while INL measures global accuracy (how far the overall transfer function deviates from a straight line). A converter can have good INL but poor local DNL, or vice versa. For control applications, DNL is often more critical than INL.

Mathematical Definitions

Common Error Patterns by Architecture

Architecture Typical DNL Pattern Primary Cause Mitigation
R-2R Ladder Spikes at major carries (midpoint, 1/4, 3/4) Resistor ratio mismatch Laser trimming, thin-film resistors
Binary-Weighted Capacitor (SAR) Repetitive 2^k pattern in histogram Capacitor mismatch, settling Better layout matching, calibration
Segmented DAC Errors at segment boundaries Segment element mismatch Dynamic element matching (DEM)
Pipeline ADC Errors at stage boundaries MDAC gain errors, incomplete settling Digital correction, background cal
Sigma-Delta Very low DNL (inherently) Multi-bit DAC in loop (if used) DEM, noise shaping moves error to HF
Flash ADC Random pattern from comparator offsets Comparator threshold variations Bubble error correction, averaging
MSB Mismatch is the #1 Enemy:

In binary-weighted architectures, each bit's influence on DNL is proportional to its weight:
  • MSB mismatch of 0.1% → DNL error of 0.1% × 2^(N-1) LSB = 0.5 LSB for 10-bit
  • For 12-bit, same 0.1% mismatch → 2 LSB error!
  • For 16-bit, need <0.003% matching for 1 LSB DNL

This is why high-resolution converters often use segmented architectures or sigma-delta topology.

What to run: a DC operating point.

What to watch: V(t1), V(t2), V(t3), V(buf).

The taps read 0.5556, 1.3889 and 1.9444 V, so the four steps are 0.5556, 0.8333, 0.5556 and 0.5556 V. Against an ideal LSB of 2.5/4 = 0.625 V that is 0.889, 1.333, 0.889 and 0.889 LSB, so DNL = −0.111, +0.333, −0.111, −0.111 LSB. INL is the running sum of those, and it returns to zero at the top — it has to, because the string ends at VREF whatever the resistors do.

Now the point. R3 is fifty percent wrong — three and a half times the 14.3% that tips lesson 15's R-2R ladder into going backwards — and this converter is still perfectly monotonic. It cannot be anything else: the taps are physically ordered along a string carrying one current in one direction, so a later tap is higher than an earlier one no matter what the resistances are. Monotonicity here is a property of the topology rather than of the components, which is why string DACs are what you reach for inside a control loop, and why they are also slow and area-hungry — 2n resistors and 2n switches, against the 2n of a ladder.
