Converter Architecture & Monotonicity

Different data converter architectures have fundamentally different monotonicity characteristics. Understanding these differences is crucial for selecting the right converter for your application.

Sigma-Delta
Inherently monotonic
SAR
Depends on cap matching
R-2R Ladder
Sensitive to MSB matching
Key Insight: Sigma-Delta converters achieve inherent monotonicity through oversampling and noise shaping, which averages out component mismatches. SAR and pipeline converters require careful component matching or digital calibration. R-2R ladder DACs are the most sensitive to resistor matching, especially at major carry transitions.

Component Matching Requirements by Resolution

Resolution Codes Matching for 0.5 LSB DNL Typical Technology
8-bit 256 0.4% (1/256) Standard CMOS
10-bit 1024 0.1% (1/1024) Good CMOS, trimming
12-bit 4096 0.025% (1/4096) Thin-film, laser trim
14-bit 16384 0.006% Segmented + DEM
16-bit 65536 0.0015% Sigma-delta or heavy cal
18-bit+ >262144 <0.0004% Sigma-delta only practical
Why Sigma-Delta Dominates High Resolution:

At 16+ bits the matching requirement is 0.0015%, which is achievable with passive components but not cheaply and not without trimming — Analog Devices' AD5791 is a 20-bit R-2R DAC with laser-trimmed thin-film resistors, and 16- to 20-bit SARs with guaranteed no missing codes are ordinary catalogue parts. What sigma-delta buys is that it never needs the matching at all:
  • The internal 1-bit DAC has only 2 levels - perfect linearity
  • Multi-bit variants use dynamic element matching to randomize errors
  • Noise shaping pushes quantization noise to high frequencies
  • Digital decimation filter provides the resolution

This is why all 24-bit audio converters and precision measurement ADCs use sigma-delta architecture.

What to run: a DC operating point for each trial code, by editing Vb3–Vb0.

What to watch: V(vcm), V(cmp).

V(vcm) is the array acting as a capacitive divider: the bottom plates selected to VREF against everything else to ground, so V(vcm) = 2.5 × (selected C)/16.08 pF. The comparator then answers the one question a successive-approximation converter asks at each step — is the trial above or below the input? Set the code, read V(cmp), keep or drop the bit, move on. Four operating points and you have run a conversion by hand.

Compare the major carry with lesson 15. Code 7 gives 1.0883 V and code 8 gives 1.2562 V, a step of 0.1679 against an ideal LSB of 0.15625: DNL = +0.075 LSB. The same 1% error on the same bit, and the sign is opposite to the ladder's −0.075. In an R-2R the MSB rung competes with the rest of the ladder, so making it bigger weakens the MSB; in a charge array the MSB capacitor simply adds to the total, so making it bigger strengthens it. The same component error produces opposite errors in the two architectures, which is worth knowing before assuming a matching specification transfers between them.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.
