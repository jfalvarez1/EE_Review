Interview Prep — Data Conversion

Converter questions are popular with interviewers because they are quantitative, they have exact answers, and they punish memorisation. Nearly all of them come down to one of three things: a resolution calculation, a Nyquist argument, or the observation that the specification on the front page is not the one that matters.

The numbers to have ready.

Part 1 — Derive it

1 Derive Standard

Where does \(6.02N + 1.76\) come from? Derive it.

How to answer
\[ q = \frac{V_{FS}}{2^N} \] One LSB. Quantisation error is uniformly distributed over \(\pm q/2\).
\[ e_{rms}^2 = \frac{1}{q}\int_{-q/2}^{q/2}e^2\,de = \frac{q^2}{12} \;\Longrightarrow\; e_{rms} = \frac{q}{\sqrt{12}} \] The \(\sqrt{12}\) is the variance of a uniform distribution. This is the only real step.
\[ V_{sig,rms} = \frac{V_{FS}}{2\sqrt{2}} \quad\text{(full-scale sine)} \]
\[ \mathrm{SNR} = 20\log_{10}\frac{V_{FS}/2\sqrt2}{q/\sqrt{12}} = 20\log_{10}\left(2^N\sqrt{\tfrac{12}{8}}\right) \]
\[ = 6.02N + 1.76\ \mathrm{dB} \] The 1.76 is \(20\log_{10}\sqrt{1.5}\) — it comes entirely from the assumption of a full-scale SINE. For a triangle or a different crest factor it changes.

"Which is why the specification is quoted for a full-scale sine input. Drive it at half scale and you lose 6 dB immediately — the quantisation noise did not change, your signal did. That is a real effect in systems that leave headroom for transients."

What they are testing: whether you know where the 1.76 comes from. Most candidates recite the formula; being able to say "it is the crest factor of a sine, and it changes for other signals" is what a converter person says.
2 Derive Harder

A 16-bit ADC sampling at 1 MSPS. How much aperture jitter can you tolerate on a 100 kHz input before jitter, not quantisation, dominates?

How to answer
\[ v(t) = A\sin(2\pi f t) \;\Rightarrow\; \left.\frac{dv}{dt}\right|_{max} = 2\pi f A \] A timing error becomes a voltage error through the slew rate of the signal, so it is worst at the zero crossing and worst at high frequency.
\[ \mathrm{SNR}_{jitter} = -20\log_{10}(2\pi f t_j) \]
\[ \mathrm{SNR}_{16\,bit} = 6.02(16)+1.76 = 98\,\mathrm{dB} \]
\[ t_j = \frac{10^{-98/20}}{2\pi(10^5)} = \frac{1.26\times10^{-5}}{6.28\times10^5} = 20\,\mathrm{ps} \]

"Twenty picoseconds — and note the sample rate never entered the calculation. Jitter is set by the INPUT frequency, not the clock rate, which is the counterintuitive part. Undersample a 100 MHz IF with the same converter and you need 20 femtoseconds, which no practical oscillator delivers. That single fact is why direct RF sampling was impossible for so long and why clock distribution is the hardest part of a high-speed converter board."

What they are testing: that jitter scales with input frequency rather than sample rate. It is the most-missed point in converter interviews and it is immediately checkable — if you say it, you have thought about sampling properly.
3 Derive Harder

Explain oversampling and noise shaping. How much oversampling buys you one extra bit — with and without a modulator?

How to answer
\[ \text{Plain oversampling: quantisation noise is spread over } f_s/2 \] The total noise power \(q^2/12\) is fixed; sampling faster spreads it thinner, and a digital filter throws away everything above your band.
\[ \Delta\mathrm{SNR} = 10\log_{10}(\mathrm{OSR}) \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{4× for 1 bit} \] 3 dB per doubling. Expensive: 4× the sample rate for 6 dB.
\[ \text{First-order } \Sigma\Delta: \quad \Delta\mathrm{SNR} = 9\log_{10}(\mathrm{OSR}) \;\Rightarrow\; \textbf{2× for 1.5 bits} \] The modulator's feedback loop high-pass filters the quantisation noise, pushing it out of the band of interest instead of merely diluting it.
\[ L\text{th order: } \Delta\mathrm{SNR} = (6L+3)\log_{10}(\mathrm{OSR})\ \mathrm{dB\ per\ decade} \]

"So a second-order modulator gets 15 dB per octave of oversampling against 3 dB for brute force. That is how a 1-bit quantiser becomes a 24-bit converter. The costs are latency from the decimation filter, which rules it out of a fast control loop, and stability — high-order single-bit modulators can go unstable at large inputs, which is why practical designs use multi-bit quantisers or cascaded MASH structures."

What they are testing: the distinction between spreading noise and shaping it. Anyone can say "sigma-delta oversamples"; the content is that the loop moves noise out of band rather than just averaging it, and that the exchange rate improves with order.

Part 2 — Diagnose it

4 Trap Standard

You buy a 16-bit ADC and measure 12 bits of usable resolution. The part is not faulty. Give me five reasons.

How to answer

"16-bit" describes the code width, not the accuracy. ENOB is the honest number and it is always lower. In rough order of how often each is the culprit:

  • Reference noise. The reference multiplies the code directly, so its noise and drift appear at full scale. A 10 ppm/°C reference over 50 °C moves 500 ppm, which is 32 LSB at 16 bits.
  • Input driver. A SAR's sampling capacitor demands a charge kick every conversion; if the driving amplifier cannot settle to half an LSB within the acquisition window, the result is wrong before the converter does anything.
  • Clock jitter — question 2.
  • Supply and ground noise. At 16 bits an LSB on a 5 V range is 76 µV; digital return current sharing a plane with the analog ground is easily that.
  • INL and DNL. The part's own transfer function is not a straight line, and INL is often specified at several LSB.

"So I would read the ENOB and the INL from the datasheet before believing the headline, and I would look at the reference and the driver first because those are mine to get wrong, not the vendor's."

What they are testing: system thinking. The number of causes you can name is a direct measure of how many converter boards you have debugged. Naming the reference first is the mark of experience — it is the most common and the most overlooked.
5 Trap Harder

A signal is band-limited to 20 kHz. You sample at 44.1 kSPS, which satisfies Nyquist. Is an anti-alias filter still necessary?

How to answer

Yes, and this is the trap.

Nyquist is satisfied for the signal. It says nothing about everything else the input carries: broadband noise from the source, the amplifier's own noise out to its full bandwidth, mains harmonics, RF pickup, switching-supply hash. All of it folds into the band from 0 to 22.05 kHz and becomes indistinguishable from signal, permanently.

\[ f_{alias} = |f_{in} - n f_s| \]
\[ \text{A 100 kHz switcher tone: } |100 - 2(44.1)| = 11.8\,\mathrm{kHz} \] Lands right in the middle of the audio band, and no filtering after the ADC can remove it.

"The filter has to attenuate everything above \(f_s - f_{max} = 24.1\,\mathrm{kHz}\) to below the noise floor. With a 20 kHz passband and a 24 kHz stopband that is a brutally steep filter — which is exactly why converters oversample: run at 8× and the transition band becomes 20 kHz to 156 kHz, a gentle analog filter does the job, and the sharp part is done digitally where it is cheap and has exact phase."

What they are testing: whether you understand what Nyquist actually promises. "It satisfies Nyquist so we are fine" is a very common and very wrong answer. The oversampling argument at the end is what connects theory to why real converters are built the way they are.
6 Trap Nasty

A 16-bit SAR ADC driven directly by a precision op-amp with 1 MHz bandwidth. Occasional codes are badly wrong. Why?

How to answer

The driver cannot settle the sampling capacitor's charge kick within the acquisition window.

A SAR's input is a switched capacitor. When the sample switch closes, that capacitor — previously charged to the last conversion's value — is thrown onto your amplifier's output. The amplifier sees a step of charge and must settle to better than half an LSB before the switch opens.

\[ \text{Settling needed: } \frac{1}{2^{17}} \;\Rightarrow\; \approx 11.8\ \text{time constants} \] \(\ln(2^{17}) = 11.8\). Half an LSB at 16 bits is one part in 131072.
\[ t_{acq} = 200\,\mathrm{ns} \;\Rightarrow\; \tau < 17\,\mathrm{ns} \;\Rightarrow\; \mathrm{BW} > 9.4\,\mathrm{MHz} \] Ten times what the chosen amplifier has.

"And a precision amplifier is the wrong instinct here — you have optimised offset and drift, which the SAR's own calibration could have handled, at the cost of the bandwidth the job actually needs. The standard solution is an RC between the amplifier and the ADC: the capacitor, a few nanofarads, supplies the charge kick locally so the amplifier only has to replace it slowly, and the resistor, tens of ohms, keeps the amplifier stable driving that capacitance. Most converter datasheets specify that RC explicitly, and it is the first thing I would check on the schematic."

What they are testing: whether you know a SAR input is a switched capacitor rather than a high-impedance node. This is the single most common cause of disappointing performance from a good converter, and the \(\ln(2^{N+1})\) time-constant count is a nice quantitative touch.
7 Rapid

SAR, sigma-delta, pipeline, flash — one sentence each on when you would pick it.

Answer

SAR: medium speed, medium-to-high resolution, no latency — the default for multiplexed instrumentation and control loops. Sigma-delta: highest resolution, low bandwidth, significant latency — weigh scales, temperature, audio. Pipeline: tens to hundreds of MSPS at 12–14 bits with a few clocks of latency — comms and imaging. Flash: gigasamples, 6–8 bits, enormous power and area because it needs \(2^N-1\) comparators — oscilloscope front ends and as the sub-stages inside pipelines.

8 Rapid

What is DNL, what is INL, and which one causes missing codes?

Answer

DNL is the error in the width of each individual step; INL is the accumulated deviation of the whole transfer curve from a straight line. DNL below −1 LSB means a code has zero width — a missing code, and "no missing codes guaranteed" on a datasheet is a statement about DNL. INL is what limits absolute accuracy and it is the one that matters for measurement; DNL is what matters for a control loop, where a missing code is a discontinuity in the feedback path.
