Bandgap Voltage References

Bandgap references produce a stable voltage (~1.2V) by combining two temperature-dependent voltages with opposite tempcos. This cancellation yields excellent temperature stability.

VBE Tempco
≈ -2.1 mV/°C (CTAT)
ΔVBE Tempco
≈ +0.086 mV/°C per ln(n) (PTAT)
It is k/q exactly, so an 8:1 emitter area ratio gives ln 8 × 0.086 = +0.18 mV/°C. Quoting it without the ln(n) makes the cancellation look like a coincidence rather than a design choice.
Bandgap Voltage
≈ 1.205V @ 300K
Key Insight: VBE decreases with temperature (CTAT) while ΔVBE (difference between two transistors at different current densities) increases with temperature (PTAT). By scaling and summing them, the tempcos cancel.

Bandgap Circuit Principle

Curvature Correction: First-order bandgaps have ~10-50 ppm/°C tempco. High-precision references add curvature compensation circuits to achieve <5 ppm/°C over the full temperature range.

What to run: a DC operating point first; then, if your simulator supports it, a sweep of TEMP from −40 °C to 125 °C.

What to watch: V(d1), V(d2), and their difference; then V(vref).

Why a current ratio rather than an area ratio. Textbook bandgaps get ΔVBE by running the same current through transistors of different emitter area — usually 8:1, laid out as a centre transistor surrounded by eight. A general-purpose simulator has no area parameter on its BJT, so the same physics is reached from the other side: same device, different current. Ten to one gives VT ln 10 = 59.5 mV at 300 K, exactly as 8:1 areas would give VT ln 8 = 53.7 mV. On silicon the area ratio is preferred because it is set by geometry, which matches far better than two current sources ever will.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.
