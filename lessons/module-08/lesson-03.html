Precision Analog Techniques

Precision analog circuits must minimize DC errors like offset voltage and drift. Techniques like chopper stabilization and auto-zero eliminate these errors at the cost of complexity and bandwidth.

Key Insight: Chopper amplifiers modulate the input signal to a higher frequency where 1/f noise is negligible, amplify it, then demodulate back. This effectively shifts the DC errors to the chopper frequency where they can be filtered out.

What to run: a transient run of 1 ms in steps of 1 µs.

What to watch: V(mod_p,mod_n), V(amp_out), V(demod), V(demod_n), V(output) — watch the two demodulator poles together, because they are complements and seeing them cross is the clearest picture of what chopping does.

Follow the two signals separately, because they take different routes through the same circuit. The 10 mV input is chopped twice — once going in, once coming out — and two inversions are none, so it arrives at the filter as DC and passes straight through: 1.000 V at the output. The 100 µV offset is chopped once, because it enters after the modulator, so it leaves the demodulator as a 10 mV square wave at 20 kHz. The filter corners at 1/(2π × 10 k × 100 nF) = 159 Hz, far below 20 kHz, so it barely responds within a half period and integrates the square wave into a triangle rather than attenuating a sinusoid: ΔVpp = A T/(2τ) = 10 mV × 50 µs/(2 × 1 ms) = 250 µV peak to peak. Forty times smaller than the 10 mV that went in, and — more to the point — ripple rather than error: a symmetric square wave has no DC component at all, so the offset has been moved out of DC rather than merely made small.

Note the two switches must be electrically driven changeovers, controlled by clk. A mechanical DPDT that you toggle by hand will place and wire correctly and will simply never switch.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.
