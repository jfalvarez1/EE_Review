Poles, Zeros and Bode Plots by Hand

The next lesson asks whether a feedback loop is stable. That question is answered on a Bode plot, and you cannot answer it usefully if the plot arrives from a simulator as a finished picture. This lesson builds the plot from parts, by hand, in under a minute.

Why bother, when the simulator is right there. A simulator tells you what happens. A hand sketch tells you what to change. When a loop has 30° of phase margin, the sketch shows you at a glance which corner frequency is responsible and which component sets it — and the fix follows. Reading that off a numerical plot means running a dozen sweeps to find out what you could have seen in the structure of the expression.

The whole method rests on one fact: a Bode plot is a sum. Decibels turn multiplication into addition, and phase adds already. So a transfer function with five factors is five simple pictures laid on top of one another, and you only ever have to know three pictures.

1. Get the expression into the right form first

Almost every mistake in hand sketching happens before any drawing, by starting from the wrong algebraic form. There are two, and only one of them is sketchable.

FormGood for
Root form \(\displaystyle H(s) = \frac{K'(s+\omega_z)}{(s+\omega_{p1})(s+\omega_{p2})}\) Root locus, pole positions in the s-plane. Not Bode.
Time-constant form \(\displaystyle H(s) = K\frac{1 + s/\omega_z}{(1 + s/\omega_{p1})(1 + s/\omega_{p2})}\) Bode. Every factor is 1 at DC, so \(K\) is the DC gain by inspection.

Converting is just factoring the corner frequency out of each term:

\[ H(s) = \frac{2\times10^{6}\,(s + 500)}{(s+100)(s+10^{5})} \] Root form. What is the DC gain? Not obvious.
\[ = \frac{2\times10^{6}\cdot 500\left(1 + \frac{s}{500}\right)}{100\left(1+\frac{s}{100}\right)\cdot 10^{5}\left(1+\frac{s}{10^{5}}\right)} \]
\[ H(s) = 100\;\frac{1 + s/500}{\left(1+s/100\right)\left(1+s/10^{5}\right)} \] Time-constant form. DC gain 100, or 40 dB, read straight off. Corners at 100, 500 and \(10^5\) rad/s.
Radians or hertz — pick one and say so. Corner frequencies come out of algebra in rad/s and are measured on a bench in Hz, and they differ by \(2\pi\), a factor of 6.28 that is almost a decade. Every number in this lesson after this point is in hertz, because that is what an instrument reads. \(\omega = 2\pi f\).

2. The only three pictures you need

Each factor contributes a magnitude in dB and a phase in degrees, and you add them.

FactorMagnitudePhase
Constant \(K\) Flat at \(20\log_{10}|K|\) dB \(0°\) if positive, \(-180°\) if negative
Pole \(\dfrac{1}{1+s/\omega_p}\) Flat, then \(-20\) dB/decade above \(f_p\) \(0°\to-90°\), passing \(-45°\) at \(f_p\)
Zero \(1+s/\omega_z\) Flat, then \(+20\) dB/decade above \(f_z\) \(0°\to+90°\), passing \(+45°\) at \(f_z\)
Pole at origin \(1/s\) \(-20\) dB/decade everywhere \(-90°\) everywhere
Zero at origin \(s\) \(+20\) dB/decade everywhere \(+90°\) everywhere
RHP zero \(1-s/\omega_z\) \(+20\) dB/decade — same as a normal zero \(0°\to \mathbf{-90°}\) — same as a pole
The right-half-plane zero, again

It appeared in the Miller lesson at \(\gm/C_\mu\), and here is why it is feared. It raises the magnitude, holding the gain up past where you wanted it to fall, while subtracting phase like a pole. Both effects push a loop toward instability. There is no arrangement of components that makes an RHP zero helpful; you move it, cancel it, or design around it.

Why a pole is exactly −20 dB per decade

\[ |H| = \left|\frac{1}{1 + j f/f_p}\right| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + (f/f_p)^2}} \]
\[ f \gg f_p: \quad |H| \to \frac{f_p}{f}, \qquad 20\log_{10}|H| = 20\log_{10}f_p - 20\log_{10}f \] A straight line of slope \(-20\) against \(\log_{10}f\): ten times the frequency, one tenth the magnitude, 20 dB down.
\[ f \ll f_p: \quad |H| \to 1, \qquad 0\ \mathrm{dB} \]

3. How wrong the straight lines are — exactly

The asymptotes meet at a corner the real curve never reaches. Knowing the error turns a sketch from a rough idea into a quantitative tool, and there are only three numbers to remember.

\[ \text{at } f = f_p: \quad |H| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} = -3.01\,\mathrm{dB} \] The asymptotes say 0 dB. This is the entire origin of the phrase “the 3 dB point”.
\[ \text{at } f = 2f_p \text{ or } f_p/2: \quad \text{error} = 20\log_{10}\sqrt{1.25} = 0.97\,\mathrm{dB} \]
\[ \text{at } f = 10f_p \text{ or } f_p/10: \quad \text{error} = 20\log_{10}\sqrt{1.01} = 0.04\,\mathrm{dB} \] A decade away the straight line is right to within four hundredths of a decibel. Nothing on a bench is that accurate.

So the correction is: draw the asymptotes, then pull the curve down 3 dB at each corner and 1 dB an octave either side. Everything more than a decade from a corner is already correct.

Phase, which is where the sketching actually matters

Phase is worse behaved than magnitude, and it is what decides stability. The exact phase of a pole is \(-\arctan(f/f_p)\), which reaches \(-45°\) at the corner but is already at \(-5.7°\) a decade below and still \(-84.3°\) a decade above. The standard approximation makes it a straight line on the log axis:

\[ \angle H \approx \begin{cases} 0° & f < f_p/10 \\[2pt] -45° \cdot \log_{10}\!\left(10\,f/f_p\right) & f_p/10 < f < 10f_p \\[2pt] -90° & f > 10f_p \end{cases} \]
The consequence people get wrong. A pole affects the phase two decades wide, while it affects the magnitude only above its corner. So a second pole a full decade above your crossover frequency, which looks harmless on the magnitude plot, has already eaten \(5.7°\) of phase margin — and one only half a decade above costs \(17.5°\). Poles you can barely see are the ones that destabilise loops.

4. The procedure, as five steps

  1. Time-constant form. Factor every corner out so each term reads \(1 + s/\omega\). Read \(K\) off as the DC gain.
  2. List the corners in order, smallest first, each marked P or Z.
  3. Start at the far left. Draw a horizontal line at \(20\log_{10}|K|\). If the expression has an \(s^n\) in it, start on a slope of \(20n\) dB/decade instead and anchor it wherever you can compute one point.
  4. Walk right. At each corner change the slope by \(-20\) dB/decade for a pole and \(+20\) for a zero. Nothing else changes.
  5. Phase separately. Each pole ramps from \(0°\) to \(-90°\) between a decade below and a decade above its corner; each LHP zero ramps up to \(+90°\); each RHP zero ramps down to \(-90°\). Add them.

Worked example: the loop that the next lesson will ask about

An op-amp with a DC gain of \(10^5\) (100 dB), a dominant pole at 10 Hz and a second pole at 1 MHz, used at a closed-loop gain of 10 (so \(\beta = 0.1\)). Sketch the loop gain \(T = A\beta\), because that is the thing whose phase margin matters.

\[ T(s) = \frac{0.1 \times 10^{5}}{\left(1 + \tfrac{jf}{10}\right)\left(1 + \tfrac{jf}{10^{6}}\right)} \]
\[ |T|_{DC} = 10^{4} = 80\,\mathrm{dB} \] Step 3. Feedback cost 20 dB of the op-amp's 100.
\[ \text{flat to 10 Hz, then } -20\ \mathrm{dB/dec}; \text{ from 1 MHz, } -40 \] Step 4, and that is the entire magnitude plot.
\[ 80\ \mathrm{dB} \text{ at 10 Hz falling 20 dB/dec reaches 0 dB four decades later} \]
\[ f_c = 10 \times 10^{4} = 100\,\mathrm{kHz} \] The crossover, found with no algebra: count decades.
\[ \angle T(f_c) = -\arctan\!\frac{10^5}{10} - \arctan\!\frac{10^5}{10^6} = -89.99° - 5.71° = -95.7° \]
\[ \text{PM} = 180° - 95.7° = 84.3° \qquad \text{comfortably stable} \]

Now do it again at unity gain, \(\beta = 1\). The magnitude plot lifts by 20 dB, so crossover moves a decade right to 1 MHz — landing exactly on the second pole, where that pole contributes \(-45°\):

\[ \angle T = -90° - 45° = -135°, \qquad \text{PM} = 45° \]
What the sketch just told you

This op-amp is fine at a gain of 10 and marginal at a gain of 1 — which is exactly why datasheets say “stable for gains ≥ 5” and why unity-gain buffers are the configuration that rings. The sketch also names the culprit (the 1 MHz pole) and the two available fixes (move the dominant pole down to cross over earlier, or add a zero near 1 MHz to cancel it). None of that came from a simulation.

5. Reading a plot backwards: from picture to circuit

The skill that makes this worth learning is the reverse direction. Given a measured response, name the components.

What you seeWhat it isWhat to look for
Flat, then \(-20\) dB/decOne pole An R and a C somewhere: \(f = 1/2\pi RC\)
\(-20\) dB/dec from the far left, no flat region Pole at the origin — an integrator A capacitor in a feedback path with no DC resistor across it
\(-40\) dB/dec straight awayTwo coincident poles Two identical stages, or an LC. Expect peaking if \(Q\) is high.
Falls, flattens, falls againPole, then zero, then pole A zero is usually a resistor in series with a capacitor
Rises at \(+20\) dB/dec then flattensZero, then pole A lead network — someone was adding phase margin
Magnitude rises but phase fallsRHP zero A feedforward path bypassing an inverting stage. Miller's \(\gm/C_\mu\), or a boost converter's duty-cycle-to-output.
Peak before the roll-offComplex pole pair, \(Q > 0.707\) An LC, or a feedback loop with too little phase margin. Peak height in dB is roughly \(20\log_{10}Q\).
Two poles at the same frequency are not the same as one pole twice as steep. A single \(-40\) dB/decade region tells you the slope, not how the poles are arranged. Two real poles a decade apart, two coincident real poles, and a complex pair all reach \(-40\) dB/decade — but their phases at any given frequency differ by tens of degrees, and only the last one peaks. When stability is the question, the phase plot carries the information and the magnitude plot does not.

Problem Set — sketching

Do these on paper before checking. The point of the exercise is the sketch, not the number at the end.

1Level 1

Put \(\displaystyle H(s) = \frac{5\times10^{4}}{(s+50)(s+2000)}\) into time-constant form and state its DC gain in dB.

Solution

\[ H(s) = \frac{5\times10^{4}}{50 \cdot 2000\left(1+\tfrac{s}{50}\right)\left(1+\tfrac{s}{2000}\right)} = \frac{0.5}{\left(1+\tfrac{s}{50}\right)\left(1+\tfrac{s}{2000}\right)} \]

\[ 20\log_{10}(0.5) = -6.02\,\mathrm{dB} \]

A gain below one at DC, which the root form gave no hint of — the \(5\times10^4\) in the numerator looks like a large number and is not.

2Level 1

A response is flat at 40 dB, breaks at 1 kHz and falls at 20 dB/decade. At what frequency does it cross 0 dB, and what is its exact magnitude at 1 kHz?

Solution

40 dB to fall at 20 dB per decade is two decades: \(1\,\mathrm{kHz} \times 100 = \mathbf{100\,kHz}\).

At the corner the true magnitude is 3.01 dB below the asymptote, so \(\mathbf{36.99\ dB}\), not 40.

The gain-bandwidth product is \(100 \times 1\,\mathrm{kHz} = 100\,\mathrm{kHz}\) either way — the same number as the 0 dB crossing, which is why a single-pole amplifier's GBW and its unity-gain frequency are the same thing.

3Level 2

Sketch \(\displaystyle H = \frac{100\left(1 + s/10^{4}\right)}{\left(1+s/10^{2}\right)\left(1+s/10^{6}\right)}\). Give the slope in each region and the phase at 10 kHz.

Solution

Corners at 100 Hz (P), 10 kHz (Z), 1 MHz (P). DC gain 40 dB.

Below 100 Hz: 0 dB/dec at 40 dB. 100 Hz–10 kHz: \(-20\), falling 40 dB to 0 dB at 10 kHz. 10 kHz–1 MHz: flat at 0 dB, because the zero cancels the pole's slope. Above 1 MHz: \(-20\) again.

Phase at 10 kHz, exactly:

\[ -\arctan\frac{10^4}{10^2} + \arctan\frac{10^4}{10^4} - \arctan\frac{10^4}{10^6} \]

\[ = -89.43° + 45° - 0.57° = -45.0° \]

The flat region between a pole and a zero is exactly what a lead-lag compensator is, and the phase recovering to \(-45°\) in the middle of it is the phase margin it was inserted to buy.

4Level 2

An amplifier's loop crosses over at 500 kHz on a clean \(-20\) dB/decade slope. A parasitic pole sits at 5 MHz. How much phase margin does it cost, and would you have noticed it on the magnitude plot?

Solution

\[ \Delta\phi = -\arctan\frac{500\,\mathrm{k}}{5\,\mathrm{M}} = -\arctan(0.1) = -5.71° \]

On the magnitude plot at 500 kHz that pole contributes \(-20\log_{10}\sqrt{1.01} = -0.04\,\mathrm{dB}\) — invisible. It has taken nearly six degrees of margin while being undetectable in the magnitude.

This is the reason phase margin is quoted rather than gain margin for most amplifier work, and the reason a parasitic a full decade out still has to be counted.

5Level 3

An op-amp has \(A_0 = 10^6\) and a single pole at 5 Hz. You want 60° of phase margin in a closed-loop gain of 100. A parasitic second pole exists at \(f_2\). What is the lowest \(f_2\) you can tolerate?

Solution

Loop gain at DC: \(A_0\beta = 10^6/100 = 10^4 = 80\,\mathrm{dB}\). Crossing 0 dB four decades above 5 Hz:

\[ f_c = 5 \times 10^4 = 50\,\mathrm{kHz} \]

The dominant pole already contributes \(-90°\) there, so the budget for the second pole is:

\[ 180° - 60° - 90° = 30° \]

\[ \arctan\frac{f_c}{f_2} = 30° \;\Rightarrow\; f_2 = \frac{f_c}{\tan 30°} = \frac{50\,\mathrm{kHz}}{0.5774} = 86.6\,\mathrm{kHz} \]

So \(f_2 \ge 86.6\,\mathrm{kHz}\), which is only 1.73× the crossover. Note how much less demanding this is than the unity-gain case: at \(\beta=1\) the crossover would be at 5 MHz and you would need \(f_2 \ge 8.66\,\mathrm{MHz}\). Closing the loop harder always makes the parasitics matter more.

6Level 3

You measure a response that is flat at 20 dB to 2 kHz, falls at 20 dB/decade to 20 kHz, is flat at 0 dB to 200 kHz, then falls at 40 dB/decade. Write \(H(s)\).

Solution

Each slope change names a corner. Flat→\(-20\) at 2 kHz is a pole; \(-20\)→flat at 20 kHz is a zero; flat→\(-40\) at 200 kHz is two poles, since the slope changed by 40.

\[ H(s) = 10\;\frac{1 + \dfrac{s}{2\pi(2\times10^{4})}}{\left(1+\dfrac{s}{2\pi(2\times10^{3})}\right)\left(1+\dfrac{s}{2\pi(2\times10^{5})}\right)^{2}} \]

Check the DC gain: 20 dB is a gain of 10 ✓. Check the drop across the first region: one decade at \(-20\) dB/dec is \(-20\) dB, taking 20 dB to 0 dB ✓.

The caveat from section 5 applies: the measurement cannot tell you whether those two poles at 200 kHz are really coincident, or a complex pair, or two real poles that happen to be close. If the magnitude peaked at all before falling, it was a complex pair.

7Level 4

A boost converter has a right-half-plane zero at 8 kHz. Standard practice is to cross the loop over at a third of that. Explain why, using only the phase rules from this lesson, and work out what the phase margin would be if you crossed over at the RHP zero instead. Assume the loop is otherwise a clean single pole.

Solution

An RHP zero subtracts phase like a pole: \(-\arctan(f/f_z)\). With the dominant pole already at \(-90°\):

At \(f_c = f_z/3 = 2.67\,\mathrm{kHz}\):

\[ \angle T = -90° - \arctan(1/3) = -90° - 18.4° = -108.4°, \quad \text{PM} = 71.6° \]

At \(f_c = f_z = 8\,\mathrm{kHz}\):

\[ \angle T = -90° - \arctan(1) = -135°, \quad \text{PM} = 45° \]

45° sounds survivable, but the magnitude is the other half of the problem: the RHP zero has been lifting the gain at \(+20\) dB/decade above 8 kHz, so the loop does not cross where the sketch without it says it does — it crosses later, at a frequency where the phase is worse still. The zero attacks from both directions at once, which is why the rule is a third and not a half.

The physical reason is worth carrying: to deliver more current, a boost converter must first increase inductor current, which means spending more time with the output disconnected. Asking for more output momentarily produces less. No amount of loop gain can fix a plant that initially moves the wrong way; you can only ask it slowly.

Lab — measure a response and read the circuit off the plot

Time 50 min Skill gain and phase measurement; asymptote fitting; identifying a network blind Parts resistors, capacitors, op-amp, signal generator, two-channel scope

Two halves. First measure a network you built, and confirm the sketching rules. Then have someone build one you have not seen, and identify it from the plot alone.

Part A. Build a two-stage RC low-pass, buffered between the stages so they do not load each other: \(R_1 = 16\,\mathrm{k}\), \(C_1 = 10\,\mathrm{nF}\) into a unity-gain buffer, then \(R_2 = 1.6\,\mathrm{k}\), \(C_2 = 10\,\mathrm{nF}\). The predicted corners are \(1/2\pi RC\) = 995 Hz and 9.95 kHz.

  1. Measure the output amplitude at 30 Hz, 100, 300, 1 k, 3 k, 10 k, 30 k, 100 k, 300 k Hz. Three points per decade is enough to see a slope.
  2. Measure the phase at the same points, using the scope's time difference between channels: \(\phi = -360° \cdot \Delta t \cdot f\).
  3. Plot magnitude in dB against \(\log f\). Draw the two asymptotes by eye and find where they intersect — that is your measured pole.
  4. Check three specific claims: the response is 3 dB below the asymptote at each corner, 1 dB below an octave away, and the slope between the two corners is 20 dB/decade while above both it is 40.
  5. Check the phase at the first corner. It will not be \(-45°\), because the second pole is only a decade up and has already contributed \(-\arctan(0.1) = -5.7°\). Predicted total: \(-50.7°\).
  6. Part B. Have someone build one of: a single RC, an RC with a resistor across the capacitor, a series RC to ground, or two cascaded stages — without telling you which. Measure it, sketch it, and name it before looking.

What you should find. Corners within a few percent of 995 Hz and 9.95 kHz — resistor and capacitor tolerance is the whole error budget, and electrolytics would be far worse, which is why the parts list says film or ceramic. The three claims in step 4 should hold to better than half a decibel.

Where it goes wrong, and why that is the interesting part. Remove the buffer between the stages and repeat. The two corners will not stay at 995 Hz and 9.95 kHz, even though no component changed: the second stage loads the first, and the poles of a loaded cascade are not the poles of its parts. They spread apart — one moves down, one moves up — and the product of the two stays put. That is the single most common error in hand analysis, and it is why the OCTC method of the Miller lesson computes a resistance with the rest of the circuit in place rather than treating each stage alone.

The second trap is the phase measurement itself. At 300 kHz a period is 3.3 µs and a typical scope cursor resolves perhaps 20 ns, so a 2° reading error is unavoidable — and at low frequencies the generator's own output impedance and any AC coupling add phase you did not intend. If your measured phase is a few degrees off everywhere in the same direction, suspect the instrument before the circuit.
