Two-Stage Amplifier Compensation

The two-stage operational amplifier is the workhorse of analog IC design. However, it has an inherent stability problem: two high-gain stages create two significant poles, leading to potential instability. Miller compensation is the elegant solution that has been used since the μA741 (1968) and remains the dominant technique today.

The Two-Stage Problem

A two-stage op-amp (differential input stage + common-source output stage) naturally has:

  • Pole 1: At the high-impedance output of the first stage (differential pair)
  • Pole 2: At the high-impedance output of the second stage (common-source)

Both poles may be at similar frequencies, causing rapid phase degradation and potential oscillation.

Miller Compensation Principle

Miller compensation exploits the Miller effect: a capacitor connected between the input and output of an inverting amplifier appears multiplied by (1 + Av) at the input.

Miller Effect

For a capacitor Cc between input and output of an inverting amplifier with gain -A:

Cin,eff = Cc(1 + A)
Cout,eff = Cc(1 + 1/A) ≈ Cc

This creates a very low-frequency dominant pole at the input with a small physical capacitor!

Two-Stage Op-Amp with Miller Compensation

The compensation capacitor Cc is connected from the output of the first stage to the output of the second stage. This achieves:

  1. Pole Splitting: The first pole moves to lower frequency, the second pole moves to higher frequency
  2. Dominant Pole Creation: The Miller-multiplied capacitance creates a very low-frequency pole
  3. Bandwidth Definition: The unity-gain frequency becomes predictable: fu = gm1/(2πCc)

Pole-Zero Analysis

A detailed small-signal analysis of the two-stage amplifier with Miller capacitor reveals:

Two-Stage Transfer Function (Simplified)

With Miller compensation:

A(s) = A0 · (1 - s/ωz) / [(1 + s/ωp1)(1 + s/ωp2)]

Pole and Zero Locations

Dominant pole:

ωp1 ≈ 1 / (R1 · gm2 · R2 · Cc)

Non-dominant pole:

ωp2 ≈ gm2 / CL

Right-half-plane zero (RHP zero):

ωz = gm2 / Cc

The RHP Zero Problem

The RHP zero is problematic because:

  • It adds phase lag (like a pole) instead of phase lead
  • It can significantly degrade phase margin
  • It occurs at the same frequency order as the non-dominant pole

Solution: Use a nulling resistor in series with Cc to move or eliminate the zero.

Nulling Resistor Technique

Adding a resistor Rz in series with Cc modifies the zero location:

Zero Location with Nulling Resistor

ωz = 1 / [Cc(1/gm2 - Rz)]

Three cases:

  • Rz = 0: RHP zero at gm2/Cc (original case)
  • Rz = 1/gm2: Zero at infinity (eliminated!)
  • Rz > 1/gm2: LHP zero (can improve phase margin!)

Design Procedure (Razavi Method)

Step-by-Step Miller Compensation Design

Step 1: Determine Required Unity-Gain Bandwidth

From specifications: fu (or GBW) required for settling time, slew rate, etc.

Step 2: Size Cc for Unity-Gain Frequency

Cc = gm1 / (2π · fu)

This sets the dominant pole location.

Step 3: Verify Non-Dominant Pole Location

For 60° phase margin, need ωp2 > 2.2 × ωu:

gm2 / CL > 2.2 × gm1 / Cc

Rearranging: gm2 > 2.2 × gm1 × (CL/Cc)

Step 4: Check RHP Zero Location

The RHP zero should be at least 3× beyond ωu:

gm2 / Cc > 3 × gm1 / Cc

This requires gm2 > 3 × gm1

Step 5: If Needed, Add Nulling Resistor

If RHP zero degrades phase margin, add Rz ≈ 1/gm2 to eliminate it, or Rz > 1/gm2 to create helpful LHP zero.

Worked Example: Two-Stage CMOS Op-Amp

Specifications

  • Unity-gain frequency: 10 MHz
  • Load capacitance: 5 pF
  • Phase margin: ≥ 60°
  • Available: gm1 = 200 μS, gm2 = 2 mS

Step 1: Calculate Cc

Cc = gm1 / (2π × fu) = 200×10⁻⁶ / (2π × 10×10⁶) = 3.18 pF

Choose Cc = 3 pF (standard value)

Step 2: Verify Non-Dominant Pole

fp2 = gm2 / (2π × CL) = 2×10⁻³ / (2π × 5×10⁻¹²) = 63.7 MHz

Check: fp2 / fu = 63.7 / 10 = 6.4 > 2.2 ✓

Step 3: Check RHP Zero

fz = gm2 / (2π × Cc) = 2×10⁻³ / (2π × 3×10⁻¹²) = 106 MHz

Check: fz / fu = 106 / 10 = 10.6 > 3 ✓

Step 4: Calculate Phase Margin

At fu = 10 MHz:

  • Phase from p1 (very low): ≈ -90°
  • Phase from p2: -arctan(10/63.7) = -8.9°
  • Phase from RHP zero: -arctan(10/106) = -5.4° (adds lag!)

Total phase: -90° - 8.9° - 5.4° = -104.3°

Phase margin: 180° - 104.3° = 75.7° ✓ Exceeds 60° requirement

Optional: Add Nulling Resistor

If we wanted to improve further:

Rz = 1/gm2 = 1/(2×10⁻³) = 500 Ω

This eliminates the RHP zero, adding ~5° to phase margin.

Final Design

Cc = 3 pF, Rz = 500 Ω (optional)

Unity-gain frequency: 10.6 MHz

Phase margin: 76° (or 81° with Rz)

Alternative Compensation Techniques

Cascode Compensation

In a cascode stage, the compensation capacitor can be connected to an internal low-impedance node, eliminating the RHP zero:

Razavi Insight

Connecting Cc to the source of a cascode device (a low-impedance node) prevents feedforward through Cc that causes the RHP zero. The zero moves to very high frequency or becomes an LHP zero.

Ahuja Compensation

Uses a current buffer (common-gate stage) in series with Cc to:

  • Eliminate the RHP zero
  • Push the non-dominant pole to higher frequency
  • Achieve higher bandwidth for given Cc

Practical Considerations

Art of Electronics: Real-World Issues

  1. Parasitic capacitances: Add to CL and can create additional poles. Always include estimated parasitics in design.
  2. Process variation: gm varies with process corners. Design for worst-case (slow corner has lower gm, higher poles).
  3. Temperature effects: gm decreases at high temperature, affecting pole locations. Verify stability across temperature range.
  4. Layout parasitics: The compensation node is high-impedance and sensitive. Keep it short and shielded.
