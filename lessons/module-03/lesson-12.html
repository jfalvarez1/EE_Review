Interview Prep — Feedback and Stability

This is the module that decides how senior the rest of the interview gets. Anyone can compute a gain. Whether you can look at a loop and say where it will ring, why, and what you would change is what separates a designer from someone who assembles reference designs.

Two facts do most of the work. Everything in this module is an application of one or both.
\[ A_{cl} = \frac{A}{1+A\beta} \qquad\qquad \text{loop gain} = A\beta \]
The closed-loop behaviour is set by \(A\beta\), and \(A\beta\) alone. When \(A\beta \gg 1\), \(A_{cl} \to 1/\beta\) — the amplifier disappears and only the feedback network remains, which is the entire reason we use feedback. When \(A\beta = -1\), the denominator is zero and the circuit oscillates.

Part 1 — Derive it

1 Derive Warm-up

Derive \(A_{cl} = A/(1+A\beta)\) from a block diagram, then tell me what feedback buys and what it costs.

How to answer
\[ V_{out} = A(V_{in} - \beta V_{out}) \] The amplifier sees the input minus the fed-back fraction of its own output.
\[ V_{out}(1 + A\beta) = AV_{in} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{V_{out}}{V_{in}} = \frac{A}{1+A\beta} \]
\[ A\beta \gg 1 \;\Longrightarrow\; A_{cl} \approx \frac{1}{\beta} \] Independent of \(A\). That is the whole trick: trade gain you have too much of for accuracy you do not.

What it buys: gain accuracy, reduced distortion, controlled input and output impedance, wider bandwidth — every one of them improved by the factor \((1+A\beta)\), which is why it is called the desensitivity factor.

"And it costs exactly one thing: the possibility of oscillation. Every one of those benefits scales with loop gain, so you always want more loop gain, and more loop gain always pushes you toward instability. That tension is the entire subject."

What they are testing: whether you can state the cost in one sentence. Candidates list the benefits fluently and then go quiet on the price. "It can oscillate, and the benefits and the risk are both proportional to loop gain" is the answer.
2 Derive Standard

Define phase margin. Why 45°, and what does a design with 90° look like on a step response?

How to answer
\[ \text{PM} = 180° + \angle A\beta \big|_{|A\beta| = 1} \] How much extra phase lag the loop can absorb at the crossover frequency before \(A\beta = -1\).
\[ \text{PM} = 90° \Rightarrow \text{no overshoot, no peaking, slowest settling} \]
\[ \text{PM} = 60° \Rightarrow \approx 4\% \text{ overshoot, fastest settling to } 1\% \]
\[ \text{PM} = 45° \Rightarrow \approx 23\% \text{ overshoot, visible ringing} \]
\[ \text{PM} = 30° \Rightarrow \approx 44\%, \text{ several cycles of ring} \]

"45 degrees is a floor, not a target. I design for 60, because it is close to the fastest settling you can get and it leaves room for the things that eat phase margin after you ship — load capacitance, temperature, part-to-part spread in GBW, and the PCB. A loop that measures 45 degrees on the bench at room temperature is not a loop with 45 degrees of margin in the field."

What they are testing: whether you can convert phase margin into a picture. Being able to say "about 4% overshoot at 60 degrees" is a strong marker, because it is the number you use when someone shows you a scope trace and asks what the margin is.
3 Derive Standard

An amplifier has poles at 100 Hz, 1 MHz and 10 MHz, and a DC gain of 100 dB. Sketch the Bode plot by hand and find the phase margin at unity noise gain.

How to answer
\[ 100\,\mathrm{dB} \text{ flat to } 100\,\mathrm{Hz}, \text{ then } -20\,\mathrm{dB/dec} \]
\[ \text{At } 1\,\mathrm{MHz}: \ 100 - 20\log_{10}\!\frac{10^6}{100} = 100 - 80 = 20\,\mathrm{dB} \]
\[ \text{Past 1 MHz the slope is } -40\,\mathrm{dB/dec}; \ 20\,\mathrm{dB} \text{ takes half a decade} \]
\[ f_c = 10^6 \times 10^{0.5} \approx 3.2\,\mathrm{MHz} \]
\[ \angle = -90° \;(\text{p1}) - \arctan\!\frac{3.2}{1} \;(\text{p2}) - \arctan\!\frac{3.2}{10}\;(\text{p3}) \]
\[ = -90 - 72.6 - 17.7 = -180.3° \;\Longrightarrow\; \text{PM} \approx 0° \] Unstable, or on the edge of it.

"So it will oscillate as a unity-gain follower. The fix is dominant-pole compensation: move the 100 Hz pole down until crossover happens well before the second pole. To get 60 degrees I want crossover near 1 MHz / 1.7, call it 600 kHz, which means dropping the first pole by a factor of about five, to roughly 20 Hz. I have traded bandwidth for stability, which is the only trade available."

What they are testing: whether you can do a Bode plot without a simulator. The arithmetic — 20 dB per decade, half a decade for 20 dB on a 40 dB/dec slope — has to be quick. This is the most common whiteboard exercise in the whole subject.
4 Derive Harder

What is a right-half-plane zero, where does one come from, and why is it so much worse than a left-half-plane zero?

How to answer
\[ \text{LHP zero } (1 + s/\omega_z): \quad +20\,\mathrm{dB/dec}, \quad \textbf{+90°} \]
\[ \text{RHP zero } (1 - s/\omega_z): \quad +20\,\mathrm{dB/dec}, \quad \textbf{−90°} \] The gain goes UP, which pushes crossover out to higher frequency, while the phase goes DOWN like a pole. Both effects hurt, and they hurt together.

Where it comes from: any topology where energy must first be stored before it can be delivered. In a boost or buck-boost converter, increasing the duty cycle to raise the output first takes the inductor away from the load for longer, so the output momentarily falls before it rises. The system's initial response has the wrong sign, and no amount of loop gain can fix that — it is physics, not compensation.

\[ \omega_{RHP} = \frac{R_{load}(1-D)^2}{L} \]

"So you cannot compensate it away; you have to cross over below it, typically at a fifth of the RHP zero frequency. And the zero moves down as load current rises and as input voltage falls, so you have to design for the worst corner — low line and full load — which is exactly where you also want the most bandwidth. That is why boost converters have such disappointing transient response compared to bucks."

What they are testing: for any power-electronics role this is close to mandatory. The phrase they are listening for is "you cannot compensate it, you have to cross over below it", and knowing that the zero moves with operating point is the senior-level detail.
5 Derive Harder

Explain Miller compensation in a two-stage op-amp. Why does the capacitor between the stages work so much better than one to ground, and what is pole splitting?

How to answer
\[ C_{eff} = C_c(1 + |A_2|) \] Miller multiplication. A 5 pF capacitor with a second-stage gain of 200 behaves like 1 nF — which is why this is done on-die, where 1 nF is impossible and 5 pF is nothing.
\[ p_1 \approx \frac{1}{2\pi R_1 C_c A_2} \quad \text{— pushed DOWN} \]
\[ p_2 \approx \frac{g_{m2}}{2\pi C_L} \quad \text{— pushed UP} \] POLE SPLITTING. The capacitor shunt-feedbacks the second stage, lowering its output impedance, which moves the output pole to higher frequency. Both poles move apart, which is exactly what you want and is why this beats brute-force loading.

"There is a catch. \(C_c\) also creates a feedforward path straight from input to output, giving a right-half-plane zero at \(g_{m2}/C_c\). On a modern low-power design that zero can be near crossover and it costs you phase margin. The standard fix is a nulling resistor in series with \(C_c\): set \(R_z = 1/g_{m2}\) and the zero moves to infinity; make it a little larger and you can pull the zero into the left half plane and use it to cancel the output pole."

What they are testing: for IC design roles this is the standard question. Pole splitting and the nulling resistor are the two pieces of vocabulary they are listening for; getting to the RHP zero without being prompted is what marks a strong answer.

Part 2 — Diagnose it

6 Trap Standard

A circuit is stable at a gain of 10 and oscillates at a gain of 1. Explain.

How to answer

Because \(\beta\) went UP, so the loop gain went up.

\[ \text{Gain of 10: } \beta = 0.1, \quad \text{loop gain} = 0.1A \]
\[ \text{Gain of 1: } \beta = 1, \quad \text{loop gain} = A \] Ten times more loop gain, so crossover moves a decade higher — into whatever pole was safely above it before.

"Unity gain is the worst case for stability, not the easiest. It is why datasheets say 'unity-gain stable' as a feature and why decompensated parts exist — an amplifier specified as stable only at gains of 5 or more has less compensation capacitance, so it is faster, and the price is that you may not use it as a follower."

What they are testing: the single most common misconception in the subject. Almost everyone initially believes lower gain is safer. If you answer this cleanly the interview usually moves up a level.
7 Diagnose Harder

A transimpedance amplifier with a 1 MΩ feedback resistor and a photodiode rings badly. Why, and what do you add?

How to answer

The photodiode capacitance and \(R_f\) form a pole in the feedback path, which is a zero in \(1/\beta\) — so the noise gain rises at 20 dB/decade and crosses the falling open-loop gain at a closing rate of 40 dB/decade. That rate of closure is the signature of about zero phase margin.

\[ f_z = \frac{1}{2\pi R_f (C_d + C_{in})} \] With \(R_f = 1\,\mathrm{M}\Omega\) and \(C_d = 50\,\mathrm{pF}\), that is 3.2 kHz — far below crossover, so the noise gain has been rising for decades by the time it gets there.
\[ C_f = \sqrt{\frac{C_d + C_{in}}{2\pi R_f\,\mathrm{GBW}}} \] The standard result for 45° of phase margin: place the feedback pole at the geometric mean of the zero and the gain-bandwidth product.
\[ C_d = 50\,\mathrm{pF},\ \mathrm{GBW} = 10\,\mathrm{MHz}: \quad C_f = \sqrt{\frac{50\mathrm{p}}{2\pi\cdot10^6\cdot10^7}} = 0.9\,\mathrm{pF} \]

"Under a picofarad — which means the board parasitics are the same size as the component, so this is as much a layout problem as a schematic one. Bandwidth then comes out around \(1/2\pi R_fC_f = 177\,\mathrm{kHz}\). And I would reverse-bias the photodiode to cut \(C_d\), which buys bandwidth directly, at the cost of dark current and therefore noise."

What they are testing: whether you think in terms of noise gain and rate of closure rather than signal gain. TIA stability is the standard hard question for optical, LIDAR and instrumentation roles, and the \(\sqrt{}\) formula is worth memorising.
8 Trap Nasty

A buck converter is stable at full load and unstable at 10% load. Its compensation has not changed. What moved?

How to answer

Several things at once, and a good answer separates them.

  • The output pole moved. In voltage mode the LC double pole is load-independent, but its Q is not: damping comes largely from the load resistance and the inductor's series resistance. At light load the Q rises sharply and the double pole peaks, adding phase lag fast.
  • CCM to DCM. At light load the converter likely enters discontinuous conduction, which changes the plant entirely — the double pole becomes a single pole and the DC gain changes. A compensator designed for CCM is compensating a different system.
  • The ESR zero did not move, but everything around it did, so its usefulness changed.

"So I would check whether it is entering DCM first, because that is the biggest change and the easiest to confirm — look for the inductor current touching zero. If it is a Q problem, forced-continuous mode fixes it at the cost of light-load efficiency. And the general lesson is that you compensate at the worst corner, then verify at every other one; light load and no load are corners people forget because efficiency work pulls attention to them for entirely different reasons."

What they are testing: whether you know the plant changes with operating point. Candidates who have only compensated on paper treat the plant as fixed. Naming DCM is the answer they are waiting for.
9 Trap Nasty

Is a system with 60° of phase margin and 12 dB of gain margin definitely stable?

How to answer

No. Those margins are computed at a single crossing, and a conditionally stable system has more than one.

If the phase dips below \(-180°\) at some frequency below crossover while the gain is still above unity, the system is stable as designed but unstable if the loop gain is reduced — the opposite of the usual intuition. And loop gain does get reduced, in every real system: during start-up, during current limit, during saturation recovery, or simply as a part ages.

"Bode's criterion assumes a single crossing. The honest check is a Nyquist plot, where the question is how many times the contour encircles \(-1\), and that catches conditional stability and right-half-plane poles that gain and phase margin simply cannot see. I would also simulate a start-up transient and a large-signal overload recovery, because that is where a conditionally stable loop actually reveals itself."

What they are testing: whether you know the limits of the tool you use every day. Almost everyone reads gain and phase margin off a Bode plot without thinking about the assumption underneath. Naming Nyquist and giving the start-up scenario is a senior answer.

Part 3 — Rapid fire

10 Rapid

How do you measure loop gain on a working board without breaking the loop?

Answer

Middlebrook's method: inject a small signal across a low-value series resistor placed in the feedback path at a point where the impedance looking one way is much higher than the other, and measure the ratio of the voltages either side with a network analyser. The DC loop stays closed the whole time, so the bias point is undisturbed — which is the entire point, because you cannot open the loop of a circuit that needs the loop to sit at its operating point.

11 Rapid

What does an ESR zero do for a buck converter's loop?

Answer

The output capacitor's ESR forms a zero at \(1/2\pi R_{ESR}C\), which adds \(+90°\) and helps recover the phase the LC double pole took. Old aluminium electrolytics had enough ESR to provide it for free. Ceramics do not — which is why replacing an electrolytic with a "better" low-ESR ceramic can make a previously stable supply oscillate, and why some controllers require a minimum ESR.

12 Rapid

Series-shunt, shunt-series, series-series, shunt-shunt — what does the naming actually tell you?

Answer

Input connection first, output second. Series at a port raises that port's impedance by \((1+A\beta)\); shunt lowers it by the same factor. So series-shunt is a voltage amplifier — high in, low out — which is the non-inverting op-amp configuration, and shunt-shunt is a transimpedance amplifier — low in, low out. You can read off both impedances from the name without analysing anything.

If you remember five things

Everything is \(A\beta\)
Benefits and instability both scale with it. That tension is the subject.
Unity gain is the worst case
\(\beta = 1\) is maximum loop gain. Followers are the hardest thing to stabilise.
60°, not 45°
About 4% overshoot and near-fastest settling, with room for what happens after you ship.
Rate of closure
40 dB/decade where noise gain meets open-loop gain means roughly zero phase margin.
An RHP zero cannot be compensated
Cross over below it. It moves down at low line and full load.
