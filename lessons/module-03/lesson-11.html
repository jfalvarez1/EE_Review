Case Study 1: Precision Instrumentation Amplifier

Specifications

  • Gain: 100 V/V (40 dB)
  • Bandwidth: 10 kHz minimum
  • Phase margin: ≥ 60°
  • Source impedance: Up to 10 kΩ
  • Load capacitance: Up to 100 pF (probe + cable)

Analysis

For a non-inverting configuration with gain G = 100:

  • Feedback factor: β = 1/100 = 0.01
  • Noise gain: 1/β = 100
  • Required GBW: Bandwidth × Noise gain = 10 kHz × 100 = 1 MHz minimum

We'll use an op-amp with GBW = 10 MHz to provide margin. The closed-loop bandwidth will be approximately GBW × β = 10 MHz × 0.01 = 100 kHz.

Stability Considerations

The 100 pF capacitive load with the op-amp's output impedance (~50Ω at high frequency) creates a pole at:

fp,CL = 1/(2π × 50Ω × 100pF) = 31.8 MHz

This is well above our 100 kHz bandwidth, but let's verify with simulation.

Interactive: Instrumentation Amp Analyzer

Design Solution

Component selection:

  • Op-amp: OPAx177 or similar (10 MHz GBW, low noise)
  • Rf = 99 kΩ, Rin = 1 kΩ (for G = 100)
  • Riso = 100Ω (isolation resistor for capacitive load)
  • Cf = 1 pF (optional feedback capacitor for additional stability margin)

Case Study 2: Active Filter with High Q

Specifications

  • Type: Second-order bandpass filter
  • Center frequency: 1 kHz
  • Q factor: 10 (narrow bandwidth)
  • Gain at center frequency: 20 dB

The High-Q Challenge

High-Q active filters are notorious for stability problems because:

  • The filter poles are close to the jω axis (high Q means low damping)
  • The internal loop gain can be very high at resonance
  • Op-amp non-idealities become significant

Art of Electronics Warning

For Q > 10, use state-variable or biquad topologies instead of Sallen-Key. The multiple-feedback (MFB) topology also handles high Q better than single op-amp designs.

Design Approach: State Variable Filter

The state variable topology uses three op-amps and provides independent control of Q, frequency, and gain:

Stability Analysis

For Q = 10 at 1 kHz, the filter poles are at:

s = -ω0/(2Q) ± jω0√(1 - 1/(4Q²))

s ≈ -314 ± j6267 rad/s

The poles are very close to the jω axis (σ = -314, |jω| = 6267)

Each op-amp in the filter needs adequate phase margin at the frequencies where internal loop gain is high.

Op-Amp Requirements

Parameter Requirement Reasoning
GBW > 100 × f0 × Q = 1 MHz Must maintain gain at resonance
Slew Rate > 2π × f0 × Vpeak Avoid slew limiting at full output
Output Current Sufficient for Q-dependent peaking Internal currents can be Q× higher

Case Study 3: Transimpedance Amplifier (Photodiode)

Specifications

  • Photodiode capacitance: 50 pF
  • Transimpedance: 100 kΩ
  • Bandwidth: 100 kHz
  • Phase margin: ≥ 45°

The TIA Stability Problem

Transimpedance amplifiers are challenging because the photodiode capacitance Cd creates a zero in the noise gain that peaks at high frequency:

Noise Gain for TIA

Noise Gain = 1 + Cd/Cf + jωRfCd

The noise gain has a zero at fz = 1/(2πRfCd) and increases at 20 dB/decade above this.

If the noise gain crosses the open-loop gain with insufficient phase margin, the TIA oscillates.

Interactive: TIA Stability Analyzer

Design Solution

The feedback capacitor Cf is critical for stability. The optimal value balances bandwidth against stability:

TIA Compensation Design

For 45° phase margin:

Cf = √(Cd / (2π × Rf × GBW))

For 50 pF diode, 100 kΩ, 10 MHz op-amp:

Cf = √(50pF / (2π × 100kΩ × 10MHz)) = 0.28 pF

Use 0.3 pF or rely on parasitic capacitance.

Resulting Bandwidth

f-3dB = √(GBW / (2π × Rf × Cd))

= √(10MHz / (2π × 100kΩ × 50pF)) = 178 kHz

Case Study 4: Voltage Reference Buffer

Specifications

  • Input: 2.5V precision reference (high impedance output)
  • Output: Drive 10 mA into varying loads
  • Load capacitance: 0.1 μF to 10 μF (decoupling caps)
  • Settling time: < 10 μs to 0.1%

The Challenge

Large capacitive loads create low-frequency poles that can severely degrade phase margin. A 10 μF capacitor with 50Ω output impedance creates a pole at only 318 Hz!

Solution: Unity-Gain Stable Op-Amp with Output Stage

Options for driving large capacitive loads:

Approach Pros Cons
High output current op-amp Simple, low output Z Expensive, may still need compensation
Isolation resistor Simple, cheap Load regulation suffers
In-loop buffer transistor High current, good regulation Adds pole, needs careful design
Composite amplifier Best performance Complex, more components

In-Loop Buffer Design

Using an NPN emitter follower inside the feedback loop:

Stability Analysis

The emitter follower adds a pole at approximately:

fp,buffer = gm / (2π × Cload)

With IC = 10 mA, gm = 0.4 S:

fp = 0.4 / (2π × 10μF) = 6.4 kHz

This is manageable if the op-amp crossover is above this frequency with adequate margin.

Design Checklist

Feedback System Design Verification

  1. Define loop gain: T(s) = A(s) × β(s)
  2. Identify all poles and zeros in the loop gain
  3. Calculate crossover frequency where |T| = 1
  4. Check phase margin: PM = 180° + ∠T(jωc) ≥ 45°
  5. Check gain margin: GM = 1/|T| at -180° phase ≥ 6 dB
  6. Verify capacitive load handling: Add isolation or compensation
  7. Check over temperature and process variation
  8. Simulate step response: Look for ringing, overshoot
  9. Prototype and measure: Real circuits have parasitics!

Common Mistakes Summary

Feedback Design Pitfalls

  1. Ignoring capacitive loads: Cables, PCB traces, and input capacitances all add load
  2. Forgetting about power supply rejection: High-frequency noise can couple through poor PSRR
  3. Insufficient decoupling: Creates local oscillations
  4. Not considering startup: Systems may be unstable during power-up transients
  5. Over-relying on simulation: Models don't include all parasitics
  6. Ignoring component tolerances: Phase margin must be robust to variations
  7. Using wrong op-amp topology: Decompensated op-amps need external compensation
