Introduction

Op-amps are designed to be stable with resistive feedback, but real-world loads are rarely purely resistive. Capacitive loads are the most common stability challenge, adding an extra pole that can degrade phase margin and cause oscillation. Understanding how to maintain stability with reactive loads is essential for practical analog design.

The Capacitive Load Problem

When you connect a capacitor to an op-amp output:

  • The capacitor and output impedance form an additional pole
  • This pole is inside the feedback loop
  • It adds phase shift at a frequency that may be near the unity-gain crossover
  • Result: Reduced phase margin, ringing, or oscillation

Op-Amp Output Impedance

An op-amp's output is not a perfect voltage source. It has an open-loop output impedance Zo that varies with frequency:

Closed-Loop Output Impedance

Zout,CL = Zo / (1 + Aβ)

At low frequencies where loop gain Aβ is high, Zout,CL is very low (milliohms).

At high frequencies where Aβ → 0, Zout,CL → Zo (tens of ohms to hundreds of ohms).

The Extra Pole

When capacitive load CL is added, it forms a pole with Zo:

Capacitive Load Pole

fp,CL = 1 / (2π × Zo × CL)

Example: Zo = 50Ω, CL = 100pF → fp,CL = 31.8 MHz

This might seem safely high, but remember: Zo increases with frequency!

Solution 1: Isolation Resistor

The simplest and most common fix is to add a small resistor in series between the op-amp output and the capacitive load:

Isolation Resistor Design

Riso moves the capacitive load pole and adds a zero that improves phase margin:

Zero: fz = 1 / (2π × Riso × CL)
Pole (original, now higher): fp ≈ 1 / (2π × (Riso + Zo) × CL)

Design Rule of Thumb

Choose Riso such that fz ≈ 0.5 × fcrossover:

Riso = 1 / (2π × 0.5 × fc × CL) = 1 / (π × fc × CL)

Art of Electronics Warning

The isolation resistor causes a DC error when driving DC current into the load:

Verror = Iload × Riso

For precision applications, consider feedback from after Riso, or use other techniques.

Solution 2: Snubber Network (RC Damping)

A snubber adds a series R-C to ground near the op-amp output. This provides damping without the DC error of an isolation resistor:

Snubber Design

The snubber resistor Rs and capacitor Cs add a zero-pole pair:

  • Zero: fz = 1/(2πRsCs)
  • Pole: fp = 1/(2π × Zo × (CL + Cs)) (approximately, when Rs >> Zo)

Design Guidelines

  1. Cs ≈ CL (same order of magnitude)
  2. Rs chosen to place zero near crossover: Rs = 1/(2πfcCs)
  3. Verify with simulation - snubber design is iterative

Solution 3: Feedback Capacitor (Noise Gain Shaping)

Adding a small capacitor across the feedback resistor increases the noise gain at high frequencies, reducing the crossover frequency before the capacitive load pole takes effect:

Feedback Capacitor Effect

Cf in parallel with Rf creates a zero in the noise gain:

fz,noise = 1 / (2π × Rf × Cf)

Above this frequency, noise gain increases at +20 dB/decade, reducing loop gain and crossover.

This is essentially "soft" lead compensation in the feedback network.

Razavi Insight

The feedback capacitor technique trades bandwidth for stability. It's most effective when bandwidth reduction is acceptable and you want to avoid the DC errors of isolation resistors.

Cable Capacitance

Coaxial cables add significant capacitance (typically 30-100 pF/meter). This is one of the most common real-world sources of capacitive loading:

Cable Type Typical Capacitance 1m Cable 3m Cable
RG-58 (50Ω) ~100 pF/m 100 pF 300 pF
RG-174 (50Ω) ~100 pF/m 100 pF 300 pF
RG-59 (75Ω) ~70 pF/m 70 pF 210 pF
Standard hookup wire ~30 pF/m 30 pF 90 pF

Don't Do This: Ignoring Cable Capacitance

A common mistake is designing an op-amp circuit on the bench with short leads, then deploying it with meters of cable. Always include expected cable capacitance in your stability analysis!

Inductive Loads

Inductive loads (motors, relays, solenoids) present different challenges:

Inductive Load Effects

  • Inductor and output resistance form a zero (not a pole)
  • This can actually improve phase margin in some cases
  • But: inductive kickback on current changes can cause voltage spikes
  • Always use flyback diodes for inductive DC loads

Worked Example: Driving a Scope Probe

Problem

An op-amp buffer (gain = 1) with GBW = 20 MHz and Zo = 75Ω drives a 10× scope probe (10 MΩ || 15 pF) through a 1-meter RG-58 cable (100 pF). Determine stability and fix if needed.

Step 1: Calculate Total Capacitive Load

CL = Ccable + Cprobe = 100 pF + 15 pF = 115 pF

Step 2: Find Capacitive Load Pole

fp,CL = 1 / (2π × 75Ω × 115pF) = 18.5 MHz

Step 3: Assess Stability

For unity-gain buffer, crossover ≈ GBW = 20 MHz

Capacitive load pole at 18.5 MHz is very close to crossover!

Phase shift from this pole at crossover: arctan(20/18.5) = 47°

Combined with internal op-amp phase: likely unstable or severely ringing

Step 4: Design Isolation Resistor

Target: Place zero at ~10 MHz (0.5 × crossover)

Riso = 1 / (2π × 10MHz × 115pF) = 138Ω

Use standard value: Riso = 150Ω

Step 5: Verify

New zero at: 1/(2π × 150 × 115pF) = 9.2 MHz ✓

Phase boost from zero at 20 MHz: arctan(20/9.2) = +65°

Net effect: significant improvement in phase margin

Solution

Add 150Ω resistor in series with op-amp output

Trade-off: 150Ω × Iout DC error (negligible for scope probe's 10 MΩ)
