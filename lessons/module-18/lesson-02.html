Select surge, fuse, filter and regulator ratings together from the vehicle transient requirement; no universal clamp voltage is implied. TI: Automotive reverse-polarity protection.

ISO 7637-2 test pulses, 12 V systems (severity level III / level IV):
  • Pulse 1: −75 V / −100 V, td = 2 ms, Ri = 10 Ω — an inductive load being disconnected while the module stays powered.
  • Pulse 2a: +37 V / +50 V, td = 50 µs, Ri = 2 Ω — current interrupted in a harness inductance.
  • Pulse 2b: +10 V, td = 0.2 s, Ri = 0.05 Ω — a DC motor acting as a generator after the ignition is switched off. Note the duration: two hundred milliseconds, not microseconds, and from a near-zero source impedance. It carries far more energy than either pulse 2a or pulse 3, and it is the one most often under-designed for.
  • Pulse 3a: −112 V / −150 V and Pulse 3b: +75 V / +100 V, tr = 5 ns, td = 100 ns, Ri = 50 Ω — switching bursts superimposed on the supply. Fast, low energy, and an EMC problem rather than a survival one.
  • Load dump is specified in ISO 16750-2 since the 2011 edition of 7637-2 (it was pulse 5 before that): 65 V to 87 V unsuppressed for a 12 V system, td = 40–400 ms, alternator Ri = 0.5–4 Ω. A 24 V system is 123 V to 174 V. Test B is the suppressed case, where a central load-dump suppressor holds the bus near 35 V and the module only has to survive that.

This is the test bench, not the vehicle

In a car, load dump is what happens when the battery is disconnected and the alternator drives the bus alone. ISO 16750-2 Test A reproduces it with a generator: UA = 13.5 V plus a pulse to US = 87 V, the top of the 65–87 V range, behind Ri, here 1 Ω from the standard’s 0.5–4 Ω. So even with no pulse the generator’s Ri sags the 10 Ω load to 13.5 × 10/11.07 = 12.20 V. The 200 ms rectangle holds the peak for the whole duration and so carries more energy than the standard’s decaying pulse of the same td; treat it as a worst case.

Choosing the clamp. The TVS must stay off through every sustained abnormal voltage, and for a 12 V module the highest is the jump-start test: 24 V for 60 s. That sets a 24 V stand-off. Vishay’s SM8S24A row gives VBR = 26.7 V minimum at 5 mA and VC = 38.9 V at 170 A, modelled as BV = 26.7 V and rz = (38.9 − 26.7)/170 = 70 mΩ. Its cathode goes to the rail: a unidirectional TVS turned round is a forward-biased diode across the supply.

On the plateau, 87 V behind 1.05 Ω pushes about 53.8 A into prot: 3.05 A to the load and 50.7 A in the TVS, which holds 30.53 V. That is 26.7 + 50.7 × 0.07 = 30.25 V plus the junction’s rise above its knee. The regulator behind the filter sees about 30.5 V, so it needs a rating above that with margin for the TVS spread (VBR up to 29.5 V). The TVS dissipates about 1.55 kW for 200 ms, roughly 310 J. Vishay rates the SM8S series at 5200 W for a 10/10 000 µs pulse, which carries about 75 J, and draws its load-dump curve for a 10 ms exponential waveform. This pulse carries about four times the energy of that rating pulse, so one module TVS is not a credible answer to an unsuppressed 87 V, 1 Ω dump. That is the arithmetic behind Test B: a central suppressor holds the bus near 35 V, or the module uses an active surge stopper rather than a clamp.

Bench acceptance: confirm TVS polarity and leakage first with a current-limited supply at 24 V; Vishay specifies 10 µA maximum at stand-off at 25 °C. Then run the pulse from a generator whose US, Ri and td are stated, and record V(prot), the TVS current and the regulator input together. Check the TVS case temperature and the filter capacitor’s voltage rating against the measured peak, not the nominal clamp.

What this build cannot tell you: the straight-line rz fit is exact only at its two datasheet points; a hot TVS clamps higher; and nothing in the solver limits pulse energy, so it will clamp a pulse the real part cannot survive. The FET is a fixed 50 mΩ, not a device.

References: Vishay SM8S10A thru SM8S43A datasheet (document 88387): maximum ratings, the SM8S24A row and Fig. 2; Analog Devices, “Low Quiescent Current Surge Stopper”, which summarises the ISO 16750-2 jump-start and load-dump tests and the surge-stopper alternative. Neither endorses this build.

Then change one thing at a time and predict the result before you run it. A simulator is only worth the time if you are checking an answer you already have — otherwise it is a very slow way of being surprised.

Sources

  • ISO 7637-2:2011 — Road vehicles — Electrical disturbances from conduction and coupling — Part 2: Electrical transient conduction along supply lines only. Pulse designations, amplitudes, durations and source impedances above are its 12 V test-pulse tables. This is the edition that moved load dump out to ISO 16750-2, so a pre-2011 reference will still call load dump “pulse 5” — which is why you see both in circulation.
  • ISO 16750-2 — Road vehicles — Environmental conditions and testing for electrical and electronic equipment — Part 2: Electrical loads. Load dump in both forms: Test A unsuppressed and Test B suppressed, with the 0.5–4 Ω alternator source impedance and the 40–400 ms duration used in the energy calculation.
  • Henry W. Ott, Electromagnetic Compatibility Engineering, Wiley, 2009. Why a transient does what it does rather than merely what its envelope looks like. The chapters on transient suppression and on grounding cover the filter and clamp choices here.
  • On the energy arithmetic. The TVS dissipation figure is not taken from a vendor note; it follows from the standard's own source impedance. With the bus behind Ri and the diode clamping at VC, the diode has VC across it and (VS − VC) is dropped in Ri. Any vendor selection guide will agree; the point of deriving it is that you can check the guide.
